## Loads and Analysis

### Core Concept
This is AISC Night School Session 4 of an introduction to steel BRIDGE design, presented by Dr. John Kulicki of Modjeski and Masters [00:01]. The subject is loads and structural analysis under the AASHTO LRFD Bridge Design Specifications, not the AISC 360 building code [06:09]. It teaches the LRFD limit-state philosophy built around four limit states: service, strength, extreme event, and fatigue/fracture [02:34]. The governing form is the LRFD equation, a sum of factored load effects (eta times gamma-i times Q-i) that must be less than or equal to the factored resistance (phi times R-n) [08:11][22:02]. Safety is expressed as a reliability index rather than a factor of safety, with a target near 3.5 selected to match historically acceptable performance [21:31][23:38].

### Technical Details
Live load is HL-93, a notional envelope (not a real vehicle) that pairs an axle group with a uniform lane load; for negative moment and interior reactions two trucks 50 feet apart are combined with the lane and factored by 0.90 [25:42][87:26]. Multiple presence factors are 1.2 for one loaded lane, 1.0 for two, and 0.75 declining for more, with the greater-than-one value explained by the limited 10,000-truck Canadian database extrapolated to a 75-year design life [26:50][57:21]. Distribution factors moved from the conservative S/5.5 rule to NCHRP equations incorporating girder-to-slab stiffness ratio (longitudinal vs transverse rigidity), which can differ from S/5.5 by up to roughly 50% and in some geometries is even unconservative [18:29][19:29][20:29]. Permanent loads carry both maximum and minimum factors so light-load cases that reduce stability or eccentricity are captured; transient loads are applied at their maximum or omitted entirely [59:41][61:46]. Refined analysis (grid, plate-with-eccentric-beam, finite element) is required for heavy skew, sharp curvature, or large stiffness variation [62:48].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The speaker frames pitfalls in analysis and load application rather than weld or bolt rejections. Named issues: misapplying S/5.5 distribution factors outside their calibrated variable range, where simple methods do not apply [62:48][63:19]; assuming S/5.5 is always conservative when certain geometries make it unconservative [21:01]; complexity in refined finite-element models obscuring behavior and hiding modeling errors ("blunders") [64:21][66:25]; and down-drag from settling soil that has pulled foundation elements out of footings or driven piles through weak strata when not accounted for [37:20]. ### Bid / Cost Implications
HL-93 produces roughly 34% more moment than HS-20 on a 100-foot span and over 40% more on a 140-foot span, so member sizing and steel weight scale up unless an improved distribution factor offsets it [17:57][24:40]. Where the distribution factor did not change, the heavier HL-93 hits the design directly with no offset; shear sees a proportionally larger change than moment [25:10][25:42]. On existing structures, refined analysis can reveal extra capacity and avoid load-posting, repair, or replacement, which is a direct cost avoidance [64:21]. Strength IV puts a floor on very-high-dead-load spans (over 600 feet) because the low dead-load factor would otherwise drop the reliability index [56:03].
