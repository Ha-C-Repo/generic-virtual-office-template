## Second-Order Elastic Analysis - Getting it Right

### Core Concept
This is session four of AISC's Night School on Stability Design of Steel Structures, presented by Dr. Donald W. White of Georgia Tech [00:02]. The subject is how to get a second-order elastic analysis right, meaning equilibrium taken on the deflected geometry of the structure [03:35]. White separates the two amplification effects that drive every steel building check: P-small-delta, the extra moment from axial load acting through member curvature relative to the chord between member ends [03:35][87:29], and P-large-delta, the sidesway effect from axial load acting through the story drift [21:34]. He builds from the basic B1 and B2 amplifiers, shows where member-by-member hand methods break down on real unsymmetric frames, and closes with how many finite elements a general-purpose analysis needs to stay accurate [55:40]. This is an engineering-analysis lecture, not a fabrication, connection-design, or erection lecture.

### Technical Details
For an end-loaded member the amplifier is C-sub-M over (1 minus P/PE1), and B1 is never taken less than one [40:42][41:12]. White warns that member-by-member B1 cannot catch second-order interaction between adjacent members: in an L-shaped frame the knee moment falls to zero as column axial load reaches its pinned-pinned Euler load, and the beam moment can climb past wL-squared/8 because the beam must brace the column [10:43][12:17]. He recommends switching to a rigorous general-purpose second-order analysis once B1 exceeds 1.2 in members that materially affect the structure [14:50]. For sidesway, B2 is 1 over (1 minus 1/gamma-story), where gamma-story is the load multiple to buckling, and R-sub-M (about 1 minus 0.15 times PMF/P-story, ranging 1.0 to 0.85) accounts for P-small-delta feeding the drift [27:16][29:51]. On element choice he contrasts stability-function, cubic Hermitian, and P-large-delta-only elements, and gives element-count rules: one stability-function element per member suffices, the cubic Hermitian element needs one to two per member (up to eight within an unbraced length for lateral-torsional-buckling work), and a bare P-delta element needs two to three or more [55:40][72:07][73:11].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The named failures here are analysis errors, not shop or field inspection rejections. Using member-level B1 on a frame with large gravity-load sidesway underestimates moments badly: White's market-shed example shows the simplified "multiply total moment by B2" method returning 60 ft-kips against an exact 104, and a separate gravity-plus-lateral shortcut being grossly in error for an unsymmetric frame [42:46][43:48][91:09]. A single P-delta element on a contrived cantilever underestimates sidesway displacement by 34 percent, moment by 24 percent, and the unity check by 9 percent [63:23][64:58]. The subtle software trap is torsion: a 12-degree-of-freedom element carrying only GJ/L (omitting warping/Cw) roughly doubles the computed twist and makes a second-order run "go berserk" with false instability before the design load is reached [76:43][80:20]. White also flags that many programs compute only first-order moments between nodes, silently dropping P-small-delta there [16:23].
