## Steel  Framed Stairway Design Pt 1

### Core Concept
This is part one of a two-part AISC webinar by Adam Friedman of CSD, previewing the then-forthcoming AISC Design Guide 34, Steel-Framed Stairway Design [01:05][32:18]. The thesis is that stairs and handrails are formally outside the Code of Standard Practice scope, defined there as "other steel" items, yet the speaker strongly recommends adopting the full AISC suite as the reference standard so the detailer, fabricator, and erector all share one set of expectations [04:52][06:01]. Part one covers stairway components and classes, code-driven layout (IBC vs OSHA), gravity loading, design methodology (sloping-beam vs horizontal-plane, simple-span vs frame analysis), and common member and connection types. Seismic, serviceability for drift, and delegated design are deferred to part two [02:54][87:50].

### Technical Details
Five stair classes (industrial, service, commercial, architectural) and geometric types (straight, parallel/switchback, angled, scissor, circular, curved, alternating-tread/ship ladder) are defined per the NAAMM stair and railing manuals, which the design guide updates structurally [06:43][07:45]. IBC risers run 4 in min to 7 in max with a 3/8 in tolerance between adjacent risers; the speaker recommends designing to 6-5/8 in so field misfit at the top or bottom riser stays code-legal [19:31][20:35]. IBC tread 11 in min, guard top 42 in, handrail 34 to 38 in, 1-1/2 in finger clearance, 4 in sphere infill rule [frame 0006][24:06]. OSHA post-2017 allows steeper geometry (9-1/2 in tread, 9-1/2 in riser), 42 in guard, 30 to 38 in handrail with 2-1/4 in finger clearance, 19 in sphere [25:21][27:00]. Gravity live loads from the IBC 2015 / ASCE 7-16 slide: stair tread 300 lb concentrated on 4 in^2 or 100 psf uniform (non-concurrent); landing 100 psf; guard top rail and handrail 200 lb point or 50 lb/ft any direction; infill 50 lb over 1 sq ft [frame 0011][37:57]. OSHA uses five-times-normal live load or a 1,000 lb concentrated load, 60 psf recommended floor minimum [39:46][41:30]. Design methodology: sloping-beam and horizontal-plane methods give near-identical reactions (1.83 vs 1.82 kip in the example) so either is acceptable; adjust stringer self-weight by the slope ratio when using horizontal-plane [44:05][45:25]. Simple-span (pin plus roller) is preferred and most conservative for deflection; a pinned-pinned frame analysis is statically indeterminate and produces large axial reactions from arching/catenary action that must be carried by connections and supports [45:45][47:01].
### Fabrication Notes
Build in tolerance: design risers below the 7 in absolute max so fabrication and erection variation does not push a riser out of code [19:53]. Lay out guard pickets center-to-center on a 4 in module so the clear opening lands near 3-3/4 in rather than a hard-to-hold 3.99 in [24:48]. The speaker favors a carrier angle under pan treads over direct welding to the stringer because it gives fit-up tolerance in the shop [51:58]. Stringer-member fit-up is a real shop constraint: a nominal "1-1/2 in pipe" is 1.90 in OD per A53 Gr B, and if an architect actually wants a 1.50 in OD member that is custom tubing and a separate, costlier buyout, so send an early RFI [60:51][61:43]. The MC12x14.3 was specifically rolled with a wide enough 2-1/8 in flange to seat a 1.90 in OD pipe post in a channel stringer; mismatched post and channel flange widths force a poor weld [66:01]. Checker plate is typically ASTM A786 with a wide yield-stress range, so call out the grade and verify availability with the fabricator before relying on Table 3-18b [53:54]. Grating and many treads are buyout items with manufacturer load tables; verify ADA and IBC opening size in grating treads on egress stairs [54:43].
### Erection Notes
The speaker's heavy-industrial background shows: switchback stair flights were preassembled on the ground and craned into a 350 ft modular tower as ready-to-use construction access, set in three-story-tall sections by one of the largest cranes in North America [09:11][09:30]. Leaving the slab-edge angle or pour stops loose for field installation buys plus or minus one inch of erection adjustment all around the opening [35:27][frame 0010]. Post-supported and hanger-supported landings with four support points are preferred because they erect as independent, freestanding units; integrated landings spanning between stringers complicate erection and lateral analysis [56:54][58:25]. Separate stair flights that keep top-of-steel low let each flight serve as construction access as soon as it is set [58:53]. Provide a partial tread or 5 to 6 in of clear space at stringer-to-floor connections so a welder has field access or a post-installed anchor can be installed without the stair in the way [32:31][33:35].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Field-modified industrial stairs where a mechanical or plumbing line was run straight through the stair, cutting the stringer, are called a life-safety failure inspectors should watch for [10:17]. Specifying the seven-inch absolute max riser then "borrowing" tolerance from it is a violation that cannot be cured elsewhere [19:53]. Architect-versus-fabricator pipe-size confusion (1-1/2 in nominal vs 1.5 in OD) surfaces as a shop-drawing-review dispute when it should have been an early RFI [61:43]. Frame 0018 shows the named failure: a pipe post that did not fit the channel stringer flange, producing an oversized, irregular field weld that does not meet AWS D1.1 [65:54]. Arbitrary spec deflection limits like a copied "1/4 in max" with no stated basis (live or total, finish or vibration driven) drive oversized members and bid variance; the speaker tells engineers to challenge them [49:36][50:03].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Adopting the AISC suite up front removes scope gray area among detailer, fabricator, and erector, which reduces RFI churn and field rework cost [05:57]. An unexplained tight deflection limit (the copied 1/4 in spec) materially changes member size and stiffness and "could have a big difference in terms of cost based on what fabricator or contractor bid the job," so it is a bid-risk flag to clarify before pricing [50:21]. The pipe-OD confusion is the clearest cost trap: a custom 1.50 in OD tube is more expensive than A53 1.90 in pipe and should be caught at bid or early design, not at shop-drawing review [61:17]. Reusing the building's standard connection type on the stair maximizes repetition for design, detailing, fabrication, and erection and avoids field errors, which is a direct labor-cost lever [62:00]. Frame analysis instead of simple span generates large axial connection forces, increasing connection and supporting-member cost, so the method choice has a price [47:31].
