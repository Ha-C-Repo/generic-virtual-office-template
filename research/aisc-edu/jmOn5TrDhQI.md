## Effects of Curvature and Skew

### Core Concept
This is session L3 of AISC's Introduction to Steel Bridge Design series, presented by Dominic Coletti (HDR) and Ronnie Medlock (High Steel Structures), covering how horizontal curvature and support skew change the behavior, analysis, and erection of steel plate girder bridges [00:00]. The central physics is torsion: curved girders carry torsion in nearly every case because the load center of gravity is offset from the chord line between supports, producing an eccentricity times vertical force [09:19][24:15], while straight skewed bridges develop torsion from differential deflection between girder ends that a cross frame connects at points of unequal vertical movement [18:04]. The second half, led by Medlock, is about cross frame fit: as a bridge deflects, the relationship of its parts changes, so the detailer must choose which load state the cross frames are built to fit [50:06][51:40].

### Technical Details
I-girders are open cross sections with low St. Venant torsional stiffness, so they resist torsion mainly through warping, which equates to lateral bending of the flanges; box girders are closed sections that resist torsion through circumferential shear flow with large moment arms [05:39][07:48]. The radial flange force is quantified by statics as Q = M / (R x H), where M is vertical moment, R is radius of curvature, and H is the distance between flanges [12:25][13:25]. In curved spans the top and bottom flanges act as beams spanning between cross frames and undergo flange lateral bending that must be designed for, while cross frames pick up horizontal and vertical forces transferring load between adjacent girders [13:25][14:26]. Skew effects scale with skew magnitude and framing layout: skew under 20 degrees with parallel cross frames is generally negligible, but larger skews create a transverse "nuisance stiffness" load path through the cross frames that can be softened by selectively omitting diagonals (lean-on bracing) [16:30][22:38]. Three analysis tiers exist, 1D approximate (V-load, M/R, line-girder-plus-factors), 2D refined (grid, and plate-and-eccentric-beam), and 3D finite element; a common 2D weakness is modeling only St. Venant stiffness and ignoring warping, which misstates I-girder torsion [30:00][34:35].

### Fabrication Notes
The fabricator builds the chosen fit into the geometry of the cross frames and their connections to the girders, laying out to the camber values shown on the plans, since the shop cannot independently know how the bridge will deflect [62:54][64:26]. Three named fit conditions exist: no-load (fully cambered) fit, steel dead load fit, and total (full) dead load / final fit; the cross frames are detailed to close up in whichever girder load state is specified [65:28][66:28]. For total dead load fit the frames are fabricated to match the fully loaded geometry, so the assembled steel will not naturally close under self weight alone and the girders sit laid over before the deck goes on [62:54][57:18]. Medlock urges that the fit be communicated by naming the load condition, not by saying when the girder should be "plumb," because plumb is ambiguous [66:59].

### Erection Notes
With steel dead load fit the bare steel goes together readily because the cross frames are detailed for the self-weight geometry, and skewed girders stand plumb at erection but then lay over as the deck is cast [74:46][75:16]. With total dead load fit on a skewed bridge the crew must force the girders together, typically with a come-along (the example notes it does not take much force), accepting an initial lay-over that resolves out as dead load is applied [57:18][75:16]. AASHTO requires the engineer of record to designate the fit condition, and some owners prescribe practices by bridge type [70:07]. Curved bridges are stiffer and twist less, so they are almost always built to steel dead load fit; total dead load fit is to be avoided on curved bridges because the geometry only closes when all loads are present, which is impossible during erection and locks in additive force effects on long or sharp-radius spans [59:50][76:16][77:19]. The recommended thresholds: skewed bridges over 200 ft or with stiffness factor I_s greater than 0.3 should use steel dead load fit; curved spans over 250 ft move to no-load fit [73:44][77:19].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The video does not catalog weld or bolt rejections; its failure mode is fit and analysis error. A field example is shown where an erector had fewer cross frames than needed and the released girders laid over dramatically along their length, which was corrected by craning a worker up to add frames, after which the welds were inspected and passed [58:19]. A recurring conceptual error named is attributing skew lay-over to loose connections or oversized holes when it actually comes from girder flexibility, so leaving bolts loose or enlarging holes between frame and girder is the wrong fix [59:19]. An analysis-side failure is using a 2D package that ignores warping stiffness and so mismodels I-girder torsion on bridges with significant curvature, skew, deep girders, or long or variable-depth spans [34:35][35:35]. A construction sequencing trap is pouring part of a heavily skewed span and letting the concrete set, which locks in an intermediate deflected condition [75:46].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Analysis method choice is a direct effort and budget trade: moving from 1D to 2D to 3D raises modeling effort and the volume of output to process, and the answer to which to use is "it depends" on bridge complexity, schedule, and budget [27:55][38:13]. Selectively omitting cross frame diagonals (lean-on bracing) on a skewed bridge reduced loads in all the cross frames and produced a more economical design than repeatedly strengthening frames that then attract still more load [22:38][23:41]. Boundary-condition modeling carries real cost sensitivity: changing end bearings from fixed to guided on a 2-span 225 ft curved bridge shifted composite live load deflections by as much as 31 percent and composite moments and dead load deflections by about 5 percent [48:04]. Choosing the wrong fit condition forces field rework (forcing girders, adding frames, re-inspecting welds) or locks in unintended dead load forces, both of which cost erection time [58:19][77:19].
