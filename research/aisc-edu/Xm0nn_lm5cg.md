## The AISC Direct Analysis Method from Soup to Nuts
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xm0nn_lm5cg | Duration: 1:36:02 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This is Session 5 of an AISC "night school" stability-design series, presented by Dr. Donald White of Georgia Tech on March 2, 2015 [00:01]. The subject is the Direct Analysis Method (DM) for steel stability design, which AISC names as the preferred of the three sanctioned approaches in Chapter C, the others being the Effective Length Method (ELM) and the First-Order Analysis Method [03:36]. The DM assesses overall system stability by carrying geometric imperfections and stiffness reduction directly inside a second-order load-deflection analysis [05:11], which then lets the engineer take K equals 1 and treat all members as braced in the resistance checks [05:42]. The payoff is that the DM gives a more representative picture of the real internal forces and applies uniformly to sway, braced, and combined frames [06:13].
### Spec References
The talk cites these explicitly: AISC Specification Chapter C and Section C1 (general stability requirements) [03:36][06:13]; equation C2-1 for notional load N = 0.002 (or 1/500) times Y_i [42:25][42:55]; equation C2-2b for the 0.8 stiffness reduction and the tau-b column inelastic factor [26:21]; Appendix 8 for the B1 and B2 amplifiers [62:33]; Appendix 1 for distributed-plasticity / inelastic analysis with the 0.9 stiffness reduction [77:03][82:15]; Chapter F for the girder flexural-resistance checks [71:51]; the AISC Code of Standard Practice for the L/1000 out-of-straightness and L/500 (delta-0/L = 0.002) out-of-plumb tolerances [18:40][40:20]; and AISC Design Guides 25 and 28 as companion reading, with DG 25 supplying the K=0 / cross-section-strength extension when alpha-P_r is under 10% of the buckling load [03:05][28:55][33:32]. ASCE 7 is referenced for seismic eccentricity and drift relaxation [51:42][56:20].
### Technical Details
The six stability requirements of Section C1 are component limit states, flexural/axial/shear deformations, second-order P-large-Delta and P-small-Delta effects, geometric imperfections, stiffness reduction, and uncertainty in stiffness and strength [06:44][07:45][08:46]. For ASD the working loads are scaled up by alpha = 1.6 before analysis and divided back afterward [10:20]. Leaning (gravity) columns and walls not in the lateral system must still be carried, often through a pinned "dummy column" strut-connected to each level [11:24]. A structure is "stability critical" when second-order drift over first-order drift exceeds 1.7 for the DM reduced stiffness, or 1.5 for the ELM nominal stiffness [25:20][88:05]. The worked example is a squat single-story, five-bay frame with W12x65 lateral columns and a W24x62 roof girder, where wide footprint plus large gravity load on small lateral stiffness produced a B2 amplifier near 4.8 and a beam-column unity check of about 0.63 [53:46][54:47][63:07][71:21]. White warns that under the DM you cannot read spare capacity off a low unity check because gravity-only stability-critical frames scale nonlinearly [71:21].
### Fabrication Notes
The video is an analysis-and-design webinar and does not address shop fabrication, fit-up, or weld procedures. The only fabrication-adjacent content is the imperfection tolerances the analysis assumes: out-of-straightness L/1000 and out-of-plumb L/500 per the Code of Standard Practice [18:40][40:20], which are the as-built limits a fabricator and erector must hold for the design assumption to remain valid.
### Erection Notes
Erection procedures, sequencing, and rigging are not covered. The relevant link is that the L/500 maximum out-of-plumb erection tolerance from the Code of Standard Practice is the physical basis for the notional load and the modeled lean [40:20][42:55]. White also notes that for an existing structure measured to be out of plumb beyond the code tolerance, the DM lets you model the actual measured out-of-plumbness rather than 0.002 [72:53].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
No shop or field inspection failures are named. The errors discussed are analysis mistakes: forgetting to include gravity/leaning-column loads in the second-order model [11:24]; reducing E inside the specification resistance equations, which is wrong because stiffness reduction is already implicit there [29:56]; assuming a low unity check means large reserve capacity on a stability-critical frame [71:21]; and the common confusion that summed notional loads leave a net foundation shear, when the missing P-Delta shear restored at the foundation brings the gravity-only total back to zero [52:15].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The DM is presented as more analysis work for a payoff in the resistance side, removing the effort and uncertainty of computing K factors for irregular frames with setbacks [16:36][37:06]. The headline economic point is that on the example frame the DM (with the DG 25 extension giving an anchor of phi-P_y = 860 kips versus phi-P_n on KL = 69.2 kips) developed about 46% more column axial strength than the ELM, but that gain costs roughly 133% more required girder moment [80:09][82:47]. That trade, more demand pushed into the girder to unlock column capacity, is exactly the kind of member-sizing consequence that changes tonnage and connection cost.
