## The Splice is Right
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aVtV6LWfoMg | Duration: 1:29:44 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This is a five-speaker Pecha Kucha session covering column, truss, and member splices across the full lifecycle: design (Carol Drucker), fabrication (Sylvie Boulanger), erection (Larry Owner of Douglas Steel), welded connections (Duane Miller of Lincoln Electric), and architecturally exposed structural steel splices (Tara Mayer Bulic). The governing theme is that a splice is for shipping, handling, and constructability, and is "right" when it is simplified, the complex welding stays in the shop, and the field splice is direct, accessible, and discreet [06:43][17:11][41:32]. A splice is distinct from a connection: it rejoins one continuous member that was cut for transport, so it should disappear from view [51:57][89:06].
### Spec References
Speakers cited the following: AISC Manual Part 14 for standard gravity column splices [07:15]; Manual splice "type 8" carrying both PJP and CJP welded options, with Manual PJP sizes matching the spec minimum PJP sizes by material thickness [08:16]; ASCE 7-05 (ASD) and ASCE 7-10 (LRFD) for wind, plus ASCE 37 for construction-period loads with its wind-velocity reduction factor [07:46]; the AISC Seismic Provisions requiring gravity columns to carry shear equal to the least plastic moment strength at the splice over column height, with intermediate/special concentric braced frame and BRB columns at twice that, and the 2016 seismic provisions newly allowing PJP welds in IMF/SMF splices "with restrictions" where CJP had been required [10:22][11:24]; the AISC specification compression-splice stability force of 50% of compression acting in tension, or shear/moment from 2% of compression for members other than columns [12:59]; AISC Design Guide 1 base plates [10:22], Design Guide 4 extended end plates [13:32], and Design Guide 10 erection bracing listing bending failure of the base plate, buckling of anchor rods, and anchor-rod pullout [39:28]; the 14th edition Manual fill-plate rule capping the bolt reduction at 15% [15:05]; OSHA 1926 Subpart R, specifically 1926.756 (300 lb eccentric load 18 in from the column face) and 1926.760 plus the perimeter-column 48 in extension and 42 to 45 in cable-hole provisions [30:38][31:10]; RCSC for the limited slip-critical range and the bearing X/N bolt thickness check [81:07]; AWS D1.1 preheat thresholds [77:18].
### Technical Details
Gravity column splices need minimal calculation and the contractor can pick any Manual Part 14 type [07:15]. With tension or lateral load the splice must be designed; first check whether tension exists at all, and with an envelope solution pair minimum axial with maximum moment so compression is not overstated [09:19]. Built-up and box-column welds are analyzed as a line weld (force per unit length over available stress to size the throat), with a plastic stress-block method available to cut bolts or weld size [09:50]. Truss splices distribute force by area (shear lag U = 1 when each element is connected) or assign all tension to the flanges with the web taking shear [14:35]. Sharing axial load between bolts and welds in one splice is a "no-no" due to strain-compatibility [16:08].
### Fabrication Notes
The repeated fabrication principle is to push complex welding into the shop where it is controlled and leave a simple direct splice for the field [17:11][19:17]. Order material only after the splice location is fixed; a shop CJP splice was used to extend a member to allow a bolted field splice [15:37]. In the shop a PJP is preferred over CJP to cut weld volume, but field splices trend CJP for fit-up [15:37]. Weld access holes on CJP truss splices must be remembered so the web is not lost, and they can be large enough to see daylight through [16:40]. Trial fit-up of complex splices in the shop or yard is a core "splice is right" tip: a two-story transfer truss was laid on its side filling the whole shop, splice plates used as drilling templates for shim and diagonal, and the bottom chord was never released until the top fit and was re-bolted before shipping [23:24][24:24]. Heavy three to three-and-a-half inch column splice plates take one welder a week each, involving web plates, preheat, heating/warming, and final bolting [27:27].
### Erection Notes
Larry Owner framed erection around OSHA Subpart R and constructability. Column splices are located five feet above finished floor so splice plates do not interfere with the perimeter safety cable, since OSHA's 48 in is a minimum not a maximum, and 60 in is a convenient working height [33:46][41:01]. Columns should not run more than two stories due to weak-axis bending under the eccentric erection load plus wind [34:48][41:01]. Erection aids or two channels on either side of the web stabilize a welded splice before the weld is made, then are removed [36:52][37:54]. Where an upper shaft is smaller, move the gauge line and keep a flat (not tapered) splice plate [36:20]. The column-to-concrete base is itself a splice: oversized washers dropped over the anchor rods keep shim stacks from being kicked over and preserve base elevation [40:29]. Field welding skill loss is managerial and experiential, not technological; preheat is the welder's friend in cold weather [62:18][77:18].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named failures: anchor-rod pullout, buckled anchor rods, broken-off anchor rods, kicked-over shim stacks, and base-plate bending failure during erection [39:28][40:29]. Putting the column splice at mid-height or too low forces a worker into a man-lift and can violate Subpart R when splice plates sit where the perimeter cable belongs [32:12]. CJP welds specified where a PJP or bolted detail would do are an over-design that draws fabricator pushback; engineers of record are urged to give forces rather than dictate CJP [16:08][84:29]. The X-bolt field risk is the wrong-length bolt from the bolt bag leaving the nut not flush, requiring careful inspection and a bold cloud on the erection diagram [81:07]. Bad welds: incomplete grinding that looks worse than as-welded, and over-polishing that removes weld strength and takes paint unevenly [49:22].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The headline number: a bolted splice of a W14x730 tension chord needed about 6,900 extra pounds of steel and 128 high-strength bolts, while a welded version used roughly 70 pounds of weld metal, and contractor pricing showed a 79% cost reduction for the welded connection [42:34][43:37]. Butt-plate column splices are the most cost-effective when architecture allows [11:57]. Bearing bolts in oversized holes can cut truss-splice bolt count by 50% versus slip-critical [13:32], and an X-bolt is a 25% strength gain essentially for free when the ply thickness already creates the condition [83:12]. Engineering-record CJP defaults and "pay the EOR for the forces" practice add avoidable cost [85:29]. AESS adds attention and cost: distance-to-view (a 20 ft threshold) decides where to fuss, since beyond 20 ft splices need far less remediation [50:56][51:26].
