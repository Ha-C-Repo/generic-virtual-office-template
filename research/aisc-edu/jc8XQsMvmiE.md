## Design of Facade Attachments, Session L1: Facade Attachments, Part 1

### Core Concept
This webinar by Alec Zimmer of Simpson Gumpertz and Heger teaches how the structural steel frame supports building facades and how the joints between the facade and the frame are sized to absorb structural deflection, thermal change, and moisture growth [00:34][01:35]. It walks three facade families: hand-laid masonry cavity walls, panelized systems including precast, and aluminum glass curtain walls, then closes with a method for sizing vertical movement joints [02:06]. The controlling idea is that the steel spandrel beam carries the facade eccentrically, so it both deflects and rotates as a rigid body about its top flange, and that rotation drives both a vertical drop and a horizontal translation at the shelf-angle tip that the joints must accommodate [13:47][65:44].

### Spec References
The single document cited repeatedly is AISC Design Guide 22, Facade Attachments to Steel-Frame Buildings, including its shelf-angle deflection tables and worked Example 7-1 [01:05][19:28][71:57][72:28]. Two research papers behind the DG 22 shelf-angle tables are named: Grimm and Yura, and Tide and Krogstad, the speaker preferring the Tide and Krogstad approach for fewer designer limitations [19:28]. For load combinations on joints he cites ASCE 7-16 commentary, specifically dead plus half live plus a reduced-return-period wind Wa [71:24]. No AISC 360 section, AISC 341 seismic provision, AWS D1.1 clause, or RCSC bolting reference is stated in this session; those are general principles, no section cited.

### Technical Details
Shelf-angle sizing targets roughly two-thirds of the masonry veneer bearing on the angle so the resultant stays over the angle [10:13]. Hot-rolled angles are preferred over bent plate for less variability in the horizontal-leg position, with shims behind the vertical leg used to tune the long horizontal leg now driven longer by thicker mineral-wool insulation [10:44][11:15]. Curtain wall out-of-plane deflection is typically span over 175, tightened to span over 360 with brittle finishes and capped near 3/4 inch, with in-plane drift commonly span over 360, 3/4 inch max, and as tight as 3/8 inch on recent jobs [55:33][56:34]. Aluminum mullions expand about twice the rate of steel framing, so horizontal mullion joints must absorb that movement [55:02]. Brick growth is irreversible moisture expansion of as much as 0.07 percent, and sealants typically compress 30 percent, up to 50 percent for high-performance products [69:53][14:52][70:23]. Panel gravity load must bear at exactly two points per panel to keep the load path statically determinate [34:57].

### Fabrication Notes
Shelf angles are galvanized in the shop as standard, with stainless steel used on some projects partly to mitigate thermal bridging, because concealed angles can corrode undetected if flashing joints leak [18:58][19:28]. Threaded studs that carry the shelf-angle-to-hanger connection are shop-welded into the hanger angle, and the designer is warned those studs see flexure as well as shear through the thickness of the thermal isolation pad [50:50][24:07]. Any bolt holes that must be drilled in the curtain-wall hardware should be done in the shop and coordinated with the primary structure [61:39]. Bent-plate pour stops, gussets welded to the spandrel web, and full-depth web stiffeners for local bending are the recurring fabricated steel pieces [57:34][58:05].

### Erection Notes
The shelf angle is first erection-bolted to the hanger angle, set to its final field location, then field welded, and the weld is touched up with zinc-rich coating where galvanizing burned off [22:04]. The hanger angle is then fillet welded to the gusset on the spandrel beam [22:36]. The slab edge is set by the steel erector and poured long before the veneer goes on, so in-and-out adjustment of the shelf angle is made with steel shims [30:52][64:00]. Curtain-wall erectors prefer field-welded connections for adjustability, using a self-drilling erection fastener to set position before the structural fasteners go in, after which the erection fastener is abandoned or removed [52:58][57:04]. Spray fireproofing applied before the curtain wall requires block-outs at anchor locations, patched after anchors are installed [52:27]. Spandrel-beam rotation from the eccentric veneer is restrained with double-angle kickers or small roll beams perpendicular to the spandrel [22:36][23:06].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Sealant joints over-compress and bulge or the brick cracks when joints are not sized for the full set of movements [05:08][84:24]. A shelf angle set out of tolerance can leave less than two-thirds bearing, forcing a field-welded extension [31:55]. The most cited field failure is missed embeds, brackets, block-outs, and recesses in a complicated panel erection sequence, described as a daily struggle on a live project [48:24]. Other named problems are joints in the architectural panel layout not coordinated with load-application points on the primary frame, slotted holes too short to absorb slab-edge or spandrel-sweep variability, and crushing of the thermal isolation pad if compression stress is not kept near one fifth of the pad ultimate strength [49:25][61:39][85:27].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Tight architectural joints drive cost into the steel: a 3/8 inch mortar-matched sealant joint forces very tight spandrel-beam deflection limits, which can mean heavier beams or shorter spans [83:20][56:34]. Galvanized or stainless shelf angles, thermal isolation pads, kickers, roll beams, gusset plates, and field welding all add per-floor connection labor and material beyond a plain spandrel [18:58][22:36]. Long hangers driven by thicker insulation add flexural rotation and may upsize members [25:11][10:44]. The division of design responsibility between the engineer of record and the panel or curtain-wall specialty engineer is a coordination cost that, if mishandled, surfaces as field rework and missed embeds [43:17][49:25].
