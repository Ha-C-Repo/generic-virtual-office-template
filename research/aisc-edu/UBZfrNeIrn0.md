## New Composite Design Provisions in the 2010 AISC Specification
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UBZfrNeIrn0 | Duration: 1:26:27 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
Will Jacobs of Stanley D. Lindsey, vice chair of AISC Task Committee 5 on composite construction, walks through how Chapter I of the 2010 AISC 360 specification was rewritten and expanded [01:36, 08:45]. The mandate was minimal change, so ordinary composite beam design and shear-stud strengths in composite deck did not change [06:43, 22:40], but the chapter grew from five to nine sections and from 12 to 24 pages to cover composite columns, load transfer, composite diaphragms, and a new steel-anchor section [05:42, 08:45]. The big terminology shift is that "shear stud" is replaced by the generic "steel anchor" for ACI 318 Appendix D consistency [09:45].

### Spec References
AISC 360 Chapter I, reorganized into I1 general, I2 axial, I3 flexure, I4 shear, I5 combined forces, I6 load transfer, I7 composite diaphragms and collector beams, I8 steel anchors, and I9 special cases [10:17, 46:08]; load transfer equations I6-1 (force to steel) and I6-2 (force to concrete) [38:00, 74:42]; steel-anchor-in-composite-component provisions in I8.3 [44:04]. External references cited: ACI 318 (rebar and concrete detailing, the 6-inch lateral-support tie rule, 1% minimum longitudinal reinforcement) and ACI 318 Appendix D (concrete breakout) [08:45, 18:03, 78:16]; AISC Design Guide 6 as a fourth combined-force method [31:50]; AISC Design Guide 3 (deflection limits, 10% ponding allowance) [58:20, 62:29]; AISC Design Guide 19 and ASCE 29 for filled-column fire resistance [83:50]; ASCE 37 (25 psf construction live load for hose-placed concrete) [59:21]; ASCE 7-10 load combinations [43:04]; IBC 2009 Table 721 for encased-column fire rating and the L/360 full-live-load deflection rule [83:50, 70:07]; SDI diaphragm design guide [42:01].

### Technical Details
Concrete-filled tubes (CFT) now get compact, non-compact, and slender local-buckling classifications, so a 30-inch round tube can run a wall down to about 3/16 inch [11:48, 12:19]. The confinement factor C2 is 0.85 for rectangular and 0.95 for circular compact sections, dropping to 0.7 for non-compact [20:35, 21:37]. Encased columns use a plastic strength model with minimum encased-shape ratio 1% and minimum longitudinal reinforcement 0.4% (about 1.4% total), with the economical encased ratio at 8 to 12% [15:25, 16:28]. The old "0.009 square inch per inch" tie rule is now plainly stated as a number 3 bar at 12 inches [16:59]. Steel anchors in load-transfer zones need H/D greater than 5 so failure is in the steel shank and the concrete limit state can be ignored, with phi 0.65 [45:06, 45:37]. Bond strength for filled sections is only 60 psi with phi 0.45, the lowest in the spec [41:01]. Composite beams should run 40 to 50% composite action minimum, since 40% already reaches about 80% of full strength [67:34, 68:04].

### Fabrication Notes
Steel headed stud anchors lose length to burn-off when welded: about 3/16 inch welded directly to base metal and about 3/8 inch when welded through deck, so anchor length must be specified to still extend 1.5 inches above the deck after installation [64:31, 65:02]. The 2010 spec wording was corrected so the half-inch concrete cover over studs is a specified value, not a field-reject trigger, and a stud ending at 3/8 inch cover is acceptable for strength as long as it does not protrude through the slab [63:30, 85:22]. For encased columns, two-piece U-ties through holes drilled in the steel section are an option contractors dislike, so bundling rebar in the corners or using weldable rebar or carry bars is preferred [18:34, 19:35, 50:42].

### Erection Notes
This webinar is a design-and-detailing talk and does not cover crane, rigging, sequencing, or field bracing for erection. The nearest field item is unshored composite beam construction, where ponding from beam and girder deflection during the pour adds roughly 10% of slab weight unless the slab is poured to constant thickness, and a 25 psf construction live load is applied for hose-placed concrete [57:50, 58:20, 59:21].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The named pitfalls are the "non-composite composite beam," where a few studs added only for drag-strut force fracture under gravity load, so a minimum 25 to 50% composite action is required [42:34, 51:42]; the unconservative corner near point D-prime-prime in the plastic interaction curve when pushing a column to its limit, avoided by the simplified straight B-C method [29:49, 31:20]; the 13th edition manual CFT tables that listed non-composite flexural capacity, fixed in the 14th edition [28:47]; and field rejection of studs at 3/8 inch cover that was never the spec intent [63:30]. Concrete breakout toward a free edge is not precluded by the H/D and spacing rules and must be checked separately [77:45].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Adding studs beyond about 40 to 50% composite action buys little strength and wastes labor and material, so the takeoff should target that range rather than full composite [68:04]. Choosing a 7.5-inch normal-weight slab on 3-inch deck achieves a 2-hour UL fire rating without sprayed fireproofing on the deck, trading concrete weight for avoided fireproofing scope [57:19]. The load-introduction-length limit (anchors within twice the column transverse dimension above and below the transfer point) can force an extra non-composite floor lift when a high-rise lands on a podium, which is a real material and detailing cost to carry [79:48, 80:48]. The 2010 design examples are free at aisc.org/designexamples, reducing engineering hours on composite checks [55:14].
