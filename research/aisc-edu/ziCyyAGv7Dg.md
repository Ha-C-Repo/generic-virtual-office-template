## Field Fixes - Part 12

### Core Concept
This is an engineer-to-fabricator constructability webinar. The thesis is that a model that satisfies the analysis program does not automatically produce a buildable, economical detail, and the engineer should detail with the shop and erector in mind. The speaker walks through member selection guidelines, spandrel and cantilever decisions, connection-type economy, and the practical problem of who designs connections and supplies stamped calculations. A recurring theme is that worshiping concentricity at a work point pushes eccentricities and moments into connection material, and it is often cheaper to accept a small moment in a heavy member than to build an oversized gusset [17:38].

### Spec References
The speaker cites by name: AWS D1.1 stud placement requiring the stud in the center of the flange [01:05] and AWS D1.1 requiring a CJP groove weld (not a partial-penetration weld) when splicing a member with a groove weld [05:11]. He repeatedly cites the "2005 spec" (AISC 360-05): the changed effective-flange / bolt-hole rule for bending, where the old roughly 8% bolt-hole threshold in 50 ksi material that forced an elastic-section-modulus reduction was relaxed so holes can reach roughly 25 to 30% of the flange before reduction, allowing use of the plastic section modulus [12:28]; the relaxed continuity-plate-to-flange weld requirement, where the plate-to-flange connection now develops only the additional strength the flange needs rather than the full plate strength, attributed to University of Minnesota research by Dexter and Hajjar [13:58]; and new stud-capacity formulas based on number of studs per corrugation that generally reduce values [08:19]. He references the AISC Code of Standard Practice for maximum camber recommendations [07:48], the AISC Manual of Steel Construction 13th edition [18:09], the AISC website steel-tools column-reinforcement program by Charlie Carter [13:28], AISC erection frame tolerance versus an adjustable plus or minus 3/8 inch tolerance [01:35], IBC 2000 / 2006 code adoption of the AISC spec [24:14], and the existence of pre-qualified end plates for seismic connections [11:57].

### Technical Details
Member-selection guidance: use a W12 minimum depth for floor framing so a three-row connection fits with reasonable stiffness, and avoid W8s where two rows barely develop capacity, especially when coped [06:14]. Move a girder to a 14-inch member when a wide thick flange would otherwise require cope reinforcement [06:45]. Avoid beams with 4-inch flanges at spandrels because deck bearing, gauge edge form, and a centered stud all compete for the same flange width [01:05]. For composite beams, limit deflection to avoid large cambers and design closer to 50% composite rather than 100%, ideally one stud per corrugation [07:48]. Beams parallel to deck ribs that need studs must land under a rib and may require blowing away deck to place the stud [08:49]. The eccentricity argument: shifting an incoming member to the center of the column flange reduces weak-axis eccentricity and improves the connection [00:00].

### Fabrication Notes
A 13/16 hole pair will not fit cleanly in a 4-inch flange, and such holes cannot run down the punch lines and must be drilled by hand, which is a cost the engineer should avoid creating [06:45]. Splicing a misordered or miscut member is acceptable practice and not a failure, but it must follow the AWS D1.1 CJP-groove rule, not a fabricator pulling stock from the scrap pile [05:11]. Building out a column to run a beam continuous over the top, adding continuity plates and widening the flange, can cost about the same fabrication as the original cantilever detail while removing field welding and shoring [02:39].

### Erection Notes
The perimeter-tolerance conflict is the sharpest erection point: a member erected to AISC frame tolerance can be off an inch or more, yet a deck edge form holds plus or minus 3/8 inch, leaving no room on a 4-inch spandrel flange [01:35]. On long cantilevers (15 feet or more) the speaker recommends running beams through and stacking columns to eliminate field shoring and field CJP welds and improve safety [02:39]. He recounts a hospital case where erectors left cantilever moment connections incomplete, which drove an owner in-house rule to run beams through and stack columns, though that rule does not make sense for a small beam on a 14-inch 311 column where a bolted end plate is better [03:39]. End-plate connections are preferred for cantilevers for ease and safety of erection [15:01].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The named failures are design and detailing errors rather than weld rejects. Brace details that show only the diagonal force with no load path, where the assumed force cannot physically pass through the roof diaphragm because the diaphragm capacity is nowhere near adequate [15:32]. Engineers calculating a brace member but never working out the load path or drag-strut and diaphragm-connection forces [15:32]. Crowding a 4-inch spandrel flange with deck bearing, edge form, and stud so the stud cannot be centered without burning away material [01:05]. The closing anecdote describes a connection firm questioning suspiciously light loads, only to learn the structural engineer of record had sent actual design loads rather than maximum, a load-transmittal error [28:37].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Showing reactions and forces on framing plans is more economical, avoids errors, and reduces RFIs [09:54]. Standard stock shear connections submitted up front cover the vast majority of shear connections and remove RFI churn, which is why firms agree to standardize them even when they currently charge for connection design [25:37]. Running cantilevers through at similar fabrication cost removes field welding and shoring cost [02:39]. The 2005 bolt-hole and continuity-plate changes both reduce required reinforcement and material, a direct fabrication savings on bolted moment connections and heavy plates [12:28]. Most detailing is done offshore in India, China, and South Korea, while connection design is done by registered in-house staff or a hired registered firm, sometimes on a cost-reimbursable basis [20:12].
