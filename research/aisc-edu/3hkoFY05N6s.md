## SteelDay 2012: 50 Tips for Designing Constructable Steel Buildings
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3hkoFY05N6s | Duration: 1:31:19 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
Clifford Schwinger, PE of The Harman Group teaches constructability as the art of structural engineering: the goal is the lowest total cost of a steel frame, not the lowest tonnage. His four principles are that simplicity equals economy, that least weight does not always equal least cost, that fewer pieces means greater economy, and that it is all about the connections [07:46]. He estimates roughly half the cost of a steel frame building is tied in some way to connections, because once the mill steel reaches the shop nearly everything that follows (detailing, drilling, coping, shipping, field connecting) is connection work [10:57]. The single most important tip is to show the reactions, moments, and axial forces on the contract drawings so connections are not over-designed for two to four times the real demand [09:21]. His tips target R=3 gravity and routine lateral systems, not high-seismic systems [05:41].

### Spec References
Cited verbatim by the speaker: AISC 360-10 (3610) [20:45]; AISC 341-10 (34110) seismic provisions, with Section D2.2 stating bearing-bolt shear values are permitted even when R is greater than three if holes are standard or short-slotted with the load perpendicular to the slot, provided bolts are pretensioned and faying surfaces prepared as for slip-critical [25:26]; AISC 303-10 (30310) Code of Standard Practice for delegating connection design and for required drawing information [31:36]; Table 3-6 ("table 36") uniform load capacities in the Steel Construction Manual [14:36]; Specification equation J10-1 ("j101") for checking whether stiffeners can be eliminated at hanger/concentrated-load points [74:56]; ASCE 7-05 and ASCE 7-10 ("c705 and asce 710") R-factor tables, with ordinary steel concentrically braced frame R=3.25 and ordinary steel moment frame R=3.5 [67:13]; AISC 360-05 ("3605") cited only as the prior edition that had used "slip critical" language now deleted [23:55]. He also references the AISC Steel Construction Manual, the Manual design examples, the Engineering Journal, and the AISC Steel Solutions Center as resources [33:09].

### Technical Details
Bolt grades named are A325 and A490, with a worked example that a fabricator standardizing on 7/8 in A325 will jump to a larger diameter such as 1-1/8 in A490 rather than swap grade at the same diameter when forces exceed economical A325 capacity [45:32]. Slip-critical connections are genuinely required only with oversized holes, or with slotted holes loaded parallel to the slot; all other cases usually accept pretensioned bearing bolts [21:50]. All slip-critical bolts are pretensioned but not all pretensioned bolts are slip-critical, and the two terms must not be used interchangeably [24:56]. Tension members in braced frames and trusses should be held to about 75% of yield capacity, because net-section loss at bolt holes governs and A992 (Fu/Fy = 65/50 = 1.3) is less forgiving than A36 (58/36 = 1.61) [43:00][82:08]. Strong-axis column moment of inertia is about 2.5 times the weak axis for W-shapes, so orient moment-frame columns for strong-axis bending [69:15].

### Fabrication Notes
Short-slotted holes with snug-tightened bolts are permitted for most shear connections and are needed to accommodate fabrication tolerance, temperature change at erection, and end rotation from camber; requiring standard holes at both ends of a cambered beam can make it impossible to bolt into place [30:32]. One-sided connections (single angle and single-plate shear tabs) are safe and economical, are the practical solution for skewed beams, and should not be prohibited [27:26]. Camber rules: do not camber moment-frame, braced-frame, short, or light beams, do not over-camber, do not specify camber under 3/4 in, and do not require camber be verified after erection because some camber dissipates [76:58]. Plate-girder web-to-flange welds are usually small fillet welds sized for shear flow, not CJP, and only the engineer of record knows the shear flow, so the EOR must provide shear and moment diagrams rather than delegate the weld sizing [59:26]. Favor fillet over groove welds and avoid arbitrary all-around or CJP moment welds [62:04].

### Erection Notes
Frame girders to column flanges and beams to column webs, since girders carry heavier reactions and seat more easily against a flange without sharing bolts [70:17]. Configure framing so no more than one beam frames into any one side of a column and so skewed connections are minimized; orient columns square to braced frames, preferably to the flanges, and never skew a braced-frame column [37:54]. Verify that members can physically be installed: the speaker recounts a field call where a correctly designed but tight HSS-brace gusset could not be rotated into position [64:11]. Keep brace and truss diagonals between 35 and 55 degrees so the connection stays compact [63:39]. Fewer beams means fewer connections and fewer crane picks, so maximize slab span (for example 3 in deck versus 1-1/2 in deck) to drop member count [64:42].

### Bolting and Welding Requirements
Bolting per AISC 360-10: use bearing bolts in standard or short-slotted holes for the majority of shear connections; reserve slip-critical for oversized holes or slots parallel to the load [21:50]. Even under AISC 341-10 Section D2.2 with R greater than three, design for bearing-bolt shear values but pretension the bolts and prepare faying surfaces as for slip-critical [25:26]. Pretensioned (not necessarily slip-critical) bolts or welds are required for the listed connections including column splices in tall buildings, beam-to-column connections in tall buildings, supports for large cranes, and supports for machinery or impact/reversal live loads [51:00]. Welding: strive for downhand or vertical welds, size plate-girder fillet welds for shear flow, and avoid unnecessary CJP welds [62:04]. No AWS D1.1 clause, electrode, preheat, or NDE procedure was cited; this is a design-constructability webinar, not a welding-procedure session.

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The headline error is the drawing note "connections shall be designed to support the full shear strength of the member," which is wasteful and hard to achieve once a beam is coped and holes are punched, especially in grade 50 steel [13:31]. A second common note, designing connections for 150% of the Table 3-6 uniform load capacity, is usually overly conservative but can be dangerously under-strength near a concentrated load: his W24x76 example shows a 113-kip connection demanded where the real reaction near the load is 234 kips [14:36]. Other recurring bad notes: requiring all connections be slip-critical, prohibiting one-sided connections, mandating all 3/4 in bolts and all standard holes, and arbitrarily requiring loads "in any direction" when they do not occur in all directions [18:44][27:26][29:31]. Failing to coordinate with the mechanical engineer produces unanticipated beam web penetrations discovered only at fabrication [55:21].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Roughly half of a steel frame's cost is connection-related, so over-specified connection notes add real cost without adding safety [10:57]. Fabricators price on tonnage and complexity together, not tonnage alone; stiffeners at every moment connection and "gingerbread" little pieces drive up the dollars-per-ton, with a change-order anecdote of small pieces billed near $8,000 per ton against a $2,000 per ton base [51:11][72:20]. Delegating connection design to the fabricator under the Code of Standard Practice is the more cost-effective path because each shop applies its preferred bolt diameter and detail [44:31]. But the EOR must still show reactions and any required stiffeners and web doubler plates on the bid drawings, because a fabricator cannot price accurately without knowing where stiffeners are required [57:55].
