## Truss Design and Construction
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KHYmUafNFMg | Duration: 1:26:59 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This webinar by Thomas Meyer of Magnusson Klemencic Associates teaches the full design workflow for what he calls a "straight over tackle" steel truss, a generally 2D long-span truss carrying floors or roofs, with decking at the top chord, a free open bottom chord plane, and a truss stability system [01:03][01:35]. The flow is design criteria, geometry, member shape selection, analysis, member design, connections, and stability, closing with a worked 90-foot span example [02:07]. The recurring theme is that long-span trusses behave differently from beam-and-girder floors: self-weight and connection-material weight are significant, serviceability and vibration often govern, and depth has an optimization sweet spot rather than a "deeper is always better" rule [02:38][11:30][72:22].
### Spec References
The speaker cites AISC Design Guide 11 (floor vibrations, with a note that a second edition has superseded the first edition shown) as the acceptance criteria source for walking and rhythmic vibration [08:18][31:15]; AISC 360 for member design checks of combined axial and bending forces [34:23][66:36]; the AISC Code of Standard Practice for camber tolerance of L/800 measured to the nearest support [07:17]; AISC Design Guide 29 for truss-end connection design analogous to vertical bracing connections [50:01]; and ISO 10137 for uncoordinated group-walking vibration criteria [29:44]. Acceleration limits cited: 1.5 percent g for airports, shopping malls, and indoor pedestrian bridges, 5 percent g for outdoor pedestrian bridges, and 2 percent g for diners adjacent to a dance floor [08:49][09:20][09:52].
### Technical Details
Span-to-depth ratios for the flat part of the weight-optimization curve are roughly 12 to 15 for roof trusses, 8 to 10 for strength-controlled floor trusses, and 6 to 8 for vibration-controlled floor trusses [14:04][14:35]. Web layouts covered are Pratt (longest diagonals in tension), Howe (longer members in compression, faster load path to support), and Warren (diagonals carry both tension and compression); the firm most often uses Warren for lighter truss weight in daily practice [16:09][76:41]. Chord force is estimated as WL squared over 8 divided by effective depth [11:30]; in the example a hand statics check gave 667 kip end shear, 16,750 kip-ft midspan moment, and an estimated 2,130 kip chord force under 1.2D plus 1.6L, against a computed 2,109 kip chord and 1,100 kip end diagonal [61:54][62:24][64:30]. Connection material runs about 15 percent of member centerline-to-centerline self-weight for a well-proportioned truss [53:05].
### Fabrication Notes
Member shapes: web members commonly double angles, HSS, or wide flanges; chords commonly wide flanges, HSS, or WT, with connection ease and architectural exposure driving the choice [22:01][23:34]. Chord-size changes at splices create filler-plate conditions; a filler thicker than 1/4 inch triggers a reduced bolt value, welding the filler to develop force, or extra bolts per AISC requirements [25:04][39:34][40:04]. Double-angle bolting rules: a leg under 6 inches allows one row, 6 inches allows two staggered rows, 8 inches allows two inline rows; stitch plates are required for compression members [44:12][44:42]. Slotting and wrapping an HSS over a thick gusset removes net section and can require an exterior patch plate, ideally welded in the shop [45:18][45:51]. The recommended posture is consistency: one bolt diameter, one N or X choice, one slip-critical or bearing choice across all connections [37:32].
### Erection Notes
Shipping governs splice location; full prefabricated trusses are rarely feasible (a 90-foot floor truss shipped whole only because the fab shop was down the road), so the engineer must talk to the contractor about truck limits and material sourcing to locate splices [19:51][20:56][21:28]. A photo shows ironworkers driving bolts into misaligned holes with sledgehammers, so field welding must be available as a fit-up fallback [38:33][39:03]. The preferred truss connection welds in the shop and bolts in the field [46:23]. Top-chord bracing assumptions must be carried through: if each floor beam braces the chord at 10-foot spacing, each of those beams must be designed for the bracing axial load [34:53][35:25]. Stability requires diaphragm trusses at the top-chord plane, bottom-chord tie beams with diagonals, attention to bracing stiffness not just strength, cantilever tips, and chord axial-load reversals [54:11][55:44][56:14].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The speaker frames most failures as design and detailing traps rather than weld or bolt rejections. Scheduling gussets by table without drawing the geometry produces connections that "work technically" but are clearly wrong, so each scheduled gusset should be drawn to verify geometry [43:11][43:42]. Eccentricity between gusset, knife plate, and HSS at an end connection becomes "RFI number one" if the centering assumption is not stated on the drawings [46:53]. Other named pitfalls: deep incoming beams clashing with a shallow W14 chord, doubler plates from net-section loss (avoid by upsizing the member), unsupported deck over a web-horizontal W14 needing shim support, and negative roof drainage or ponding from uncontrolled deflection [24:04][47:53][48:54][07:17]. No specific bolting or NDE rejection categories are named in this design-focused talk.
### Bid / Cost Implications
Truss weight is the cost driver and is non-linear with depth: past the optimization low point, longer web members add weight faster than chord savings reduce it, so over-deepening costs money [11:30][72:22]. Deeper trusses also add cladding, floor-to-floor height, and longer vertical-transport and duct runs, making the true optimum a whole-building cost conversation with contractor and architect [13:04]. Critically for estimating, drawings often go out to bid with members shown but no connection information on a delegated-connection job, so the bidder must add roughly 15 percent of centerline member weight for connection material, more when loads are high or proportions are poor [52:35][53:05][53:36]. The worked example showed flipping one tension diagonal to compression cut a chord moment from 2,200 to 1,500 kip-ft and trimmed truss weight from 58.1 to 55 tons, a real material saving from a layout change [68:47][69:47][70:18].
