## Erection Engineering: Stability During Construction
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ECCIeyWO8JY | Duration: 1:12:32 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This 2018 AISC Night School session (Session 6), presented by Benjamin Miller PE/SE of Rubin Associates, covers erection engineering: keeping a steel frame stable in its incomplete, sequenced condition during construction [00:02][01:32]. The EOR is responsible for stability of the final completed structure only, which exists once the final diaphragms, steel, and connections are buttoned up [06:13]. At every intermediate sequence the lateral system is incomplete, so the erector must add temporary measures, and the erection engineer designs them [06:45][03:36]. The thrust of the talk: an open, uncladded steel frame can draw far higher wind force than the finished building because of larger drag coefficients and longer unbraced lengths, so temporary bracing is engineered to the same probability of exceedance as the final design, not to a casual everyday wind [49:38][52:12].

### Spec References
Cited verbatim by the speaker: AISC Code of Standard Practice (AISC 303) Section 7.10.1 parts A and B (EOR identifies the lateral force resisting system and any required erection sequence) [08:15][22:43]; Section 7.10.2 (EOR identifies non-structural elements of the lateral system) [17:04]; Section 7.10.3 (loads the erector need not consider) [25:51]; Section 3.1.2 part B (EOR shows shears, moments, axial and transfer forces for members and connections) [12:29]. Also cited: ASCE 37 (Design Loads on Structures During Construction), specifically Chapter 6 wind, Section 6.2.1 wind-speed reduction factors, and Section 6.2.2 shielding [07:15][39:11][41:49][42:51]; ASCE 7 with Figure 29.5-2 force coefficients [43:23][46:01]; IBC; the Steel Construction Manual (14th edition design example referenced) [33:34][68:03]; SJI Technical Digest 9 (handling and erection of steel joists and joist girders) [07:15]; AISC Design Guide 10 (low-rise buildings) [07:15]; and PCI Architectural Precast Concrete, 3rd edition [57:48].

### Technical Details
Wind during construction starts from ASCE 7 pressures, then ASCE 37 Section 6.2.1 allows a wind-speed reduction tied to construction duration; a sub-six-week erection earns a 0.75 factor on wind speed, which, squared into pressure, yields about 56% of design pressure [39:42][42:21]. ASCE 37 Section 6.2.2 permits a 15% shielding reduction on the fourth and subsequent elements (85%), while the first three rows take 100% [42:51]. That relief is offset by force coefficients of 1.6 to 2.0 for flat-sided open framework from ASCE 7 Figure 29.5-2, governed by solidity ratio; roughly 1.95 for a single wide-flange [43:23][46:33]. In the 14th-edition example the construction east-west base shear hit 429 kips against a final design base shear of 170 kips [47:34][48:05]. Truss element stability was analyzed under hook versus at-set: chords web-vertical gave a top-chord unity of 5.9 at set (needing bracing before crane release), but turning the chords horizontal dropped the same members to 0.85 [60:20][61:54][62:26].

### Fabrication Notes
Detailing choices drive how much temporary bracing the field needs. A flange-plate moment connection using a WT instead of a double angle makes all four bolts strain-compatible and effective for moment, where the angle leaves the middle two bolts largely ineffective per the AISC Manual; the WT version let them skip temporary cable bracing on those moment lines [70:05][70:36]. A welded HSS-to-gusset joint with a single 3/4-inch erection bolt loaded by the HSS sidewalls (HSS 8x8 example) was limited to about 750 lb in bending, essentially zero stability until field welds are placed [70:36][71:37]. A bolted knife plate with four corner bolts is preferred because the erector can develop capacity at set and drop some cable bracing [72:07]. Base-plate detailing matters: moving anchor rods outside the column shape rather than inside reduced a wind-column base anchorage from 146% to a workable utilization without changing anchor size or embedment, only enlarging the base plate [68:03][69:04].

### Erection Notes
The erection engineer needs a blocking (sequence) plan from the erector, modeled tier by tier (column-splice to column-splice), then loads each stage per ASCE 37 including staged material, wind, and any snow/ice [35:38][36:09][37:39]. Temporary cable bracing is sized to the engineered event, not to a 20 to 25 mph site wind, which is why erectors see "so much bracing" [52:12]. Cable detail: wrap the column about 18 inches up with a turnbuckle to keep the cable taut, and weld an angle across the back of the column so the wrapped cable cannot ride up under 70 mph loading [54:14][54:45][55:16]. Erectors dislike wire rope over 1/2 inch; this job used two lines of 5/8-inch wire rope rather than going to 3/4 inch [52:42][53:43]. Temporary bracing must stay until the permanent lateral system and its diaphragms are in place per the Code of Standard Practice, even after all steel is erected [38:09][56:18]. Where a steel frame ties into perimeter precast erected first, cable bracing was too flexible for the precaster's load limits, so hard braces (W14x74 noted) replaced cables [56:48][58:49].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
This is a design webinar, so it names design and documentation failures rather than weld or bolt rejections. The recurring failure is incomplete EOR documentation: not fully describing the lateral load path and diaphragm-to-frame load transfer, not stating whether column anchorages are pinned or fixed, and not identifying non-structural lateral elements or how long temporary bracing must remain, which forces early RFIs [09:51][11:26][22:13][39:11]. An ambiguous shoring note (10 kips per joist versus 5 and 5) needed a phone call to resolve [24:18][25:19]. A specification that referenced both Code of Standard Practice and ASCE 37 created a direct contradiction on hurricane loading; the erector is flagged to red-flag this and resolve it by RFI, exclusion, or bid clarification [27:23][31:31][33:03].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Bringing erection engineering in earlier can save schedule and reduce temporary measures, for example starting erection at a braced frame line to engage the permanent lateral system sooner [05:10][15:30]. Conflicting spec language carries real cost: the hurricane-contingency plan was dropped because implementation cost was excessive, a decision reached jointly with owner and GC [32:31]. Bracing magnitude is a cost driver because construction base shear here was 2.5 times the final design shear, so material staging, cable runs, and the deck contractor cutting deck around bracing all add field cost [48:05][56:18]. Detailing that lets the erector release a pick under self weight or drop a brace line (WT moment connections, bolted knife plates, horizontal truss chords) directly reduces erection labor and rented bracing [62:26][70:36][72:07].
