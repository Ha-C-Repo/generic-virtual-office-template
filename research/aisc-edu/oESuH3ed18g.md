### Core Concept
This is part one of a two-part AISC webinar presented by Kirk Marchand of Protection Engineering Consultants on March 17 2016, scoped to blast loads and their interaction with buildings, with part two (Aldo McKay) covering structural response [01:33]. The core teaching is how to predict the air-blast pressure-time history on a steel structure from a high-explosive event: define the explosion source, convert the charge to a TNT-equivalent weight, compute a scaled standoff Z, and read peak pressure and impulse off empirical curves to build a simplified design load [38:46], [58:18]. It is a loads-prediction primer, not a connection, fabrication, or welding design course, so most of the nine fabricator dimensions are thin here.

### Technical Details
An ideal free-field (side-on) blast wave is an instantaneous rise to peak pressure followed by a Friedlander decay to ambient and then a negative phase [43:22], [44:54]. The controlling design quantities are peak side-on pressure, peak reflected pressure, and specific impulse (the time integral of pressure, a force per unit area) [45:25]. Reflected pressure exceeds incident pressure by a factor of 2 for weak shocks under about 15 psi but can reach a factor near 12 to 13 for very strong shocks around 1000 psi [54:08], [55:11]. Loads are simplified to an equivalent triangular pulse with duration equal to twice the impulse divided by peak pressure [58:49]. Face assignment on a building: the front wall takes reflected pressure with angle-of-incidence reduction, while sides, roof, and back wall take incident (side-on) pressure at progressively longer combined standoffs [60:20], [62:56]. Pressure decays with standoff at roughly an exponent of 2.8 and impulse at roughly 1.25, so every foot of standoff materially reduces the load [57:17].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
No weld or bolt inspection failures are discussed. The conceptual errors flagged are analytical: treating blast as a static or equivalent wind load (it is a transient millisecond dynamic event that does not convert cleanly to an equivalent static or seismic load) [13:22]; assuming TNT-equivalence is accurate at all standoffs when equivalency factors for pressure and impulse differ and vary with atmosphere and standoff [16:59], [39:47]; over-relying on blast walls, since an expanding shock recombines a short distance behind the wall [11:49]; and oversimplifying the load triangle when the structure period is near the load duration [59:50].

### Bid / Cost Implications
No bid pricing, schedule durations, or estimating numbers are given. The cost-relevant takeaway for a fabricator is that blast resistance is a project-specification-driven add scope (antiterrorism requirements for courthouses or government facilities, or industrial vapor-cloud, pressure-vessel, and steam-explosion scenarios) [26:47], [27:47], and that design efficiency, and therefore steel weight, is governed heavily by available standoff [57:47]. Whether negative-phase pressure must be considered is set by the project specifications, not chosen by the contractor [82:38].
