## Steel  Framed Stairway Design Pt 2

### Core Concept
This is part 2 of an AISC webinar by Adam Friedman (author of the then-forthcoming AISC Design Guide 34, Steel-Framed Stairway Design), focused on seismic design of steel stairs as nonstructural components, the new ASCE 7-16 serviceability provisions for accommodating inter-story drift, and the delegated-design relationship between the structural engineer of record (SER) and the fabricator's specialty structural engineer (SSE) [01:03][02:34]. The central message is that an egress stair must either be detailed to move freely during a seismic event (sliding or ductile connection) or be locked up and designed as part of the building's seismic force resisting system; the worst outcome is a stair that inadvertently acts as an unintended brace and draws force it was never designed for [22:41][50:00].

### Technical Details
The horizontal force simplifies for an egress stair to FP stairs = 0.24 SDS Wp times [1 + 2(Z/H)], using Ip 1.5, ap 1.0, Rp 2.5, with Z/H conservatively 1.0 [11:19][26:30]. ASCE 7-16 Table 13.5-1 splits the old single line into egress-stair members (ap 1.0, Rp 2.5, overstrength dropped from 2.5 to 2.0) and a new fasteners/attachments line (ap 2.5, Rp 2.5), which raises bolt/weld/dowel forces about 250 percent versus 7-10 [06:14][08:16]. Seismic displacement DpI from Section 13.3.2.1 can be 1 to 2.5 inches per roughly 8 feet of height difference and can exceed 4 inches on tall towers, which then conflicts with the IBC 4-inch-sphere guard opening limit [27:50][28:23]. Section 13.5.10 gives three accommodation options: (A) sliding connection with oversized/slotted holes plus keeper plates or end stops, accommodate DpI or 0.5 in min; (B) bearing detail without a stop, accommodate 1.5 DpI or 1 in min; (C) metal supports with rotation capacity, 1.5 DpI or 1 in min with no brittle failure modes permitted [19:01][20:33]. Locking the stair at both ends forces it into the building model designed for the structure overstrength, minimum 2.5 [22:10]. The deformation-to-force check (PL/AE rearranged) showed a 1-inch imposed movement on a C12x20.7 stringer over a 12-foot span generating about 1,224 kips, illustrating why rigid both-end details are unworkable [39:07].
### Fabrication Notes
The worked stringer example used a rectangular HSS 12x2x1/4 ASTM A500 Grade C with checkered-plate treads and risers, sized through Specification Section G1 and Manual Table 3-12 [56:42][57:13]. Landing diaphragms are framed like conventional floors, the speaker's preferred method being deck plus concrete over metal deck; some fabricators use stiffened plate or thicker checker/diamond plate, but quantifying built-up-beam tread/riser capacity is hard because the web-height-to-thickness ratio fails AISC and AISI local-buckling criteria, pushing designers to finite element or rational methods [44:14][46:17]. Galvanizing was covered: prefer matched fasteners and metals, watch piece-size and assembly-size limits at the galvanizer's kettle, and guard against warpage per ASTM A384 [74:18][75:22]. Tolerances are cleanest when all parties adopt the AISC Specification, Manual, and Code of Standard Practice; concrete tolerances per ACI 117 and masonry per ASTM C90 are looser, so a quarter-inch minimum to half-inch preferred gap absorbs the variation [71:44][73:17].
### Erection Notes
A practical drift detail sets a threaded rod with a jam nut and washer plate cast into the slab depth so the erector can drop the stair, cut loose, add the top washer and finger-tight upset nut, and keep moving without waiting on the concrete pour, an explicit schedule efficiency [32:29][32:59]. When concrete is not yet placed and the stair is used during construction, temporary blocking must be provided until the slab is poured [33:00]. The speaker stressed field access for bolting where a stringer sits a half inch off a concrete or masonry core wall, the possible need for flange block-outs at shear tabs, and confirming whether masonry is in place before the stair is set, with temporary supports as needed [75:53][76:24]. Maximize repetition in framing and connections, build erection aids that let a crew drop each stair assembly in and keep moving, and field-verify dimensions against actual construction rather than the plan gap [76:24][73:47]. Avoid field welding on galvanized work; shop-weld and field-bolt where possible [74:49].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The headline failure mode is the stair becoming an inadvertent load path: in a moment-frame building a rigidly tied stair acts as a brace, becomes the stiffer seismic force resisting system, and fails at the stair connections or in the members because nobody designed it for that [23:42][24:12]. Brittle limit states (bolt shear, weld rupture, weld fracture) cannot govern when relying on option C rotation capacity [20:33][37:01]. Construction errors flagged include concrete or debris dumped into the movement gap or oversized hole, which destroys the seismic displacement allowance and becomes a maintenance concern [38:03]. Specification mismatches were called out as a frequent source of rejection and RFIs, especially stock or purchased specs with an arbitrary quarter-inch deflection limit never reviewed for the project, and conflicts between the project spec and the design drawings [66:35][67:06].
### Bid / Cost Implications
If the drawings show no stair bracing, that bracing is not in the fabricator's price, so a fabricator or delegated designer should raise it before bid or clarify by RFI rather than absorb it later [48:54][49:24]. The Code of Standard Practice rule that design drawings govern over the spec means that if a delegated-design spec is paired with detailed stair drawings, a bidder will assume the SER already completed the design; conceptual or suggested details must be labeled as such in the bid documents [65:32][66:03]. Large seismic displacements are explicitly described as very costly and difficult to accommodate, so the delegated designer should request accurate per-floor drift values from the SER rather than defaulting to the conservative equation 13.3-8 upper bound [28:23][55:00]. Scope and seal expectations, including any request that the SSE seal fabricator-created shop drawings, must be settled at the bidding stage, not at submittal, since many fabricators have no in-house PE and a third-party engineer sealing drawings created by others raises jurisdiction and liability problems [69:38][71:44]. Stock quarter-inch deflection limits drive avoidable cost across design, fabrication, detailing, and erection [67:06].
