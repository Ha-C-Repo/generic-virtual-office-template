## Ethics and Accountability

### Core Concept
This is a facilitated professional-ethics session for structural engineers, fabricators, and detailers, not a code-clause technical seminar. The throughline is that ethics risk drops when a firm pairs a strong ethical culture with an actively supported code of conduct, and that "doing the right thing" holds even when it costs schedule or a client relationship [05:05][14:31]. The presenter, a Minnesota structural engineer of ~55 years in practice, builds toward a real connection-design case study where an engineer of record reported under-designed steel connections to the building official rather than let them be covered with sheetrock [56:21][59:53].
### Spec References
The speaker references the AISC Code of Standard Practice, specifically Part 3.1.2, on the option that allows a fabricator to design connections from loads shown on the design drawings, tied to a Charlie Carter presentation earlier in the conference [03:01][03:34][04:35]. He names the word "allow" versus "require" as the controlling distinction in that provision. No AISC 360 chapter, AISC 341 seismic section, AWS D1.1 clause, RCSC pretension method, or numbered Design Guide is cited in this session. Treat the takeaway as general principles, with only the Code of Standard Practice Part 3.1.2 cited verbatim.
### Technical Details
The core technical thread is delegated connection design under the Code of Standard Practice: the engineer of record shows loads, the fabricator may design connections to those loads, and the EOR remains responsible for those connections even when their design is delayed or delegated [04:05][61:55][62:25]. The case study involves a five-story structural steel frame building with girder and collector connections to braced frames that did not match the design drawings [56:21][57:22]. The presenter also voices a personal preference against post-installed connectors in tension and notes finding fasteners he could turn and pull out of a building by hand, an inspection-quality point [63:15][63:47].
### Fabrication Notes
The pivotal fabrication failure is sequencing: steel shop drawings were started off the DD-phase (mill order) set, while connections were finalized only in the CD set, so the submitted shop drawings carried inaccurate connections and were rejected [57:22]. Portions of the shop drawings were rejected for the girder and collector-to-brace-frame connections specifically. The broader lesson for shop drawing handling is to not accept incomplete submittals pushed out under schedule pressure, and to cycle an incomplete set back rather than process it [69:53][70:24].
### Erection Notes
Construction advanced on unapproved, rejected shop drawings, and the rejected connections were erected as shown and then re-evaluated at 40 to 50 percent under design even after taking all code-allowed reductions [57:52][58:22]. The fix was field remediation: the general contractor hired its own PE to design field fixes for the connections, which were submitted as shop drawings, reviewed, and approved, with no schedule interruption [60:23]. A separate site-safety anecdote covers an untied worker hanging out a nine-story window, framing field safety as a moral duty even outside contractual scope [22:37].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The named failures are connections built under design (40 to 50 percent), construction proceeding on rejected shop drawings, and a general contractor that stopped returning calls and pressed ahead on schedule while the owner was kept unaware of the defect [57:52][58:22][58:52]. Plan stamping (signing for work one did not design) is flagged as a prohibited practice, summarized as "he who designs signs" [09:20]. The presenter also calls out special inspectors not actually performing the work, including fasteners that pulled out and backfill that settled six inches despite a passing test report [63:15][64:47].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Schedule incentives and liquidated damages drove the bad behavior: completion bonuses plus penalties pushed the contractor to bury a defect, raising whether liquidated damages themselves create an ethical pressure that values completion over safety [56:51][67:20][67:50]. The remediation cost (re-evaluation PE, fabricator material, erector labor) was carried by the general contractor with no claims, and the project still finished on time [66:49]. The operational cost lesson is to write reasonable shop-drawing review time into the specification and to log every shop drawing in and out, since slow review can itself become the basis of a delay claim [69:22][69:53].
