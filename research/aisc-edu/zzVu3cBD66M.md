### Core Concept
This webinar teaches the fundamentals and behavior of mechanical fasteners, not day-to-day design procedure, so the engineer understands the basis for the design rules [00:01]. The speaker, addressing structural engineers with inspectors, fabricators, and erectors also present [01:02], stresses that for bolting the responsibility for picking the right bolt type and grade, designing the fasteners, installation, and inspection all fall on the engineer, unlike welding where responsibility is more distributed [01:33]. The recurring method is to start from the bolt tension test as the benchmark, then resolve every connection through free body diagrams to find whether each bolt acts in shear, bearing, tension, or a combination [04:08][13:20].

### Technical Details
A307 grade A has a minimum tensile strength of 60 ksi, acceptable for static loads, cheaper than high strength bolts, with pretension that is indeterminate so it is never counted on [03:38][04:38][05:09]. A325 minimum tensile strength is 120 ksi (105 ksi above 1 inch diameter, an artifact the speaker says should be removed since designers always use 120) [06:42][07:14]. A490 minimum tensile strength is 150 ksi with an upper limit of 170 ksi to keep the steel ductile and avoid brittle failure [07:45]. Type 1 versus type 3 (weathering steel) does not change strength, so the designer just picks A325 or A490 [05:09][07:14]. High strength steels show no yield point on the tension curve, so there is no design basis tied to bolt yield; the entire strength framework rests on ultimate tensile strength [08:46][09:17]. Proof load appears in the ASTM spec but plays no role in design, installation, or inspection [10:18]. Connections route load by shear in the bolt and bearing on the connected material, by friction if pretensioned, by tension, or by combined tension and shear handled with an interaction equation [12:19][23:06].
### Fabrication Notes
Fasteners and nuts are mass-identified by markings on the head and nut, so verification is a matter of reading the mark [02:05]. Selecting the matching A563 nut per the spec gives a nut that will not fail before the body of the fastener, so no further nut design is required [11:18]. Bolt, nut, and washer sets are common but not universal, and washers are not always required [11:48]. ### Erection Notes
The segment does not cover field erection sequencing, bracing, crane or rigging, or site coordination directly. It establishes the behavior an erector must respect: a plain dropped-in bolt with no nut and no clamping force is a bearing-type connection with no friction, versus a pretensioned bolt that transfers load by friction [26:40][27:13]. Pretension, when needed, can be induced in A325 and A490 (but not reliably in A307), with the how-to-install and how-to-inspect deferred to a later part [05:09][07:14].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The speaker states there are very few real problems with bolts; most disputes are interpretation of specification requirements [21:04]. Real failures concentrate where bolts are in tension, especially with fatigue and prying action [21:35]. Prying acts like a lever and raises the bolt force above the simple P over 4 division, easily 20 to 30 percent higher and sometimes more, so the calculation must be made [20:01][20:33]. He cites the Kemper Arena roof failure (referenced as the "Keer Arena," roughly Kansas City, about 15 to 20 years prior) where large trusses connected to a perpendicular roof system, and prying force in that tension connection was a predominant feature of the collapse [21:35][22:05][22:36].
### Bid / Cost Implications
A307 common bolts are cheaper than high strength bolts and are a sound choice for static loads, so grade selection is a direct material-cost lever where seismic and fatigue demands do not force high strength [04:38]. The hidden estimating risk is tension connections with prying, where the controlling bolt force can run 20 to 30 percent above the naive count-based load, meaning the true bolt size or count is larger than a quick takeoff suggests [20:33].
