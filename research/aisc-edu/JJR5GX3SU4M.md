## AISC Research:  Seismic Evaluation and Retrofit of Concentrically Braced Frames
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JJR5GX3SU4M | Duration: 1:24:35 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
Charles Roeder and Dawn Lehman present roughly 15 years of NSF and AISC funded research on the seismic behavior, evaluation, and retrofit of concentrically braced frames, contrasting modern special concentrically braced frames (SCBF) against the non-ductile braced frames (termed NCBF) built before 1988 [00:00, 07:45]. The controlling principle is capacity-based design: connections, beams, and columns are designed for the expected capacity the brace will actually deliver in an earthquake, not the force the brace was designed for [06:14, 09:17]. The desired failure is a ductile brace fracture after large cyclic deformation, and the design goal is to develop multiple "links" of yielding (a chain analogy) so secondary yield mechanisms add deformation capacity before the brace fractures [13:23, 16:30].

### Spec References
Speakers cited AISC 341 seismic provisions for SCBF and OCBF, the R=3 provision, ASCE 41 (existing steel evaluation, the current standard being replaced), the proposed AISC 342 standard (named repeatedly, with chapters C for component evaluation, D/E for modeling) [42:42, 49:24], ASCE 7-16 spectra and the 1979 UBC for comparison [52:35], the Whitmore section method and the recommended 3-4-5 (37 degree) gusset spread angle versus the traditional 30 degree [18:31, 19:01], the 2t linear gusset clearance model versus the elliptical (8t) clearance model [17:30, 20:02], and "demand-critical welds" [10:47, 27:10]. Block shear was noted as a relaxed criterion not yet approved by AISC [19:31, 79:05]. No AISC 360 chapter, table number, or Design Guide number was stated.

### Technical Details
A brace in compression buckles, forms a mid-length plastic hinge, and loses compressive capacity over cycles as it is repeatedly straightened, so braces are matched in tension-compression pairs [03:38, 05:10]. Three deficiency categories drive retrofit: cross-sectionally slender braces (high b/t, the most severe, common in pre-1988 frames) [50:58, 53:34], undersized non-demand-critical welds (brace-to-gusset and the interface welds between gusset, beam, and column) with demand-capacity ratios greater than 1 [37:31, 56:18], and weak Chevron beams and columns [63:36]. The balanced design procedure designs the brace-to-gusset weld for the brace tensile capacity and the gusset-to-frame weld for the gusset plate capacity, uses the elliptical clearance for a thinner more compact gusset, and deliberately permits gusset yielding, bolt-hole elongation, and beam yielding as secondary mechanisms [17:00, 66:13, 78:33]. The proposed AISC 342 models the gusset as a rotational spring with a rigid end zone over the full column-side gusset length and about 3/4 of the beam-side length [47:52, 48:22].

### Fabrication Notes
The headline retrofit is filling a slender hollow HSS brace with concrete (pea gravel mix so aggregate is not too large), with the concrete deliberately stopped short of the gusset connection so it does not stiffen the end and make the brace stronger rather than more ductile [25:35, 77:33]. A form is set inside the brace to hold the gap from the gusset, then concrete is poured through a hole at the far end [77:33, 78:03]. Weld overlays add metal to undersized welds to bring them to demand-critical size and behaved well in testing [27:10, 37:31]. For thin beam webs at the connection, a cover plate is recommended so the web reaches about 75 to 80 percent of the gusset plate thickness [72:23, 74:58]. The example brace named for retrofit economy was an HSS 8x8x3/8 [70:17].

The closest erection-relevant guidance is the gusset clearance detail that physically allows the brace to buckle out of plane during the earthquake, and the note that the lateral system is often not continuous vertically and column splices are a concern in older buildings [18:00, 68:46]. ### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Pre-1988 frames almost universally used overly slender braces, Chevron configurations sized only for design loads (one fifth to one tenth of current requirements), and welds that were undersized and not tough (not demand-critical) [10:17, 30:16]. Double shear plates joining web and gusset separately work against each other and perform worse than a single continuous shear plate [27:40, 30:48]. Failure of the vertical shear plate connecting gusset and beam to column is a severe problem because beam and column then separate [27:40]. Applying the 2t linear clearance to a rectangular plate produces huge oversized gusset plates and poor performance [80:36, 81:06]. Over-conservatism is itself an error: an oversized gusset or beam reduces inelastic deformation capacity, so members should be sized to what they need, not bigger [21:33, 22:34].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The research repeatedly frames retrofit choices as cost decisions. Filling a brace with concrete is called the more economical option versus replacing the brace, and was the correct answer to the assessment question on the HSS 8x8x3/8 [50:58, 70:17]. A weak beam (DCR up to about 2.5) does not change collapse probability, so replacing all the beams in a building is unnecessary cost, a stated money saver [64:40, 65:43, 67:13]. Properly retrofitting brace, weld, and gusset yields the same collapse probability as a code SCBF, with a 2475-year collapse probability between 5 and 20 percent [67:44, 68:14]. Single continuous shear plates and existing welds can often be kept and overlaid rather than removed and replaced [78:33].

For Ivan's takeoff, this reinforces that brace and gusset weld sizing keys off expected brace capacity, not design force, so any SCBF/braced-frame bid should price gusset welds and demand-critical weld material against the brace tensile capacity rather than the analysis load.
