## Benefits of Steel in Accelerated Bridge Construction

### Core Concept
This is an AASHTO/NSBA-oriented highway-bridge webinar by Michael Cuomo of CME Associates, not a building-steel or AISC 360 talk. The thesis is that structural steel is a strong choice for accelerated bridge construction (ABC) because steel beams are lighter than equivalent concrete, accommodate large force and moment reversals during lifting and moving, and fabricate to tight tolerances for fast field fit-up [05:11][07:13]. Two ABC families are covered: full bridge-system moves on self-propelled modular transporters (SPMTs) or lateral slides [08:44], and prefabricated modular deck-beam units (steel girders with a precast deck cast on top) erected span by span [30:18][30:48]. A central design idea is the link slab, a short jointless deck detail that lets simple-span steel beams behave as a continuous-deck bridge without field splices [43:05].

### Technical Details
SPMT capacity is estimated at roughly 60,000 lb per axle line, with modular six-line, twelve-line, and twelve-line double-wide (24-axle) configurations bolted together [18:31][19:32]. Lift pick points run about 0.15L from the span ends to manage negative bending in girder and deck during the move [17:29][38:31]. A man-made-earthquake hypothesis was validated by physical SPMT testing in a Houston yard (15, 25, and 50 percent load cases, accelerometers, Utah State University), producing vertical and horizontal response-spectrum curves; starting and stopping generated larger lateral forces than vertical bounce, on the order of 5 to 10 percent of bridge mass as a horizontal design force [21:34][24:11][27:16][29:18]. Link-slab design keeps the slab thin, with a debond zone of about 5 percent of span on each side; doubling slab thickness raises the slab moment by a factor of eight (h-cubed), so thicker is worse [44:06][45:39].

### Fabrication Notes
The talk frames fabrication economically rather than by shop procedure. Steel fabricates to much tighter tolerances than precast concrete, which drives better field fit-up on tight ABC schedules [08:14]. In the cost study, fabrication cost was split by part: flanges at about 1.13 dollars per pound, webs at 0.97 dollars per pound, and stiffeners and connection plates at about 2.00 dollars per pound, because fitting and welding a connection plate into a plate girder is labor-intensive handwork [57:31][58:01]. Webs were noted as the cheapest steel on the girder; the SIMON program optimizes flange and web sizes for a cost-effective section [56:31].

### Erection Notes
Modular steel deck-beam units (for example about 85 ft, over 200,000 lb) were set as single-crane picks with roughly a 500-ton hydraulic crane; an equivalent concrete double-T about 25 percent heavier could force a two-crane pick and complicate erection [30:48][31:18]. The I-93 Fast 14 project in Medford MA replaced three- and four-span interstate bridges in 55-hour weekend closures using six-section cross-sections, high-early-strength concrete (about 4,000 psi in four to five hours) for the closure pours, and span-by-span erection that eliminated field splices [37:59][40:32]. Lateral-slide moves use Teflon sliding bearings; tested lubricants included Dawn dish soap, 10W-40 motor oil, and bridge-bearing grease, with oil and grease giving lower friction [70:30]. SPMT moves are typically under 100 to 200 yards but range from 50 ft to over a mile; tire-pressure loads approximate a fully loaded truck, so driving over buried utilities is generally acceptable [89:39][90:10].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The recurring failure named is the leaking bridge deck expansion joint: joints at every pier leak, and the speaker showed beam-end and pier corrosion with a girder web over 75 percent section-loss that crippled because it lacked a bearing stiffener, on bridges only built in the mid-to-late 1970s [34:52][35:57]. Over-conservative link slabs that are made too thick crack more, not less, because slab moment scales with thickness cubed [45:39]. The simple-made-continuous-for-live-load moment connection at the pier (post-tensioning, grouting, bottom-flange bolted splice) was flagged as complex with documented live-load cracking risk at the pier, versus the simpler link slab [42:34].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The headline takeaway for estimating: least weight is not least total cost. A two-span 122 ft by 122 ft continuous girder bridge totaled about 310,635 dollars in steel but added roughly 24,000 dollars of negative-moment deck rebar (about 12,000 lb), ten bolted field splices at near 6,000 dollars each (about 58,000 dollars total), bearings, and roughly 20,000 dollars of shoring towers, for about 419,000 dollars [58:01][62:13][65:20]. The link-slab span-by-span alternative used about 70,000 dollars more steel but eliminated the bolted splices and shoring and cut deck rebar to about 1,000 lb, landing roughly 30,000 dollars cheaper overall and erecting far faster [63:17][66:22]. Bolt cost is driven by hole drilling and field tensioning labor, not the bolt itself; the study used 50 dollars per bolt from a national bridge fabricator, and the speaker stressed costs vary widely by region [54:29][60:40][62:13].
