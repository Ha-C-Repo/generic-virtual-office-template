## ASCE 37: Design Loads on Structures During Construction [E17a]

### Core Concept
The webinar, presented by Dave Ruby and Jeff Gasparot of Ruby and Associates, treats ASCE 37 as a source of construction-period loads but not as a design code. The repeated thesis is that ASCE 37 supplies the loads (dead, live, construction, wind, lateral earth pressure, temporary supports, sequencing effects) but does not say when, where, why, or how to apply them, and assigns no responsible party for field supervision [02:41][11:23][17:31][28:00]. The structure during erection is often controlled by higher wind because the open frame has no siding and no completed lateral load resisting system, so members and connections may need to be stronger than the permanent design required [14:28][15:29][34:00]. The governing idea is stability and load path: the frame must be stable at every stage as it is built one piece at a time, which means the erector runs the model many times, not once [07:17][08:21].

### Technical Details
Wind pressure scales with velocity squared, so a 100 mph wind is 100 times a 10 mph wind, not 10 times [50:29]. Erection bracing is normally analyzed to about 73 mph: starting from the 115 mph LRFD map, divide by 1.26 to reach 92 mph ASD, then apply the 0.8 duration reduction to land near 73 mph [53:35][54:06]. A worked AISC design example showed permanent base shear of 168 kips rising to roughly 602 to 608 kips on the fully bare frame, because bare beams catch wind that would otherwise pass between decked floors [25:12][40:05]. The rule of thumb for cutting a truss free from the crane is span in inches over flange width (L over B): under 80 is generally fine, 80 to 100 needs a look, over 100 calls for engineering help [76:08]. Real-world reference: a 60 to 65 mph Midwest windstorm equates to about 9 to 13 psf, roughly half of full design and about 65 percent of the 73 mph erection-analysis load [56:41][57:43].
### Fabrication Notes
The webinar is erection-focused and gives little direct shop-fabrication guidance. The one clear fabrication-relevant point is truss top chord geometry: a wide-flange top chord turned so the web is horizontal gives substantially more bending capacity and axial strength than an 8-inch upright flange, which matters for unbraced setting length and weak-axis wind resistance [69:27][72:30]. It also notes that ultra-light framing chosen to minimize tonnage is not necessarily least cost once erection labor and temporary works are added [73:00][74:02].
### Erection Notes
Erection is the heart of the talk. Stability and a continuous load path must exist at every stage, the permanent lateral system may not yet be present, and the actual build sequence is often dictated by site logistics rather than the designer's intent [04:11][08:21]. The lateral load resisting system must be known so the erector can use completed portions as bracing, and special schedules for precast, masonry, or cladding that act as the final lateral system must be communicated since the first 15 to 25 percent of cladding adds wind sail without stability [20:37][23:40][50:00]. Case studies covered an 80-foot column on an undersized 6-foot by 6-foot foundation that would slide under full ASCE 37 wind (factor of safety near one at 60 mph), and a one-mile-long light industrial building where 40-foot joists on the column line and 8-inch truss top chords forced temporary sway frames and wire-rope horizontal bracing until enough deck was placed for diaphragm action [63:19][67:26][70:59]. Hurricane plans are required during the roughly eight-month hurricane season on the coast and must be installable in a day or two, with constant weather monitoring [51:01][104:00].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
A 45-foot freestanding column failed during fit-up: anchors were 3/4-inch A36 threaded rod on leveling nuts with no shims, set in a 6-inch by 6-inch pattern inside a 24-inch by 24-inch base plate [58:43][59:13]. Forcing a girder into the column applied about a 500-pound incidental load, generating roughly 22 kip-feet of moment against an anchor capacity near 19 kip-feet, causing tensile rupture and toppling [60:16][61:49]. The named lessons: push anchor bolts outside the column profile, question a tight anchor pattern inside a large base plate, never rely on leveling nuts to carry dead load, and add shims as cheap insurance [62:19][125:00]. A church roof collapse came from masonry shear walls shown only on architectural drawings and omitted from the structural set, leaving no load path [21:38].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Temporary works (false work, wire-rope bracing, temporary sway frames, added members to shorten unbraced length, hurricane bracing) are real costs that are routinely absent from the bid number, especially on light structures [71:59][73:00]. The explicit warning to erectors is do not base a bid on tonnage alone, because the lightest framing often carries the highest erection cost and you will take it on the chin [74:02]. When the lateral load resisting system is not in the general notes, fire an RFI back at the EOR asking for it in both directions before committing [74:34]. Wind contingency is also seasonal: building off hurricane season lets the erector use the lower 115 mph base, while in season the Miami case could require on the order of 200 times the temporary bracing [51:01][52:02].
