## Get Fired Up
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K5o3Db4InEs | Duration: 1:03:11 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Maria Garlock (Princeton, 2016 AISC T.R. Higgins lecturer) teaches what a practicing structural engineer should know about fire design, contrasting the traditional prescriptive approach with structural fire engineering, which is performance-based design that treats fire as a thermal load and analyzes the real structural response to a real fire [00:02][07:17][12:26]. The central message is that designers have choices and do not have to give up efficiency, economy, and elegance for fire protection, because a structure can sometimes be designed to resist a fire rather than be shielded from it [01:36][12:57]. A key teaching point is that the fire-resistive rating is principally a thermal comparative test, not a structural test, so the rating does not imply the time that structural integrity is maintained [10:53][22:00].
### Spec References
ASTM E119 fire qualification test, cited repeatedly as the prescriptive standard fire test with no decay phase [08:49][10:22][29:10]. AISC 360 ("the steel Bible"), which allows two analysis types: qualification testing (prescriptive) and design by analysis (structural fire engineering); load combinations including fire are in AISC 360, and fire need not be combined with wind or seismic [15:34][58:56]. ASCE 7-16 fire appendix (non-mandatory, the "what" document about five pages, with a companion "how" document then in development) [16:06]. NFPA 5000 and IBC (2012 referenced) permit alternatives that meet the intent of the code [14:33][15:04]. NFPA 502 for bridges, requiring critical structural members be protected from collision and high-temperature exposure [42:01]. Bolt grades cited: grade 8.8 equated to A490 and grade 5.6 equated to A325 [33:47]. The AISC effective-width B-sub-e procedure for post-buckling capacity of simply supported plates is referenced as the method modified into an equivalent-length approach for shear [46:08].
### Technical Details
Perimeter columns in a fire develop unexpected behavior because a heated beam expands, pushes against adjacent structure, and induces axial load in the beam plus a moment in the column that was designed for axial load only [18:43]. Thermal gradients through the section matter: the web heats faster than flanges, yield strength drops non-uniformly with depth (around 6 to 7 percent of Fy at roughly 500 C), and the stiffer cooler side shifts the effective centroid, producing a moment reversal that can dominate the column response and push demand outside the assumed plastic interaction envelope [22:27][26:03][26:35]. In the One Meridian Plaza study a W14x314 perimeter column failed at about 41 to 42 minutes by combined axial-load-and-moment full plastic yielding, not lateral buckling, once fire protection was removed for study [20:20][25:32]. Connections fail during the cooling/decay phase, not the heating peak, because the beam buckles its web then flange, hangs in catenary, then contracts on cooling and pulls significant tension into the connection [29:40][30:43]. For bridge girders with slender webs, web shear buckling is the critical limit state, addressed with post-buckling tension-field theory and an equivalent-length column along the plate diagonal calibrated against 60 finite-element models and 84 physical tests [42:33][47:09][49:12].
### Fabrication Notes
The talk is analysis-focused, but connection detailing choices that fabrication controls drive fire performance. Adding a 4 mm web reinforcing plate so the 6 mm web plus plate matches the 10 mm shear-tab thickness eliminated failure across the full heating and cooling cycle, while conversely thinning the shear tab from 10 mm to 6 mm to match the web added roughly 50 minutes of endurance by making the connection more ductile and able to "breathe" with thermal movement [34:49][35:20][35:52]. Increasing the gap between beam end and supporting girder let all four bolts share load more uniformly and extended endurance from 123 to 165 minutes [36:53][37:23]. The lesson for detailing is that connections need flexibility to accommodate expansion and contraction rather than maximum rigidity.
### Erection Notes
No erection sequencing, crane, rigging, or field-bracing content is covered; this webinar is about design analysis and connection behavior under fire, not field procedures.
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The recurring conceptual error is assuming the ASTM E119 rating equals structural integrity time, and assuming an average uniform section temperature when thermal gradients can either overstate or understate capacity in different regions of the section [10:53][24:30]. Garlock notes B. Hopold's analysis of 60-foot university columns found the demand fell in the "red zone" where ignoring thermal gradients would have overestimated capacity [28:07]. In the Cardington full-scale fire tests, lower-grade bolts unexpectedly sheared off, an effect missed in the first test series because no sensors were placed on the connection [59:56][60:27]. Standard oversized and slotted holes were found too small to accommodate the thermal movement, so they made little difference [62:32].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Structural fire engineering is presented as a direct cost-saving alternative to blanket prescriptive fire protection. The 60-foot columns example showed two-hour protection costing about $20,000 per column, and the analysis eliminated protection on many columns for significant savings [28:07]. The Five Broadgate project in the UK used tensile membrane action, leaving interior filler beams unprotected, with a study showing about 40 percent fire-protection cost savings [38:25][39:57]. The Hotel Arts Barcelona exoskeleton needed no fire protection per its analysis [40:29]. The trade-off is engineering effort: this is a specialized analysis (SAFIR software, Abaqus with contact and nonlinear material) more common in the UK than the US, with only a few US firms doing it [56:50][57:54][61:29].
