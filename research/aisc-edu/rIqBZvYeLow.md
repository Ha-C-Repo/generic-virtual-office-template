## Practical Implementation of Composite Floor Designs
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rIqBZvYeLow | Duration: 1:30:30 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
William Jacobs of Stanley G. Lindsay and Associates covers field problems in composite steel floor design that the codes do not address cleanly, organized in three parts: conduit and penetrations and openings through the slab and their effect on composite beam effective width [01:34], strengthening in-place composite beams including load history and welding under load [33:37], and ten best-practice tips [67:54]. The unifying technical claim is that because composite W-shapes are braced by the slab and are compact under Chapter I, they develop full plastic strength, so load history and mixed steel grades do not reduce flexural strength as long as plasticity is achievable [43:55]. This is a practical estimating and field-coordination talk, not a connection-design lecture.

### Spec References
AISC Chapter I composite provisions and the compactness limit web slenderness less than or equal to 3.76 times sqrt(E/Fy) [51:45]; the alternate plastic-web limit cited as "B4.1b case 16" and the column lower bound H/tw less than 1.49 [53:18]; cover-plate provisions in "F13.3" for shear flow, extension, and attachment [56:55]; stitch-weld spacing s-max of 24 times the thinner part joined and not more than 12 inches, with each weld at least 4 times the leg size and not less than 1.5 inches [60:33]; the de-facto 25 percent minimum composite from the manual charts and commentary, with the 2016 spec adding a stud-ductility requirement and 50 percent commentary recommendation [61:03]; the 2010 spec stud-projection limit of not more than 1.5 inches above the deck and a half-inch minimum cover over anchors [80:13]. Non-AISC references named: retired ASCE 3-91 composite-slab standard, SDI Floor Deck Design Manual first edition 2014, ACI 318 (cited as 318-11), OSHA 29 CFR 1926.754(e)(2), AISC Design Guide 3 (ponding 10 percent slab-weight increase) [79:12], AISC Design Guide 18 (galvanized welding) [81:46], the AISC web-openings Design Guide (number not stated), SCI Technical Note 350, ASCC Position Statement 23, SDI 2012 position statement on sloped-roof fastening, and AWS limiting puddle welding on roofs steeper than 15 degrees [83:17].

### Technical Details
Conduit baseline from the retired ASCE 3 and unchanged in SDI 2014: 1 foot 6 inch minimum spacing, max conduit diameter of 1 inch or cover-over-3, no crossing, three-quarter-inch minimum cover below conduit [04:41]. Denser conduit requires reinforcing the slab to ACI 318, which allows down to three diameters spacing [06:46]. For massed pipe per SCI TN350: keep conduit out of 8 inches each side of the beam centerline, run transverse pipe in the outer thirds, discount studs within roughly 1.75 stud heights (about 8 inches) and remove the displaced strip from the compression block [08:51]. Effective-width manipulation near openings uses Wiseman's 1996 method springing a 14-degree alpha angle past openings, with eccentric compression forces resisted by added slab tension reinforcing [22:48]. The sectional approach cuts at each stud and sums the smaller QN side to build a stepped capacity diagram for openings, stepped-back slabs, or uneven stud layouts [27:25]. The specification requires enough anchors between a concentrated load and the nearest zero-moment point to develop the moment at that load [33:06].

### Fabrication Notes
Stud burn-off is the key shop number: studs shorten about 3/16 inch welded to bare steel and up to 3/8 inch welded through deck, so the installed length, not the ordered length, should be on the drawings to stay under the 1.5-inch projection limit [80:13]. Galvanized beams should not have studs welded directly because of fumes and weld inclusions; Design Guide 18 says remove or mask the coating, and the practical galvanizers' method is a two-stud shot, the first burning off coating and bin-tested, the second placed in the same spot if the first fails [81:15]. Reinforcing options run from a welded bottom plate (down-hand weld, clampable, modest gain) to a WT (overhead weld, hard to hold) to a recommended W8 or W10 section that clamps and welds down-hand while still placing steel far from the neutral axis [37:11]. Welding under load reduces section properties: stick welding renders about 1 inch of section ineffective, so the reduced mono-symmetric section must carry the load present at the time of welding or the member must be shored [55:22].

### Erection Notes
Deck openings should not be field-cut until the slab reaches 75 percent of design strength, because the deck carries the wet concrete weight and was not designed with a hole [13:29]. OSHA 1926.754 requires floor and roof openings be decked over until immediately before use [13:29]. Skewed or mis-broken deck causes field crews to pile studs into pods or shoot them through high-flute holes, which is wrong; the fix is a deck closure piece with studs welded uniformly, and Jacobs repeatedly recommends just designing such beams non-composite [72:31]. Camber only 80 percent of the pre-composite dead load, since shear-tab supports have some rotational restraint and full camber over-lifts the beam and pushes studs out of the slab top [82:16]. Coring through an existing slab to add shear transfer is messy, needs water lubricant, creates a stud-weld fire hazard, and is best reserved for making non-composite beams composite [40:48].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The opening example is a massive conduit bundle with studs poking between it that destroyed composite action and damaged the slab, fixed only with filler beams, slab reinforcing, and a girder cover plate [03:06]. Another job had a slab cored 16 times in a row mid-span, effectively cantilevering the slab and forcing a post-installed steel frame [11:56]. Contractors welding all studs into one half of the span (13 placed where 10 were called, but useless) [73:31], notching the horizontal leg of an edge bent plate at shear connectors and then cutting the deck to fit [75:34], and violating the 1-inch minimum lateral clear distance to a stud by shoving deck over [75:03] are all named field failures. Studs ruptured and "unzipped" when composite percentage was too low, dropping capacity back to non-composite, which is why 50 percent minimum composite is recommended [61:38].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Strengthening is described as generally expensive, so the first move is to change the load path (relocate the load, add a post) before adding steel [35:09]. Concrete ponding from member deflection drives overruns; Design Guide 3 advises increasing slab weight 10 percent, mostly from beam and girder deflection rather than deck, and Jacobs adds a general note warning contractors to carry the overrun in base bids so there is no later add [78:41]. Air entrainment of 4 to 7 percent required by some UL ratings draws contractor pushback and can complicate finishing, a coordination cost to anticipate [68:25]. Specifying deck-fastener pins (mechanical anchors) in lieu of puddle welds is favored on large or high-sloped jobs for quality control and speed [83:17]. Edge conditions need wider flanges (a 5-inch minimum, so a W14x22 or W12x26 rule of thumb) to fit studs and bent plates, a member-selection cost driver [75:34].
