## Non-Destructive Examination and Special Inspection of Seismic Welding

### Core Concept
Mike Mays, a welding engineer and SNT level 3 with 30-plus years in materials testing, walks through visual inspection, NDE method selection, and the special-inspection regime for welded structural steel, then narrows to the post-Northridge seismic welding rules [00:00][01:35]. The central message is that quality control is the fabricator's and erector's job while quality assurance is the owner's special inspector, and the two roles must agree the NDE scope before work starts rather than after welds are made [02:05][10:59][39:02]. He repeatedly stresses that NDE has no inherent code requirement in the IBC or the welding code; it applies only when the contract documents or the seismic provisions call for it [07:50][08:20].

### Spec References
The speaker cites the AWS D1.1 welding code as assigning QC to the fabricator and erector [02:05], and AWS D1.8 as the seismic supplement to D1.1 that governs seismic-critical welds, including Annex (referred to as the D1.8 annex) requirements that UT technicians be tested on real flaw samples [32:46][50:00][23:53]. For seismic design and inspection he points to the AISC seismic provisions and specifically "appendix Q" as the quality-assurance-plan source [32:46][33:18][38:32]. He references the international building code requiring 100 percent visual inspection in shop and field with continuous versus periodic inspection categories [05:43][06:14], and notes the code provision that NDE cannot be imposed after the fact unless there is intent to defraud or gross non-performance [09:23]. He mentions the older FEMA "yellow books" as superseded by the AISC seismic provisions for inspection [32:46]. Counting distinct citations: AWS D1.1, AWS D1.8 (seismic supplement + flaw-sample annex), AISC seismic provisions Appendix Q, IBC visual/continuous-periodic, IBC no-after-the-fact-NDE provision, FEMA yellow books.

### Technical Details
Four NDE methods apply to structural welds: radiography (good for bridge girders, poor for building T-joints, continuity plates, and stiffeners where film and source cannot be oriented), ultrasonic testing (the workhorse for looking inside CJP welds via sound-path trigonometry), magnetic particle testing (surface and near-surface, with dry, wet, and fluorescent sensitivity levels), and dye penetrant (only for ground-smooth surfaces such as cope access holes) [11:29][12:00][14:03][22:19][33:49]. He warns against re-checking a UT or RT finding with the other method because the two see different things [12:31]. Backing bars complicate UT because the unfused backing-bar corner mimics lack-of-fusion, and the beam web-to-flange curve creates a blind "gimme zone" at the lower flange, which is why backing bars come off seismic lower-flange joints [19:13][20:46][21:16]. UT is not appropriate for partial-penetration welds or fillet welds under 5/16 inch; use visual or MT instead [21:46][22:19]. Phased array is emerging for flaw location and sizing via tip diffraction, but the AWS code still compares to side-drilled-hole references and has no adopted criteria for the richer data [25:31][41:50].

### Fabrication Notes
The fabricator should run a well-defined QC program with knowledgeable people, because the speaker observes that those shops run far smoother for all parties [02:35]. Key seismic-fab requirements named: weld access holes (beam cope holes) ground smooth and inspected by MT or PT, since dye penetrant works there [33:49]; the K-area between web and flange flagged as a crack-prone zone of poor material properties that can crack even without welds [36:59][37:31]; and backing-bar handling where the steel backing typically comes off the bottom flange, the root is back-gouged, MT'd, and a reinforcing fillet added, while the top-flange backing is left and seal-welded to the column [33:49][34:52]. He notes that removing steel backing is time-consuming and messy, so some shops use ceramic or copper backing, each of which forces a fit-up root-gap qualification and a separate welder qualification, and melting copper ruins the bar and requires back-gouging the root pass [34:52][35:26]. Mocking up difficult tubular and skewed joints with deliberate flaws lets both QC and QA UT technicians prove they can find them [30:39][31:09].

### Erection Notes
The protected zone is one of the biggest field problems, and it varies by connection type [35:57]. He gives concrete violations the inspector caught and had to have cut off and redone: a curtain-wall subcontractor welding angles across RBS dog-bone flanges, light-gauge framing pins shot into the protected zone, and safety-cable holes drilled into protected flange areas, with the fix being to relocate the holes outside the zone while still serving the erector [35:57][36:28][36:59]. For lower-flange seismic welds the inspector confirms the welder pushes the electrode through the access hole and moves side to side, which takes longer; "angel wing" weld tabs give the welder a place to stand and access both sides [37:31][38:02]. He recommends raising the inspection plan and protected-zone constraints at the pre-fabrication and pre-erection meeting [46:38].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The worst case named is work accepted in the shop and then rejected in the field by a different inspector, which he calls a horrible situation [07:19]. Other failures: arbitrary inspector discretion in S-notes ("do whatever he wants") leading to disputes [10:27][11:29]; re-testing a flaw with a second method and getting conflicting calls [12:31]; misreading a backing-bar corner as lack of fusion [19:45]; the lower-flange "gimme zone" blind spot tied to many Northridge fractures [21:16]; slag left where a welder ran one side then the other without proper access-hole technique [37:31]; UT technicians who never find flaws being a red flag rather than good news [23:23]; and the industry-wide weakness that the post-Northridge round-robin showed most technicians scored poorly on real flaws, which drove the real-sample testing requirement now in D1.8 [26:33][27:04][23:53].

### Bid / Cost Implications
NDE scope drives cost and must be priced from the contract, not assumed; the speaker warns the big surprise is welding without expecting NDE and then having someone X-ray the work later, with the code forbidding owners from imposing UT or RT not in the documents [08:51][09:23][09:56]. Continuous inspection for seismic and CJP welds means the inspector stands at the weld, which is a real burden when the shop runs double shifts [06:14][06:47]. An approved-fabricator or AISC-certified-fabricator status can drop shop welds from continuous to periodic inspection, saving owner money, though seismic provisions still mandate continuous inspection and the fabricator must still negotiate who performs the NDT since AISC-certified shops generally do not run their own [06:47][27:34][29:06][47:11]. Some engineers allow a UT reduction from 100 percent to 25 percent on welders with a low reject history (roughly 40 welds under about 5 percent rejects), which is a documented schedule and cost lever [48:12]. Repairing a flaw that fails the AWS quality code is usually cheaper than the fracture-mechanics flaw-sizing analysis route, which only about five technicians nationwide can do accurately [25:31].
