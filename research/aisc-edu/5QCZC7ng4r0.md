### Core Concept
This is Session 1 of AISC Night School 17, presented by Bo Dowswell of ARC International, the author of AISC Design Guide 33 (Curved Member Design) [00:00, 01:05]. The session is a general orientation to curved steel members. Dowswell splits curved members into vertically curved and horizontally curved, where the controlling distinction is structural behavior, not just the geometric plane of curvature [02:06, 03:09]. Curving steel is a specialty operation performed by a bender roller company that normally works as a subcontractor to the steel fabricator, because a typical fab shop does not own the bending equipment [06:56, 07:27]. The session walks the Design Guide 33 table of contents, curve geometries, bending processes, and the curving mechanics (strain ratio alpha) that govern fracture and distortion risk [06:26].

### Technical Details
A standard bend is single-radius curvature about a principal or geometric axis; strong-axis bending is called hard-way and weak-axis is easy-way by bender rollers [18:10, 18:40]. Specialty bends covered: off-axis (conical rolling), compound (two arcs, same direction, optional straight tangent segment), reverse compound (S-curve), multi-axis (multi-plane, any 3D shape from a CAD file), variable-radius (parabolic, elliptical), and spiral or helical (sloped/pitched rolling) [22:53 through 31:06]. The governing mechanics are the maximum bending strain epsilon = y-o / R, simplified to D / 2R for symmetric shapes, expressed through the strain ratio alpha (max strain divided by yield strain F-y/E) [56:51, 57:21, 57:51]. Alpha at 12 is the threshold to contact a bender roller, with roughly 17 still safe; 3 percent strain is a practical upper bound and strain-hardening for 50 ksi steel begins near alpha 8.7 [58:51, 60:24, 61:25]. Detailed radius dimensions go to the inside of the flange for hard-way wide flange, to the centroid for easy-way, and to the HSS center or inside compression face per shape [19:41, 20:12].

### Fabrication Notes
Two temperature classes: cold bending (shop temperature) and hot bending (heat lowers yield, easier to bend) [32:37, 33:08]. Three structural processes: pyramid roll bending (three rolls, most common, cold, multiple passes with rolls drawn closer each pass, supplementary rolls tension the tension flange to control web distortion), incremental step bending (proprietary cantilever pull-and-feed, cold), and induction bending (the only common hot method, a narrow heated ring around the perimeter with immediate water cooling, pivoted on a radius arm) [33:42 through 46:21]. Contour rolls, grooved/special rolls, and internal mandrels brace slender elements against cross-sectional distortion; tooling cost is justified by quantity, not a single member [37:27, 38:27, 71:16]. Tension-flange bolt holes should be punched before bending only for large-radius bends or when the F-u A-fn check passes; small-radius bends should be drilled after bending, which is more expensive because the beam line cannot be used [63:29, 64:01, 65:04]. Holes, cuts, copes, and pre-bend welded fittings all reduce ductility; never cut a diamond-shaped hole before bending [62:29, 63:09].

### Erection Notes
Erection-specific procedure is light in this session. The main field-relevant point is shipping: a curved member held to a tight radius can relax from truck vibration, similar to documented camber loss, although the bender-roller committee did not agree it is real and had no proof [86:30, 87:02, 87:32]. Bender rollers add bracing on long slender curved members for the truck ride to prevent distortion or loss of radius in transit [88:33]. Induction-bent members may hold radius better in shipping because their residual stresses are lower, though this is unproven [88:02].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Distortion is any deviation from the original cross-section and occurs to some degree on every bend because initial mill imperfections are amplified [66:08, 66:39]. Named failure modes: flange or web local buckling under flexural compression, shear buckling between pyramid rolls, contact-force web crippling under the roll, and flange bending when supplementary-roll force is set too high [66:39, 67:11, 67:41, 68:12]. For square and rectangular HSS, distortion is quantified by rho-W (web bow B1/B) and rho-F (compression-flange inward bow E/D); round HSS uses ovalization rho [72:18, 72:49, 74:53]. A real 1988 case showed an HSS spiral stair stringer scrapped because only two internal support HSS were tacked in instead of three, leaving visible sidewall bowing [73:50, 74:21]. Fracture risk rises with alpha and with pre-bend holes, cuts, copes, and welds [61:25, 62:29].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Curving is a subcontracted line item to a bender roller, so the estimate must carry that scope rather than absorb it as in-house fab [07:27]. Cold bending is cheaper than induction (hot) bending; induction costs more but achieves tight radii with low distortion [47:27]. Contour and special rolls or mandrels carry tooling cost that only amortizes over quantity, so one-off curved members are disproportionately expensive [38:27, 76:08]. Drilling holes after bending breaks the beam-line flow and adds cost on small-radius work [64:01]. Bender-roller capability varies widely by shop (heavy strong-axis, spirals, induction), so the bender roller should be engaged in the conceptual stage when a tight radius is involved to avoid late rework [49:33, 50:04]. Galvanizing after bending can shift the radius from residual-stress release and force a re-roll that damages the coating, a schedule risk to flag [89:33, 90:05].
