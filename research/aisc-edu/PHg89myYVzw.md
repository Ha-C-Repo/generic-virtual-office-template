## Design with Damping
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PHg89myYVzw | Duration: 1:06:12 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Robert J. McNamara of McNamara Salvia argues that structural damping is poorly understood and should be replaced in practice by deliberately designed dissipative systems [02:06]. He frames damping as the C velocity term in the dynamic equilibrium equation, a mathematical convenience that does not exist in nature, and notes the seismic world's universal 5 percent damping assumption has no real correlation or origin [05:40][06:11]. His central thesis is that added energy-dissipation devices should be treated as corrective forces with a defined load path back to the structural frame, not as a hidden percent-of-critical number you guess at [17:58][18:30].

### Technical Details
He categorizes dissipative systems by mechanism: friction dampers and viscoelastic dampers (displacement dependent), yielding dampers (yield real material, displacement dependent), viscous dampers/shock absorbers (velocity dependent, peak force when displacement is zero), and inertia/tuned mass dampers including sloshing dampers [11:49][12:51][14:22]. Inherent steel damping is cited as roughly 0.5 to 2 percent of critical, dropping near 1 percent on very tall towers, and he warns that a rigid-diaphragm assumption or a soft load path negates the damper entirely [10:17][24:37][19:31]. Project examples include a viscous-damper toggle-brace system (lambda-shaped) that amplifies small inter-story drift roughly three times so the shock absorber sees larger velocity, with force scaling as the square of the amplification [40:30][42:02][55:26].

The closest field point is placement of dampers: because a damper carries no gravity and no overturning to the base, it can be located arbitrarily in plan and elevation, for example every other floor in the Boston tower, as long as the corrective-force load path reaches the lateral system [40:30][41:00][21:03].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The recurring failure he names is a soft or neglected load path: a well-designed damper produced essentially no response reduction under simulated seismic time history because of stiffness loss in the connecting parts, and rigid-diaphragm analysis overestimates damper effectiveness [20:01][19:31]. He also flags that serviceability dampers in stair towers are not fireproofed and require no inspection, but seismic damage-control dampers that cut design base shear (for example 5,000 kips to 2,500) demand inspection and raise unresolved fireproofing questions [63:52][64:22].
### Bid / Cost Implications
He is blunt that this work is not covered by standard structural fees: the special-systems fee can equal the entire structural fee on a tall building, and seismic time-history analysis with site filtering on deep soil (200 ft of Boston blue clay) consumes large effort [54:24][55:56]. A concrete cost data point is the Boise airport terminal, where roughly 12 installed Taylor Devices dampers provided seismic damage control for about 100,000 dollars and cut expected displacement near 50 percent [52:51][47:12]. He also shows that the assumed damping value drives wind-tunnel force reductions of 30 to 40 percent, a direct cost lever on base shear [23:35][25:08].
