## Design of Facade Attachments, Session R1: Facade Fundamentals

### Core Concept
This is AISC Night School 17, Session 5, the first session on facade attachments to steel-framed buildings, presented by Alex Zimmer of Simpson Gumpertz and Heger, a co-developer of AISC Design Guide 22 [00:03][01:04]. The core teaching is how the steel frame supports a non-bearing curtain wall facade through attachments that must carry gravity, wind, seismic, and thermal loads while accommodating differential movement between the frame and the cladding [10:44][13:17]. The central problem stated is that poorly coordinated facade attachments drive excess RFIs, shop-drawing delays, out-of-tolerance erection, and envelope failures [03:36]. The session covers facade performance fundamentals, design criteria, roles and responsibilities, thermal bridging, and accommodating construction tolerances and clearances [04:39].

### Technical Details
Facade loads to design for are dead load (the predominant gravity load, mostly no live load except for projections that can carry maintenance access), seismic perpendicular and parallel to the wall, wind primarily perpendicular plus joint racking, and thermal expansion-contraction [16:23]. The designer must locate the gravity hinge between panel and frame, since hinge position drives spandrel-beam moment and slab-edge demand significantly [17:59][18:30]. Stiffness governs by rule of thumb (L/360, brick L/600 to L/720, 0.31 in per ACI 530, 3/4 in per AAMA), but sealant compressibility often controls: a 3/4 in joint compressed to 50 percent leaving 1/8 in for tolerance and thermal limits a 30 ft spandrel to about 1/4 in, roughly L/1440 [28:18][29:50]. Interstory drift is handled at H/400 to H/500 for wind, with ASCE 7-16 requiring connections to accommodate at least 1/2 in of interstory movement [30:21][30:52]. Thermal-break design must account for bolt flexure, shear deformation, shim compression, and oversized holes that defeat slip-critical clamping; the Pankow study recommends treating bolts as single-curvature cantilevers and limiting FRP compressive stress to about 30 to 35 percent of ultimate to prevent creep [55:43][58:17][59:48].
### Fabrication Notes
The speaker repeatedly stresses that the steel fabricator and erector cannot reasonably beat AISC Code of Standard Practice tolerances, and specifying tighter frame tolerances should be exceptional and only after consulting local fabricators and erectors [62:50][63:20]. Thermal-break assemblies are built up in the shop using carbon steel plate (left unprotected behind the moisture barrier), galvanized plate outboard of the barrier, thermal isolation pads aligned to the insulation plane, isolation bushings or washers, and ASTM F593 stainless bolts; carbon-steel studs can be shop-welded to a hanger angle to pass through the pad [52:10][53:11][53:42]. Hot-dip galvanized fasteners are cheaper and more available, and field welding of galvanized connections uses common procedures but requires removing the galvanizing locally and touching up the weld-affected area after [34:56][35:27]. Material production tolerances such as beam camber sweep and panel thickness stack with fabrication and erection tolerances into accumulated tolerance that the design must absorb [63:51][64:21].
### Erection Notes
Erection tolerances include column plumbness (Code of Standard Practice allows 2 in inward or 1 in outward at the 10th story in the worked example), spandrel-beam sweep of 1/2 in, panel plan location plus or minus 1/2 in per PCI, and panel bow up to L/360 or 1.33 in [63:51][65:22]. These aggregate so a column gap could open 2.5 in or close 1.5 in, and a mid-span gap could open 4.33 in or close 3.33 in; a square-root-sum-of-squares approach gives a more realistic range of roughly minus 1.8 to plus 2.5 in [66:54][68:56]. Out-of-tolerance erection is corrected through slotted-hole adjustability, and when that is exceeded, hanger angles are unbolted and field welded or a longer shelf-angle leg is installed [86:03][86:34]. A cautionary case showed an aluminum-glass curtain wall erected before the steel sunshade because the building had to be enclosed early, forcing heavy field welding next to installed glass with large deviations [73:33][74:35]. Attachments are also at the confluence of structural erectors, waterproofers, carpenters, and masons, so barrier damage during installation is a recurring erection risk [36:28][36:58].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named failures include the Christchurch 2001 earthquake where unanchored brick veneer peeled off the entire frame, and a precast panel that fell when a slotted-insert bolt at a building expansion joint was torqued down too far and broke under cyclic movement [20:33][32:23][33:24]. Durability failures center on concealed attachments that corrode unseen: corrugated brick ties accumulated water and rusted out with no inspection access until brick fell, and thin or unprotected steel fasteners penetrating the water barrier are flagged as high risk [33:56][34:26]. Other problems are anchors that block wall drainage, anchors too flexible to prevent differential movement that then tears waterproofing and air barriers, and details that are simply not constructible in the field [35:58][36:28]. Restraint of thermal and structural movement is called out as a condition to avoid entirely because the resulting forces are very hard to predict [32:23].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Wind tunnel testing on larger buildings frequently shows design pressures below ASCE 7 values, which can reduce both cladding cost and the main wind force resisting system forces [19:01]. Thermal-break connections are expensive and the speaker repeatedly weighs whether the cost is justified, recommending them on continuous facade lines with real thermal impact but not on one-off back-of-house connections [37:28][61:18]. Stainless steel fasteners cost more than hot-dip galvanized but last longer on buildings unlikely to be reskinned, and the Northeastern study finding that thermally improved bushings add little benefit lets the designer drop bushings and just use stainless bolts [34:56][60:18]. Tighter-than-code frame tolerances raise fabrication and erection cost and should be avoided, and the team should set the adjustability budget up front rather than absorbing rework later [62:50][70:30].
