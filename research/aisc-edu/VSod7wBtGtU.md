## Performance and Behavior of Gusset Plate Connections
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VSod7wBtGtU | Duration: 1:26:59 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
Two paired papers from an AISC seismic session (session E3) on the inelastic behavior of brace-frame gusset plate connections. Charles Roeder (University of Washington, with Dawn Lehman) presents test-based design recommendations for corner and midspan gussets in special concentrically braced frames [01:02], and Larry Muir (with Bill Thornton) presents the companion work on vertical bracing connections under high seismic drift [57:45]. The unifying finding is counterintuitive: a gusset that is stronger and stiffer than needed performs worse, because the brace must yield and buckle while the connection accommodates large end rotation [30:36]. The design goal is to keep the gusset stiff and strong enough to carry the brace force and no more, so inelastic action stays in the brace and yielding is allowed across the gusset [44:58].

### Technical Details
Roeder distinguishes corner gussets (supported on two edges, stable but force a large thick plate under the 2tp rule) from midspan gussets (one edge, less stable, rotate more easily) [16:21]. His recommendations: an elliptical clearance of 8tp for corner gussets that cuts plate size by 30 to 40 percent and lets the plate get thinner [29:02]; a 6tp horizontal clearance band for midspan gussets [18:24]; size connection welds to the plastic capacity of the gusset, not the brace force, because gusset bending adds demand beyond the brace tension [28:32]; use effective length coefficient K = 0.65 for corner gussets and K = 1.2 for midspan, with the buckling length taken as the mean of three Whitmore-related dimensions [51:37]. Recommended beta factors replace resistance factors since design forces run near 100 percent above factored loads: 0.9 for net-section yielding and Whitmore tensile yielding, 0.85 for block shear, weld beta near the usual factor [49:35]. Across 27 SCBF tests at UW plus multistory frames in Taiwan, well-designed specimens reached 16 to 20 inches out-of-plane brace buckling before fracture and roughly 2 to 3 percent story drift [25:29, 40:54]. Edge buckling proved an unreliable predictor and is effectively dismissed [20:55]. Muir's papers address distortional (pinching) forces when the beam-to-column angle closes at 2.5 percent drift, with a plate buckling check (three sides simply supported, one free) and fuse, pin, or moved-work-point details to drive distortional force toward zero [60:50, 73:38].

### Fabrication Notes
Welds joining the brace tube to the gusset and the gusset to the beam and column should be CJP of matching metal or fillet welds sized just larger than the steel thickness, set to the gusset plastic capacity [28:32]. HSS braces are slit to slide over the gusset, creating a net-section concern at the slot tip [09:11]. The elliptical clearance both reduces plate size and reduces yielding damage to the beam and column [29:33]. Tapered gussets perform well and improve constructability, though they can need a slightly thicker plate and show more yielding in the welds [46:00]. Muir notes that pinned or hinged fuse details are not easy to fabricate, and that end plates or angles forming a limited hinge are more palatable to a fabricator but cannot transfer large axial force [66:57]. A reduced-section fuse, like an RBS cut, can carry the axial force and still limit distortional force [69:29].

### Erection Notes
Specimens are tested with out-of-plane restraint to simulate the lateral support a slab gives in the real frame [24:59]. For midspan gussets, lateral support is often attached only to the beam web rather than the bottom flange, so stability leans on web stiffness [18:24]. Muir's moved-work-point detail uses long slots, an increased setback from beam end to column face, snug-tight bolts, and a single standard hole as a pivot so 2.5 percent drift occurs without the gusset binding on the column [70:31]. A concern raised was bracing the column through that single bolt; the answer was that added standard holes above and below give three bolts, the column bracing force is only about 1 percent of column load, and inch-and-an-eighth A490 bolts carry ample capacity [71:33]. Bolts are kept snug-tight to allow movement, though pretensioned bolts on clean mill scale would still slot before the gusset buckled [72:34].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The first UW test, built to conventional practice with the 2tp clearance, fractured its welds early because they were sized to brace tension while ignoring gusset bending [27:32]. Making the gusset thicker, the beam heavier, or the connection stiffer consistently reduced ductility by forcing the brace into reverse curvature and shortening the plastic hinge [30:36]. A buckling-restrained brace specimen rotated and effectively buckled because beam-flange fracture let the conservatively designed gusset lose stability [15:19]. Demand-critical welds showed stable crack growth several inches long, initiating around 1.5 to 2 percent drift, treated as expected rather than a defect if cracks stay limited [27:02, 85:26]. Net-section fracture occurred in only 2 of 27 tests, tied to near-fault tension pulls or overly stiff connections [36:14]. A bolted knife-plate-and-tee splice gave poor ductility because bolted connections stack limit states in series [34:08]. Muir's main failure mode is gusset buckling while the brace is in tension, from the closing beam-column angle, which then fractures welds (the Lopez test) [61:54].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Roeder's elliptical-clearance recommendation reduces gusset plate size by 30 to 40 percent and allows a thinner plate, which directly cuts plate weight and welding on every braced bay [29:02]. His pushback on overly conservative net-section reinforcement matters to cost: a strict calculation demanded about 4 square inches of added steel, yet specimens with an inch to an inch and a half, or none at all, did not fracture [35:43]. The beta-factor approach (0.9 net section, 0.85 block shear) yields smaller, more economical reinforcement [50:06]. On test economics, Roeder cites roughly 8 to 9 thousand dollars per single connection test and four to five times that per multistory frame, with about two tons of steel per specimen [55:43, 23:58]. Muir's CJP-everything detail with stiffeners is described as very simple to design but very expensive [65:56], while the fuse and moved-work-point details trade fabrication effort for distortional-force control.
