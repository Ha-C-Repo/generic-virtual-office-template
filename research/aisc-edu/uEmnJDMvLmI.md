## Challenging Bridge Projects in Steel

### Core Concept
This session pairs two AECOM bridge case studies on highway plate-girder steel, both centered on the idea that erection and pour sequence change the internal forces in a continuous girder grid. The first talk (Shane Eavis) covers the 11th Street river bridge in Washington DC, where five of seven girders were already fabricated and erected when the owner asked to resequence construction, forcing a three-stage deck pour with a full-length longitudinal closure pour [05:40][11:20]. The controlling lesson stated repeatedly is that girders and cross frames connected together share load, so when you build a bridge in a different order it redistributes moments and shear through the grid [14:25][35:24]. The second talk (Chris Stein) covers the I-55 and Lake Shore Drive interchange in Chicago, focused on tight-radius curved girders, joint elimination, and coating selection.

### Technical Details
Member and system types: five-span continuous plate-girder bridge, seven I-girder lines about ten feet on center, with inverted-K cross frames with a top chord spaced 17 to 20 feet, the top and bottom chords forming what the speaker calls a "pseudo box" for torsional stability of the grid [04:10][38:35][39:08]. Haunched girders were used over the piers for the DC bridge [05:10]. The lean-on bracing fix added bottom-flange bracing on girder six kicked back to girders five and seven to recover lateral-torsional buckling capacity, relying on the fact that adjacent girders are not loaded to maximum at the same time [18:36][19:06]. The Chicago job ran very tight radii down to a 469-foot innermost girder, 15-foot cross-frame spacing, flanges up to 24 inches wide, and only five plate sizes across 12 units and 40 spans [59:02][58:31]; cross frames were analyzed with MDX in plate-and-eccentric-beam mode, which the speaker says yields cross-frame forces about half those of a standard 2D grid [57:26].

### Fabrication Notes
On the DC resequencing, no changes to the as-fabricated steel were permitted, so camber profiles were already cut into the webs and cross-frame drops already fixed by geometry [07:14][09:18]. Field measurement of actual weld and gusset overlap mattered because shop drawings show a typical weld-all-around length but not how far an angle laps onto a gusset, or how the fabricator blocks out cross frames to reuse a common gusset size; a blank schematic was sent to the field to record real weld lengths [25:44][26:14]. Where a cross frame could not be made to fit, the fix was to remove the members from the gussets, install a blank gusset on the connector plate, then match-mark and field-drill the gusset to the connection plate [23:09]. The fabricator had material certs on site, so actual yield strengths were used in the reanalysis rather than notional code values [25:13]. Coating: the Chicago girders were metallized at about $0.50 per pound because hot-dip galvanizing cannot be done on the curved girders; splice plates, cross frames, and bearings were hot-dip galvanized where possible [62:09][63:40].

### Erection Notes
The DC bridge had five of seven girder lines erected, two cantilevered field pieces left off, over water with the erection barge already demobilized [07:14][12:52]. To resequence, cross-frame bolts in the closure bay were removed on one side and loosened on the other so the cross frame could swing out and avoid a spatial conflict as girders three through seven deflected down under deck while girders one and two stayed up [12:52][13:24]. The grid was then tuned along a thousand-foot length using staged loads: partial sidewalk and barrier placement, plus a 15-ton jack force and 5-to-15-ton counterweights, the jack also providing a resistive couple to keep the curved girders from walking outward off the connection holes [16:00][17:32][29:47][30:18]. Overhang brackets had to be set precisely because one side moved while the other stayed fixed, with constant geometry checks coordinated with the contractor [31:19].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
No code-inspection rejections are named; the failures discussed are analysis and fit-up driven. The central error called out is assuming a bridge built out of sequence carries the same forces as one built monolithically, which overloaded the cross frames in the leading bay near the discontinuous girders [09:48][35:24]. Fit-up risk was the controlling issue: out of 46 cross frames in the closure bay, 34 came in under the 3/8-inch differential the contractor said full-size drift pins could pick up, and 12 exceeded it; of those 12, only three actually needed the blank-gusset field-drill fix [22:38][23:40]. The Chicago job documents the existing 1965 deck reaching full-depth failures from 50 years of salting at simple-span joints [45:31][46:31]. The speaker also corrects the misconception that A490 bolts cannot be field welded or always break, noting good field QC keeps yield strength in the required bracket [27:14][27:44].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The DC resequencing salvaged nearly all of about 1,600 tons of erected steel (close to 1,400 tons of I-girder plus 217 tons of cross-frame steel), costing only a couple of angles for lean-on bracing and a few gusset plates [36:25][36:55]. The DC project was a $260 million design-build, where doing more interchange movements within budget won the work [02:40]. Schedule risk dominated the Chicago job: third-party Metra catenary wires not being removed put the contractor three to five months behind [52:41]. Cost choices included metallizing at roughly $0.50 per pound versus paint and galvanizing, minimizing plate sizes to five, eliminating 45 joints and reducing spans from 85 to 40 for durability, and avoiding fracture-critical straddle bents by holding spans near 180 feet [56:51][63:10][71:55][48:34]. Contracts were deliberately split smaller to attract big contractors busy with tollway work, yet the outbound contract still drew high bids while the inbound matched the engineer's estimate [68:18][68:50].

### Your Company Application
The strongest transferable lesson for Ivan's takeoff and the ACP project controls is that erection and pour sequence change member forces, so a sequence change after steel is fabricated is not free; on a connected frame the cross frames and bracing redistribute load, and refit at the connection holes governs whether the change is cheap or costly. This is the same connection-fit-up and tolerance discipline Your Company applies to anchor rods, and it argues for capturing erection sequence in Tekla detailing and flagging any owner-requested resequence to the in-house PE rather than absorbing it as field labor. The fit-up data point (target 3/8-inch differential picked up by full-size drift pins, blank-gusset match-drill as the fallback) is a concrete field-bolting tolerance Mario's crew can use as a go/no-go before forcing a connection. The field-verify-the-weld-length point reinforces verify-do-not-generate: shop typical weld callouts understate real lap, which matters for both Mario's welding estimate and Ivan's tonnage. The A490 field-weld correction supports good shop and field QC over blanket prohibition.
