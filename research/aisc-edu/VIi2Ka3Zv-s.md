### Core Concept
This part covers composite and non-composite steel deck selection and then transitions into the role of floor and roof diaphragms in lateral load resistance. The central economic thesis is that labor cost now dominates material cost, so "least weight is least cost" is no longer true; in-place steel price is driven by manhours per ton and piece count, not pounds per square foot [00:32][01:33][02:05]. The deck discussion runs from depth and gauge economy through attachment for wind uplift, and the diaphragm discussion treats the floor as a deep horizontal beam spanning between the vertical lateral force resisting system [34:08][34:38].

### Spec References
The speaker cites the AISC specification limiting composite-action deck to a 3 inch maximum depth because deeper composite decks had not been tested [00:01]. He cites the Steel Deck Institute (SDI) composite deck design handbook 1997 and SDI minimum attachment of 18 inches on center in the field and 12 inches on center at perimeter and corners when rib spacing is less than or equal to 6 inches, otherwise at every rib [07:13][17:58]. He cites Factory Mutual roof live-load criteria of 200 lb per foot at midspan with a deflection limit of L/240, the FM uplift class system (I-90 means 90 psf uplift), Underwriter Laboratories span requirements, and the AISC Code of Standard Practice column out-of-plumb tolerance of L/500 [15:53][18:29][20:33][35:42]. Coating callouts G60 and G90 galvanizing and ASTM A992 / A36 material grades are named, as are coating designations G60 and G90 [10:14][29:58]. ### Technical Details
Composite deck comes in 1.5, 2, and 3 inch depths; cellular decks serve office under-floor access and Epic-type slotted decks allow hanging loads [00:01][04:06]. Non-composite decks split into narrow rib, intermediate rib, and wide rib (type A, type F, type B), with vented form deck required under lightweight insulating concrete so moisture can cure out [12:48][23:08]. The SDI procedure for a heavy concentrated load on a composite slab projects the B2 loading footprint through the topping to a B-sub-m effective width, then checks punching shear, one-way shear, and flexural capacity with added perpendicular reinforcing over the W distance [07:43][08:43]. Diaphragm theory treats the floor deck as the web of a deep beam and the spandrel or edge angle as the chord, transferring in-plane shear to brace frames, moment frames, or shear walls [34:38][35:09].

### Fabrication Notes
The shop and material economy points dominate over fab tolerances here. The speaker observes that 18 gauge is the practical economic floor for deck thickness; 19 and 20 gauge price well, but 16 gauge jumps sharply in cost, so heavier deck rarely pencils out even when it could span farther [05:08][05:38]. Coating selection is a fabrication-spec decision: G90 for all roofs because the deck serves as positive reinforcement and must resist corrosion, G60 or a phosphatized painted finish acceptable on floors at the owner's accepted risk [11:15][24:09]. A field-experience note: heater kerosene oil contaminating the underside of deck prevents paint adhesion and forces a re-clean, so a cleanliness clause belongs in the spec [26:24].

### Erection Notes
Piece-count reduction is framed as the primary erection-cost lever; erectors report that cutting handled pieces by a quarter to a third yields meaningful savings, achieved by longer-spanning deck that widens purlin and floor-beam spacing [02:05][02:35]. The counter-issue is that deep heavy decks (a 7 inch 15 or 16 gauge sheet) exceed safe two-man manhandling when the crane lands them, where 2 and 3 inch sheets are handled by two men per end [03:06][03:36]. Curved long-span roofs create a gap between the deck and a flat purlin top; either a continuous bent plate is run along the member so the deck bears flat, or a power-actuated fastener manufacturer will not warrant capacity unless the deck bears flat, which required field load testing on one project [30:28][31:30].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Failing to specify a vented form deck under insulating concrete leaves the concrete permanently mushy because moisture cannot escape, taking weeks or months to dry [23:39]. Hurricane-region roof membrane blow-offs are attributed to decks not attached properly at corners and edges where uplift pressures peak; engineers who say nothing about fastening get only SDI minimums from the roofing contractor, "you don't get anything you don't pay for" [18:29][24:40]. Power-driven pins gave early field trouble where varying steel hardness changed the required pin type and driving power, improved somewhat by the more consistent A992 material [29:28]. Crimped side-lap fasteners historically gave almost no diaphragm capacity unless a patented crimping process is used [28:57].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The recurring estimating message is that two designs at 12 vs 14 psf are not ranked by weight but by manhours per ton and dollars per ton across fabrication, shipping, handling, and erection, so a heavier longer-spanning system with fewer pieces can be cheaper in place [01:33][02:05]. Deck cost premiums are non-linear: gauge above 18 jumps disproportionately, and exotic coatings or paints raise unit deck cost, so spec choices directly move the deck line item [05:38][19:29]. Form deck with bar joist at 2 to 3 foot spacing remains slightly cheaper than composite deck with composite beams at 8 to 10 foot on center per the speaker's market, a comparison worth pricing both ways [22:06][22:37].
