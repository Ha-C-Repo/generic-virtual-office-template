## Simple Connections Simplified
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dVE_udNdt1o | Duration: 1:30:50 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This NASCC Steel Conference session, presented by Tim Holtermann (PE/SE, Canam Buildings, SJI Engineering Practice Committee chairman) and Jeff Martin (PE, Vulcraft-Verco) and sponsored by the Steel Joist Institute, walks through the "simple" connections that actually consume coordination time on a steel job: how open-web joists and joist girders attach to their supports, and how steel roof and floor deck attaches to the structure and to itself. The thesis is that no single connection is correct in isolation. Joist end anchorage, deck support fasteners, deck side-lap fasteners, and hung loads each have several code-compliant options, and the cheapest compliant combination depends on erector preference, regional product availability, and whether wind uplift or seismic diaphragm demand controls [01:30][50:00]. The recurring theme is to standardize the spec to the simplest detail that satisfies the heaviest member rather than chasing per-member optimization.

### Spec References
The session is SJI-centric and deck-standard-centric, not an AISC 360 talk. ### Technical Details
Joist end anchorage scales with joist series. K-series and the smallest LH take a 1/8 in by 2-1/2 in fillet and a 1/2 in A307 bearing bolt; LH02-06 step up to 3/16 in by 2-1/2 in; LH07-17 and DLH10-17 go to 1/4 in by 2-1/2 in with 3/4 in A307; the largest DLH18-25 and joist girders need 1/4 in by 4 in welds, and joist girders over 50 plf self-weight require two 3/4 in A325 bolts [04:30][11:30]. Frame 0004 shows the full SJI table verbatim. Bridging now carries specified weld sizes, starting at a 1/8 in by 1 in fillet good through LH-15, and bolted bridging uses a 3/8 in A307 through K-series and LH-12 [15:30][16:20]. Diagonal bridging must be joined at the X intersection because the design takes the unbraced length from that center point [16:40]. For deck, three support-fastener families exist: welds (strongest, slowest, leave burn marks), power-actuated fasteners (5 to 6 times faster, slightly lower strength), and self-drilling screws (#12 or #14 per SDI), with PAF capacity sensitive to chord thickness so the pin must match the support [50:00][56:00]. Arc-spot puddle welds carry a 1/2 in effective / 3/4 in visible and a 3/8 in effective / 5/8 in visible call-out, an east-versus-west naming difference shown on frame 0015 [52:00]. Side laps use button punches (cheapest, weakest), screws, top-seam / arc-top-seam welds (strongest single connection), or proprietary clinch connections that match weld system capacity at button-punch speed [62:40][66:00].

### Fabrication Notes
The joist manufacturer carries most of the shop scope here. Seat bolts only need snug-tight because web-member geometry blocks a power tool from fully tensioning them, so a tensioned bolt is neither required nor practical [06:30]. Where a bolted seat is detailed but the bolt is omitted in the field, the empty slot must be welded inside the slot first, then supplemented on the seat edges away from the slot to reach required length [07:15][08:00]. Skewed bearings under 15 degrees should be avoided by skewing the joist full-length to the far beam or by adding a manufacturer-supplied header roughly one foot off the skew intersection [08:30][10:00]. Beam-to-joist and beam-to-joist-girder connections (shear tabs, 5x5 connection angles full chord depth, coped seated underslung beams) must be coordinated with the joist manufacturer because the added load and the angle location can conflict with interior web members, and webs are not guaranteed to all sit inside [29:00][31:00]. Field drilling of joist chords or webs to hang trade items is not permitted, especially the bottom tension chord [41:00].

### Erection Notes
Bolted-and-welded joist anchorage is the industry norm on steel bearings over 40 ft because OSHA requires bolts on tie joists and long spans, and slotted-hole bolts alone may not laterally brace the support compression chord [02:00]. Horizontal bridging welds are minimal (often 1/8 in by 1 in) but the angle stock is thin, so a careless erector burns holes through the lap; frame and slide photos show rejectable burn-through [17:30]. Deck installers carry strong fastener preferences that are often unknown until the installer is hired, so the spec should leave a compliant range rather than dictate one method [50:30]. Support-attachment pattern naming (36/7, 36/4) means two welds per support on interlocking deck but one weld serving both sheets on nested deck, a real source of field miscount [70:00]. Deck laps on open-web joists should land over a chord angle, not the center of the joist, and butt joints either land both sheets on one chord angle or provide a defined load path between chord angles [79:00].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named failures: an omitted seat bolt leaving a weak welded ribbon next to the slot [07:15]; burn-through and sloppy laps on thin bridging angle [17:30]; field-coped outriggers and field-drilled chords, both discouraged [27:30][41:00]; wrong PAF pin for chord thickness, illustrated by a project where four different pins appeared in every bay and joists were color-coded by paint to sort them, a near-impossible inspection [57:00]. Weld washers must not be used unless deck is thinner than 22 gauge, a point on which AWS, AISI, and SDI agree [58:30]. SDI specifically prohibits welding 22 gauge nested deck [54:00]. Button-punch quality has no easy field check beyond pushing on it [63:30]. The closing field-fix anecdote, an engineer called out to certify that dents in delivered deck were acceptable, underlines that deck overload and handling damage drive callbacks [87:55].

### Bid / Cost Implications
This is the estimating spine of the talk. Welds are the most expensive and slowest deck attachment; power-actuated fasteners install 5 to 6 times faster at nearly the same system capacity; the cheapest compliant system is often more, faster fasteners rather than fewer, stronger ones, and lighter-gauge deck with more fasteners can beat heavier deck with fewer [50:00][90:00]. A 36/7/4 hybrid (7 fasteners at sheet ends, 4 at interior supports, 37 fasteners over 6 spans, frame 0019) buys strength near a full 36/7 at the fastener count of a 36/5, a direct material and labor saving [69:30]. The recommendation to standardize all girder bolts to A325 and all girder welds to 4 in when girders are mixed trades simplicity for a small premium and avoids field sorting [05:00][12:30]. Specifying parallel deck attachment only where lateral load actually transfers, instead of at every parallel member crossed, removes cost with no structural benefit [75:50]. Hung-load and chord-thickness decisions made early avoid expensive field rework.
