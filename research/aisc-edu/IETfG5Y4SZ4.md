### Core Concept
This webinar, presented by Aldo McKay of Protection Engineering Consultants on 2016-03-24, covers blast-resistant analysis and design of steel buildings, the second part of a two-part series after Part 1 on blast loads [00:01]. The controlling idea is radical simplification: a building hit by blast responds in compression, tension, and twisting in every direction, but the engineer analyzes it component by component and tracks one dominant mode of response, usually flexure, because bending lets a member absorb large energy cheaply [04:36][19:19]. Design is not stress-based; it is governed by deformation limits expressed as support rotation and ductility ratio, with members pushed into the ultimate plastic capacity region rather than held to first yield [12:39][13:10]. The envelope is the first line of defense, so the engineer concentrates on exposed members, generally the roof, occasional perimeter or spandrel beams, and rarely columns [16:43][17:44].

### Technical Details
Blast load arrives as a pressure-impulse pair (triangular pulse, impulse equals half the duration times peak pressure) or as charge weight plus standoff [24:31]. The member is reduced to a single-degree-of-freedom spring-mass system defined by stiffness, ultimate resistance, and mass, with a KLM load-mass transformation factor applied to the mass to keep energy equivalent to the real member [30:46][34:20]. Dynamic moment capacity multiplies the static yield by two factors: the dynamic increase factor (DIF) for strain-rate effects, roughly 20 percent for 50 KSI steel, and the static increase factor (SIF), about 5 percent, to lift the lower-bound spec strength to realistic strength [35:21][36:21]. Energy (close-form) solutions apply only when clearly impulse-controlled (load duration below one third of natural period) or pressure-controlled (duration at least 10 times natural period); the worked roof-beam example landed firmly impulsive and gave 4.6 inch midspan deflection by hand versus about 4 inches in the ESBEDS single-degree tool [47:38][48:40][49:41]. Beam-columns use the Chapter H interaction set to one, solved for reduced moment capacity, and the speaker noted he has not seen a blast column exceed 20 percent of P critical because protected interior columns carry the heavy loads [54:48][55:18].
### Fabrication Notes
The fabrication-relevant rule is the column base plate: the speaker stressed a recessed base plate with grout to keep lateral reactions in shear straight into the floor diaphragm, because a non-recessed plate lets the anchor bolts fail in shear while the column itself bends well into the plastic range [53:15][53:45]. Members must be compact sections only so they can develop full plastic capacity, and the engineer uses the full plastic section modulus Z for beams once ductility passes three, but only the average of elastic and plastic modulus for plates unless ductility exceeds about seven [37:22][38:26][38:57]. Steel grades discussed were 36 and 50 KSI, with 50 KSI (A992 for the W10x100 shear example) more typical [38:26][65:33].
### Erection Notes
The webinar is analysis-focused and gives little on sequencing, cranes, or rigging. The closest erection content is the bracing requirement: the compression flange must be braced, and for roof members the engineer often must brace the bottom flange as well because rebound makes it the compression flange [38:57]. Frames generally need no blast design because their sway natural period is so long relative to the millisecond load that the response approaches zero, which means lateral bracing systems and foundations are usually sized by the equivalent static facade reactions rather than direct blast on the frame [80:00][81:32].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The named failures are connection and base-plate failures. A test column bent deep into the plastic range but its anchor bolts sheared because the base plate was not recessed, the classic weak-point failure [53:45]. A window-retrofit steel frame pulled out of its concrete beam in a concrete breakout because the connection was not designed to stay attached, sending the whole frame into the occupied space [70:09]. The recurring design error theme is rebound: connections sized only for inbound load can fail when the member springs back, so elastic members get equal inbound and outbound connection design and yielding members can use about 50 percent of the inbound for rebound [67:37][68:08]. In the column example the flexural response passed but shear capacity was inadequate for the ultimate moment, forcing a web plate or a heavier member [78:27].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Blast work is best inserted at roughly the 65 percent design stage, after members and geometry are set for conventional and serviceability loads, so the blast engineer tweaks connection capacity, spacing, and member strength rather than starting from zero [20:22][41:28]. Designing components to bend ductilely is the cost lever: keeping members elastic under blast is described as far too expensive, and over-conservative strength assumptions drive heavy sections [19:50][91:23]. Single-degree-of-freedom analysis is favored because it balances accuracy with the speed needed on fast design-build teams, and modern tools make a single-degree run faster than a hand calculation [09:08][50:42].
