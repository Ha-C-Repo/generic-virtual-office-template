## Where Did That Force Come From? Combining Diaphragm Braced Frame Force
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Lr_k78ess2k | Duration: 1:26:55 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Raphael Sabelli of Walter P Moore presents a design methodology for reconciling three inconsistent force sets that meet where a diaphragm collector enters a braced frame: base shear forces (wind or seismic, in red), diaphragm acceleration forces Fpx (yellow), and capacity-design mechanism forces from yielding fuse members (blue) [01:33][04:07][05:10]. He is explicit that this is engineering judgment bridging a gap between ASCE 7 and the AISC seismic provisions, not a code requirement [02:04]. The method builds two or three artificial load cases, solving each connection by statics: case one uses the vertical distribution or mechanism forces and tends to govern vertical forces, case two swaps in the Fpx collector force with a statical adjustment and tends to govern horizontal forces, and a third case captures the post-buckled condition for special concentrically braced frames [22:09][67:02].
### Spec References
ASCE 7 (cited as "ASCE 7" / "A7") for the equivalent lateral force vertical distribution and the diaphragm Fpx forces; AISC 341 seismic provisions for the capacity-design connection forces and the special concentrically braced frame analysis at Section F2.3 (two analyses: maximum tension plus maximum compression, then maximum tension with compression degraded to 30 percent) [04:38][65:59][69:42]. The Omega-sub-o overstrength factor is applied to collector connections starting in seismic design category C [08:14][23:11]. Systems named by their code designations: R=3, ordinary concentrically braced frame at R=3.25, and the higher-R special concentrically braced frame (SCBF), eccentrically braced frame (EBF), and buckling restrained braced frame (BRBF) [08:14][39:05]. Material and strain-hardening factors cited: Ry for material overstrength and a 1.1 factor for BRBF connections, and 1.25 strain hardening on the EBF link yield strength [42:42][43:13].
### Technical Details
Forces are resolved at the gusset-to-beam and gusset-to-column joints using the uniform force method (or a variant), then statics gives the beam axial force, the beam shear, and the connection forces [13:19][14:53]. For modal response spectrum analysis the speaker introduces a purple set: signs are lost to the square-root-of-sum-of-squares, forces are not statically balanced, the engineer assigns directions, and section cuts must track each quantity mode by mode because differences between modal combination maxima are mathematically meaningless [26:49][29:53]. For SCBF the post-buckled case three drives a large axial drag force through the beam along a zigzag load path once compression braces lose roughly 70 percent of capacity [49:55][80:15]. In the worked four-story example, the SCBF connection horizontal force jumped to 266 kips under the post-buckled case three against only 10 kips in the elastic analysis [60:16].
### Fabrication Notes
This is a structural design webinar on how connection design forces are derived, so it does not cover shop fit-up, cutting, or QC processes directly. The fabrication-relevant takeaway is that the connection designer should be given the full set of corresponding force cases (case one, case two, and case three where it applies), because handing over only the lumped maximum of each component produces a statically inconsistent set that forces another conservative adjustment downstream [35:29][62:22].
### Erection Notes
The webinar does not address erection sequencing, bracing, rigging, or crane work. Its physical content is the in-service load path: after compression braces buckle and degrade, force must zigzag through beams to a tension brace or to an out-of-plane braced frame 30 to 40 feet away via diaphragm shear, which is a permanent-condition behavior rather than an erection procedure [80:15].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
No weld or bolt inspection failures are named. The design errors flagged are conceptual: assuming the brace forces are zero when diaphragm forces are applied, which would lead to a connection that can fail [07:42]; applying all Fpx forces simultaneously, which grossly overestimates shear and overturning demand [20:04]; over-conservatively increasing the brace-below force instead of reducing the brace-above force, which the speaker calls inconsistent with the code [25:49]; and using differences between modal combination maxima, which is meaningless [29:53].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The choice of collector load-path assumption (for example 33/33/33 across three bays versus 100 percent into the center bay, or a shortened collector that discounts part of the building width) directly changes how heavily the connections are designed and therefore the connection material and shop labor [21:07][74:29]. The post-buckled SCBF case can drive a connection from 10 kips to 266 kips of horizontal force, so a frame priced without it would be badly under-detailed [60:16]. Communicating three corresponding force cases rather than one lumped envelope avoids piling conservatism on conservatism, which trims heavier-than-needed connection steel [35:29].
