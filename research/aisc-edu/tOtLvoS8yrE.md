## Introduction to FHWA Bridge Welding Reference Manual
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tOtLvoS8yrE | Duration: 1:31:25 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Ronnie Medlock of High Steel Structures introduces the FHWA Bridge Welding Reference Manual, a roughly 300-page free download that explains and complements the AASHTO/AWS D1.5 Bridge Welding Code for designers, materials engineers, and shop-oversight inspectors [01:36][07:49]. The manual covers weld anatomy, welding processes, WPS and welder qualification, workmanship and hydrogen control, inspection and NDE, fracture control, fabrication and constructability details, and the engineer's role [08:23 through 09:54]. A recurring thread is the comparison between the bridge code D1.5 and the structural welding code D1.1, since D1.5 is excellent for bridges but not always the right code for non-bridge transportation steel [10:55][26:49].

### Spec References
The speaker named these standards aloud: AWS D1.5 Bridge Welding Code (paragraph 1.2.2 for approved base metals, Table 4.1 for material-electrode classifications) [27:50][28:20]; AWS D1.1 Structural Welding Code [26:49]; AWS D1.6 for stainless steel welding [82:51]; ASTM/AASHTO A709 as the bridge steel family (the 2015 D1.5 referenced A709 broadly, the 2020 edition lists grades 36, 50, 50W explicitly) [29:21][29:52]; A7 as a legacy steel without chemistry control [84:54]; A709 grade 50CR weathering stainless [82:20]; the AWS A5 consumable specifications that the D1.5 electrode classifications point to [87:32]; AWS A2.4 welding symbols [71:42]; and AISC Design Guide 21 on welded connections by Duane Miller [73:01]. The presenter did not cite AISC 360 or AISC 341 sections, which is expected for a bridge-code talk.

### Technical Details
Arc welding melts both base and weld metal through a roughly 6,000-degree arc against steel that melts near 2,800 degrees, with a shield protecting the arc from contaminants [13:29][15:03]. The processes covered are shielded metal arc (stick), flux-cored arc (gas-shielded or self-shielded), gas metal arc including metal-cored wire, submerged arc, and electroslag [15:34 through 21:42]. Submerged arc is the workhorse of bridge fabrication because it mechanizes well and uses large 3/32 and 5/32 inch wires versus the 0.052 to 0.05 inch wires of FCAW and GMAW, depositing far more weld metal for big flange and web butt splices [20:41][21:11]. GMAW short-circuiting transfer is prohibited by the bridge code for thick material because its low heat will not fuse the base metal, though it is useful for thin stock [18:39].

### Fabrication Notes
Approved welding procedures are required for all bridge welding, and fabrication cannot begin until procedures are approved, so reviewers must return WPS approvals in time [34:04][35:05]. WPS qualification is documented by a PQR using a wide single-side groove in one-inch plate with all-weld-metal and tension tests, and because qualified weld metal overmatches the base metal the tension specimen always fails in the base metal [35:36 through 37:41]. Prequalified standard joints in the code carry detailed fit-up tolerances; a fabricator can show a root face from zero to minus one-eighth on a quarter-inch detail and gets an added plus or minus 1/16 on the shop floor, but changing any setting or skipping a required backgouge turns the joint non-standard and forces requalification [30:55 through 33:34]. Hydrogen control drives much of the practice: keep consumable coils sealed until use, store stick electrodes in heated rod ovens, and apply thickness-based preheat, with optional post-heat the fabricator may add since D1.5 prescribes preheat but not post-heat [46:19 through 47:19].

### Erection Notes
Field welding is presented as fully viable when D1.5 controls are followed, used effectively for retrofits and for clean aesthetic structures where a bolted splice was not wanted [72:00][72:30]. Self-shielded flux-cored is noted as the option for remote or field situations without shielding gas [17:36]. When field-joining complete built-up girders with full-penetration web and flange welds, use a weld access hole at the corner to avoid a quality problem at the weld intersection [66:54]. The talk is bridge-oriented and does not cover crane, rigging, or member-erection sequencing in the building-steel sense.

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named discontinuities include incomplete fusion, hot (solidification) cracks, slag inclusions, undercut, overlap, craters, porosity, spatter, arc strikes, base-metal laminations and lamellar tearing, and hydrogen-assisted cold cracks [41:15 through 42:47]. The code prohibits arc strikes and spatter [41:46]. Hydrogen cracking needs the combination of hydrogen, stress, and a susceptible (hard) microstructure, avoided by using prescribed A709 steels and checking older steels with a carbon-equivalency formula in D1.5 [43:48 through 44:48][85:25]. NDE methods are magnetic particle for surface cracks, radiography, ultrasonic, and phased-array UT; the Hoan Bridge intersecting-weld crack from constraint plus a discontinuity is the cautionary failure, but the lesson was misconstrued into a wrong belief that welds should never touch [47:50 through 65:52].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Process choice is a productivity and cost lever: stick is versatile but slow, FCAW and GMAW are mid-range, and submerged arc is the most productive for the long mechanized welds bridges need [23:14 through 23:44]. Phased-array UT is presented as better and cheaper to run than RT because results are immediate, the equipment is portable, it needs no radiation standoff or night shooting, and it inspects T and corner joints that RT cannot, with Florida DOT correlation work supporting its acceptance [52:29 through 57:40]. Constructability choices move cost directly: outside-only fillet welds on a box beat full-penetration welds, single-pass fillets beat multipass, thickness transitions are preferred over width transitions to keep one girder line and one-pass equipment usable, and the manual advises checking local fabricator equipment preferences before detailing [67:24 through 71:29][78:07].

### Your Company Application
This is the AWS D1.5 bridge code, distinct from the AWS D1.1 building-steel code Mario Gutierrez runs to, but several principles map cleanly. Mario's welding program benefits from the same hydrogen discipline (sealed consumable storage, heated rod ovens, thickness-based preheat) and the same prequalified-joint logic: any deviation from a standard joint detail forces requalification, which Tekla detailing and our in-house PE should respect when authoring connection details and weld symbols per AWS A2.4. For Ivan's takeoff, the cost story is that process and joint selection drive labor, and submerged-arc-friendly long welds and single-pass fillets are cheaper than full-penetration multipass welds, which is a useful sanity check on welded-connection labor on any heavy job such as ACP.
