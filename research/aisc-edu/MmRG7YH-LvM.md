## Part 2: Seismic Design for Non-West Coast Engineers
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MmRG7YH-LvM | Duration: 1:03:07 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Mike Engelhardt (UT Austin) introduces earthquake engineering for engineers outside high-seismic regions, with the second half focused on steel [00:31]. The governing idea is that we do not beat earthquakes with strength, we survive them with ductility, trading elastic strength for controlled yielding [00:31, 09:17]. ASCE 7 sets the lateral strength and stiffness demand, and AISC 341 (Seismic Provisions for Structural Steel Buildings) supplements AISC 360 to tell us how to build ductility in through ductile detailing [08:47, 31:50]. The unifying method is capacity design: pick fuse elements to yield, detail them for ductility, then make everything else stronger than the fuse so the fuse yields first [33:24, 55:01].

No AISC 360 chapter/section or J-table numbers were spoken in this part; those are not invented here.
### Technical Details
Seismic Design Category (SDC) A through F controls everything and depends on geographic location, soil/site class (A hard rock through E soft clay, F site-specific, default D), and the building risk category 1 through 4 (most buildings are category 2) [10:49, 11:50, 16:28]. Spectral accelerations S_S (short period, about 0.2 sec) and S_1 (1 sec) come from ASCE 7 maps or the free USGS lat/long tool, are modified for soil, then multiplied by 2/3 for the design spectrum [12:20, 14:23, 17:59]. Base shear V = C_s times building weight, where the elastic demand is divided by the response modification factor R [25:40, 26:42]. R values stated: special moment frame R=8, intermediate moment frame R=4.5, special concentrically braced frame R=6, undetailed systems R=3 (allowed only in SDC B and C) [28:17, 29:47]. Systems covered: special/intermediate/ordinary moment frames (beam flexural yielding is the fuse), special/ordinary concentrically braced frames (brace yields in tension, buckles in compression), eccentrically braced frames (shear-yielding link 2 to 3 ft long is the fuse), buckling restrained braced frames (steel core yields tension and compression, casing prevents buckling), and special plate shear walls (thin web plate buckles then yields in tension field) [36:01, 39:36, 44:42, 49:19, 53:27].
### Fabrication Notes
Fuse-element detailing rules are deliberately simple: no high-strength steel (Grade 50 or less, because higher strengths tend to be brittle), low width-thickness (b/t) ratios to delay local buckling, and good lateral bracing because beams want to twist [34:26]. EBF links require many fitted stiffeners welded in to handle shear buckling, which Engelhardt calls one of the more costly detailing features [46:45, 47:16]. BRBs are proprietary and bought from certified manufacturers (Star Seismic, CoreBrace, Nippon Steel named) because the required qualification testing is not affordable to replicate; the brace is a debonded steel core sliding inside a grout-filled HSS casing [49:49, 51:22]. AISC 341 carries a quality control / quality assurance section that grew directly out of Northridge welding-quality failures [55:32]. Welding done correctly is about 90 percent of the battle of keeping a steel building standing [61:38].
### Erection Notes
The talk is design-oriented and does not walk field sequencing, crane, or rigging. The erection-relevant content is that lateral frames are placed only in selected locations, not everywhere, because they are expensive, while the rest is gravity framing [36:01]. Braces must point in both directions so that as half buckle the other half carry tension [43:11]. Connections must be detailed to let a brace rotate when it buckles (in-plane or out-of-plane) rather than tear the connection apart, which is a fit-up and gusset-geometry concern that carries into the field [44:12].
### Bolting / Welding
This part cites welding standards, not bolting. Welded beam-to-column moment connections were the central Northridge failure, where connections fractured instead of the beam yielding as intended [05:40, 06:13]. For high-level seismic detailing the controlling welding documents are AISC 358 prequalified moment connections plus AWS D1.8, the seismic supplement to AWS D1.1 [61:07, 61:38]. No bolt grades (A325/A490), no RCSC pretension or turn-of-nut method, and no electrode or preheat values were stated, so none are recorded here.
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named real failures: 1985 Mexico City, a 21-story welded steel building collapsed onto a 14-story steel building [04:38]. 1994 Northridge, no collapse but widespread brittle fracture of welded beam-to-column connections, plus fractured HSS brace and brace connections and a cracked heavy base plate, all non-ductile behavior the design philosophy did not intend [05:40, 06:13, 06:44]. 1995 Kobe, welded beam-to-column fractures, brittle fracture of heavy welded box columns, brace-connection failures, and residual drift [07:14, 07:45]. The recurring lesson is a fracture or buckling limit state reaching capacity before the chosen fuse yields, traced largely to welding quality [32:22, 55:32].
### Bid / Cost Implications
SDC drives everything downstream: SDC A (Austin) requires essentially no seismic design, B and C calculate forces but usually skip ductile detailing, and D, E, F demand full California-level detailing even far from California [22:08, 23:09]. Dividing the elastic force by R gives very low design lateral forces (16 percent of building weight in Santa Clarita, 6.5 percent in Memphis for an R=8 SMF), so members and foundations are economical, but the offset is real money spent putting ductility in, since ductility is not free [29:47, 30:50]. EBF link stiffeners and BRB proprietary braces are concrete cost adders, and special detailing pulls in AISC 358, AWS D1.8, and a QA/QC program that adds inspection scope [46:45, 51:22, 55:32]. Site-class geotechnical work can justify a class above the default D and save money [16:58].
### Your Company Application
For Houston (Harris County), Texas is low-seismic, generally SDC A or B, so most Your Company work uses undetailed systems designed like wind (R=3) with no AISC 341 ductile detailing, and Ivan's takeoff should confirm the SDC from the structural general notes before assuming seismic scope is absent. The doctrine value is the capacity-design and fuse logic, which maps cleanly onto our verify-don't-generate posture: if a set does invoke AISC 341 (an out-of-region GC, a higher risk category, or a Memphis/out-of-state job), the bid must flag demand-critical welding, AISC 358 prequalified moment connections, AWS D1.8, EBF link stiffeners, or BRB proprietary braces as cost and schedule adders, and Mario's welding plus the in-house PE stamp must address D1.8 and the QA/QC program rather than default D1.1. Anchor-rod and base-plate work ties to the cracked-base-plate Northridge failure, reinforcing that column bases must develop the fuse capacity in any detailed system.
