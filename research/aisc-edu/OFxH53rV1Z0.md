### Core Concept
This segment teaches the mechanics behind bolted-joint strength: why bolt shear strength is independent of pretension, how pretension is generated and then dissipated, and when a slip-critical connection is actually required. The instructor walks bearing-type (shear) bolts first, then transitions to the slip-critical limit state, building both from first principles before stating the AISC specification rules [02:39, 17:41].

### Technical Details
Pretension is the bolt's elastic resistance to small axial elongation as the nut is turned; once shear deformation and shear yielding begin in the connected plates, those small axial elongations dissipate and bolt pretension at the shear-failure point is essentially zero, confirmed by tests and strain-gauge measurements [00:33, 02:08]. Nominal shear strength is 0.62 Fu (a von Mises shear-yield approximation of test results), then cut 20% to 0.50 Fu, giving 60 KSI for A325 (0.5 x 120) and 75 KSI for A490 (0.5 x 150) for the threads-excluded case [03:42, 04:15]. Threads in the shear plane force an additional reduction of about 20% for reduced area [04:46, 08:25]. Slip load is a function of clamping force (total pretension from all bolts), slip coefficient (mu), and number of slip planes, and is independent of contact area per the inclined-plane friction principle [10:59, 11:31, 17:41].

### Fabrication Notes
The instructor stresses that faying-surface contact must exist over most of the connection to develop the clamping reaction, but small gaps or area variation do not need to be policed because slip resistance is area-independent; he calls leaving large gaps poor practice while noting it does not compromise integrity [12:02, 12:32]. Surface condition drives the slip coefficient (bare mill scale steel example), so the fabricated faying-surface class is the controlling input for any slip-critical joint [10:28]. No shop fit-up tolerance numbers or WPS/weld-procedure details are given in this clip.

### Erection Notes
Pretension must reach at least 70% of bolt ultimate tensile strength for slip-critical performance (120 KSI A325 means roughly 84 KSI minimum), and that target is reduced for non-standard holes (about 15% reduction recalled for an oversized hole) [19:13, 19:43]. The speaker explicitly advises against inspecting for bolt tension on bearing-type shear connections that are not slip-critical, since shear strength does not depend on pretension; pretensioning for the installer's own purposes is fine, but do not argue about an inspection that was never required [00:01]. The field anecdote (welders arriving without the bolts) underscores that bolt installation and inspection are separate erection steps still to be covered [09:57, 08:55].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The named failure mode is fatigue cracking in slip-critical joints under repetitive load: relative slip scratches the faying surfaces, scratches grow into fatigue striations, and the crack initiates not at the hole (a forgiving detail) but at larger surface scratches away from the hole, then propagates toward it [14:35, 15:05]. The recurring practical error is unnecessary tension inspection on plain bearing connections, and a second is defaulting to no-slip-at-factored-loads without justification when no-slip-at-service-loads would do [00:01, 16:39, 17:10].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The instructor states plainly that no-slip-at-factored-loads is more expensive than no-slip-at-service-loads and should be the exception, justified rather than defaulted [16:08, 17:10]. The 20% long-joint penalty (joints over 50 inches) and the threads-in-shear-plane reduction both lower allowable bolt capacity, which can drive more or larger bolts and added cost; for shorter joints (he cites up to about 15 inches) he believes the penalty is conservative, implying potential capacity recovery on a case-by-case engineered basis [05:50, 06:20]. Specifying slip-critical only where repetitive load, load reversal, or geometry-sensitive cases genuinely require it avoids needless pretension and inspection cost [13:02, 13:32].
