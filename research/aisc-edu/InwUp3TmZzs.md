## Practical Steel Metallurgy

### Core Concept
This AISC webinar, presented by Doug Reese Evans of Steel Dynamics, teaches the working metallurgy a structural steel user needs without going deep into theory. It is organized around four practical questions: what is the difference between iron and steel, why are there multiple grades of steel, how can a mill control chemistry, and if I retest a product will I match the mill test report. The controlling idea is that steel properties come from chemistry plus microstructure together, not chemistry alone [56:25], and that grain size from rolling reduction can drive strength more than alloy content does [62:35]. The takeaway for a fabricator is that grade names are context labels on the same underlying material, mill test report values are nominal not exact, and retesting on the wrong coupon gives misleadingly different numbers.

### Technical Details
Steel is iron alloyed with up to 2 weight percent carbon; above 2 percent it is cast iron, which forms graphite flakes that act as stress risers and is unsuitable structurally [06:37, 22:34]. Cast steel is not cast iron and can be engineered to normal carbon-steel chemistries [24:35]. Iron is allotropic, transforming from face-centered-cubic austenite to body-centered-cubic ferrite on cooling; austenite holds 2 percent carbon, ferrite only 0.02 percent, and the rejected carbon forms cementite, giving the ferrite-pearlite microstructure [14:23, 15:24, 17:27]. Slow cooling gives pearlite, fast cooling gives bainite or brittle martensite [19:29]. Finer grain size raises strength because dislocations pile up at grain boundaries, and grain size is set mainly by rolling reduction: a light shape gets more reduction and finer grain than a heavy one [60:32, 62:35]. The practical illustration: a W14x398 carries more chemistry yet lower yield than a W16x36 because the heavy flange sees less reduction and coarser grain [63:06].

### Fabrication Notes
The fabrication-relevant content is metallurgical rather than shop-process. Carbon equivalent is the key number for weldability: it folds alloying elements into an equivalent carbon figure to predict heat-affected-zone behavior, and higher carbon content trades weldability and ductility for strength [85:47, 86:18]. Killed steel matters for clean welds and sound sections; aluminum or silicon additions tie up dissolved oxygen so no carbon-monoxide voids form, and continuous-cast structural product is fully killed [71:49, 73:22]. Laminar tearing is driven by inclusion level and type, shows up mainly at weld joints, and is reduced by cleaner steel practice; rolled steel is anisotropic for laminar tearing even though it is isotropic at yield [82:40, 83:43]. ### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The most actionable failure mode for a fabricator is retest mismatch. A third-party lab that lacks capacity for the full-thickness ASTM A370 plate coupon often substitutes a 0.505 round, which samples a local lamination rather than the through-thickness average and can report exaggerated, location-dependent yield, especially in thick shapes or A913 quench-self-temper material [78:35, 79:05, 79:37, 80:07]. The core area of a heavy W shape (ASTM 1.5 in, AISC 2 in or greater) has the coarsest grain and the worst sulfur and phosphorus segregation, so it reads lowest in strength and elongation [77:33, 78:03]. Historic errors named: brittle cast-iron railroad bridges that fractured under load [24:35], and the standing specification warning against acid Bessemer steel because that process could not remove sulfur or phosphorus [48:48].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The multi-certification point is the estimating lever: a single mill-rolled shape can simultaneously satisfy A36, A572-50, A709-50, and A992, so the same physical steel carries four grade names and the grade is a context choice, not a different product [37:01, 39:33]. A992 cannot always be supplied in light single angles because the section is too light to hit the alloy and carbon levels the grade requires [40:04]. Specifying a fracture-critical or low-temperature Charpy suffix (A709 F-grades, 25 ft-lb) adds testing and cost over the plain base grade [33:52]. The historical cost contrast (Bessemer steel far cheaper than puddled iron) underlines that grade and process selection have always been cost decisions [47:47].
