## Design of Facade Attachments, Session L2: Facade Attachments, Part 2

### Core Concept
Alex Zimmer of Simpson Gumpertz and Heger, co-author of AISC Design Guide 22, teaches how to design the slab edge, the spandrel beam, and the steel hardware that carries a building facade into the steel frame [01:01][02:02]. The central theme is that minor architectural or structural differences at the slab edge drive large construction cost differences, and that spandrel-beam design is governed by serviceability (vertical deflection plus torsional rotation that opens or closes facade joints), not by flexural or shear strength [02:02][33:52][40:38]. Two slab-edge approaches frame the session: approach one lets the slab or deck cantilever past the spandrel beam and resolve the facade eccentricity as a beam, and approach two transfers all facade load through a bent plate as both vertical force and torsion onto the spandrel beam [03:03][09:37].

### Technical Details
Slab flexure must address negative moment over the spandrel beam from the cantilever, so top reinforcement is required well into the span, and deformed bar or bar mats are preferred over welded wire fabric because WWF gets walked down toward the deck during construction [05:34][08:37]. Effective compression-block width depends on deck orientation: deck perpendicular to the spandrel gives full slab depth but only the concrete width between flutes, while deck parallel gives full width but reduced effective depth [06:36]. Facade point loads are spread at a non-codified 1:1 (45 degree) slope to set effective width for shear and flexure [07:37]. Worked example 5.6 used FV 1.8 kips at 5 in eccentricity, FH 1.8 kips, a 9 in bent-plate cantilever, a 7/16 in A36 plate, and 1.5 in fillet welds at 12 in on center, dragging roughly 5.8 kips into each threaded bar coupler [23:36][27:10][28:10]. For spandrel torsion the beam is correctly idealized as rotating about a pin at the top flange (restrained by the slab), not about its centroid; Design Guide 22 introduces the "modified Design Guide 9 method" and "modified flexural analogy method" for this, with flexural analogy accurate for short torsional spans near 10 ft and Design Guide 9 closer to FE results at 30 ft spans [48:50][50:22][53:28][54:29].

### Fabrication Notes
Bent-plate attachment has three fabrication options: shop welded (option A, most economical, no field adjustment), shipped with an erection bolt then field welded (option B), or short-slotted-hole slip-critical (option C); the speaker prefers B or C for field adjustability [19:27][19:57]. Light-gauge pour stops are 12 to 20 gauge stay-in-place forms, sized only for wet concrete plus construction live load and not for facade loads [11:41][12:42]. Headed studs or deformed-bar anchors with female couplers are welded to the bent-plate vertical leg to drag facade force into the slab, and the deformed-bar coupler is now preferred because studs on the leg are a tripping hazard for ironworkers; these are often field installed rather than shop installed [16:15][18:23]. Hot-rolled bent plates were limited to 3/8 in in the Design Guide 22 selection tables [15:13]. Stud placement near the web can cause burn-through, so flange thickness may need to increase when the slab edge is left/right adjustable [20:59].

### Erection Notes
Adjustability is the dominant field theme: a long slotted hole, as much as a 3 in slot, lets the erector set the bent plate in or out, after which the plate is field welded to the top flange and the erection bolt is no longer load bearing [18:55][76:10]. Pour stops are field welded to the spandrel beam to absorb spandrel-location tolerance [11:41]. Kicker braces are time-consuming and uneconomical to erect at every hanger, so the speaker installs them only every 10 to 15 ft, or more often uses roll beams [42:41][43:13][85:24]. Roll beams are depressed below the spandrel top flange to avoid coping, connected through a full-depth fitted web stiffener with bolts designed to take the twist as a moment couple [44:44]. The speaker prefers roll beams on roof decks because they also brace the spandrel bottom flange against lateral-torsional buckling under wind uplift [85:56].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
A real-project failure was shown where the erector welded the shear-stud connector to the horizontal leg of the bent plate instead of to the beam top flange; the engineer caught it on site and required supplemental top-flange and underside welds to mobilize the studs [21:30]. Welded wire fabric being trampled down to the deck, where it no longer resists the negative moment over the spandrel, is named as a recurring construction problem driving the preference for deformed bar [08:37]. The presenter also corrected his own slide errors live (deck orientation labeled wrong, and wet concrete weight omitted from the example 5.6 bent-plate and weld check), noting both would be fixed in the next revision [37:31][77:42]. Stud-to-web burn-through is flagged as a fabrication defect when studs sit too close to the web [20:59].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Slab-edge details carry substantial construction cost, and consistent slab-edge conditions around the perimeter lower both cost and inspection effort, while a variety of conditions makes everyone's life expensive [02:02][03:03]. Serviceability, not strength, sets spandrel size, and the cost swing is large: a brick-on-CMU 30 ft spandrel that needs only a W16x26 for strength jumps to a W21x62 to hold a 3/16 in soft-joint deflection, more than double the weight per foot; brick-on-metal-stud likewise goes from W12x19 (strength) to W21x62 (service) [38:01][39:04][39:37]. The speaker warns that chasing minimum tonnage with many different spandrel sizes can cost more in facade-support engineering and field complication than the steel saved, so standardizing sections is often the cheaper total outcome [32:51][33:22]. Option A shop welding is cheapest but option B/C field-adjustable hardware is usually worth the added cost to absorb tolerance [19:27].
