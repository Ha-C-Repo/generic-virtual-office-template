## Case Studies in Construction Engineering of New and Existing Complex Steel Bridges
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3tRXaOW1wdE | Duration: 1:26:54 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This is a three-part World Steel Bridge Symposium panel on construction engineering for steel bridges, where the controlling discipline is the partially-erected state, not the finished structure. Talk one (Dan, HNTB) covers replacing a fracture-critical pin and link on the 1940 US-84 Mississippi River cantilever truss at Natchez [00:00], built around staged temporary load paths so a single member holding up the bridge can be cut and replaced. Talk two (Bob Cisneros, High Steel Structures) covers metrology and full trial assembly of a skewed, curved, haunched pedestrian bridge, and steel dead-load camber verification [31:49]. Talk three (Andrew Key Shaw, Alfred Benesch) is four erection-engineering case studies on girder stability during the pick, strand jacking, pier brackets, and a shoring tower on a barge [57:31]. The recurring theme is that structures are most vulnerable partially erected and that means-and-methods analysis, not the design code, governs that window [58:01].

### Technical Details
The Natchez truss is a five-span cantilever truss with a suspended span and quasi-suspended spans, the hangers being forged links (a roughly 10 in by 16 in by 7 ft 6 in "dog bone") on 10 in diameter pins, treated as a single fracture-critical pin-and-link system that must be replaced as an assembly [00:31][06:40]. Instrumentation tracked real forces: the diagonal bypass was tensioned to 836 kips against a member dead load of 761 kips, and the link dead load of about 660 kips showed a 655 kip change when bypassed [13:14]. Cutting the pins transferred 20 to 40 kips into the link and 1 to 3 ksi into the splice plate because stiffness tracks force [16:48]. The pedestrian bridge work centers on metrology: a total station accurate to roughly 3 mm at 300 to 600 ft, a 360 prism, horizontal control set from a MicroStation/AutoCAD model exported into the total station, and camber generated from the CAD/CAM single-source model [38:31][42:36]. Erection-engineering details include eigenvalue/geometrically-nonlinear buckling analysis, a two-girder system with factor of safety 1.21 fixed by adding top lateral bracing, quartering-wind control via twin HSS pipe braces, and "good roll" spreader-beam geometry to replumb girders during the pick [62:36][67:11][71:47].
### Fabrication Notes
High Steel's position is that full bridge assembly is rare and not recommended; the norm is unit assembly, and full trial assembly here was a contractual exception that produced near-laboratory data [34:56]. Holding tight fabrication tolerances when drilling full-size holes is a cost decision: the fabricator does not want to "build this bridge twice for free," so full-size holes demand tighter tolerance than sub-size holes that get reamed later [41:04]. A shop catch was a bearing stiffener whose incline had to be truly vertical only after all deflections; the shop welded it full-penetration in the correct skewed orientation rather than matching its tilted neighbors, and catching that in the shop is far cheaper than in the field [50:51]. On the Natchez repair, splice-plate templates were welded over the existing gussets with 300 A490 bolts through the existing steel, then the 1 in splice plates were drilled from those templates and beat to fit [14:15]. New eye bars were bored undersized then line-bored in place over about 30 hours to take a 10 in hole to 10 and 3/4 in, with a 24/7 fab shop turning pins to measured dimensions overnight [19:22][23:29].
### Erection Notes
The Natchez sequence is the model: tension the diagonal bypass first, then upper longitudinal restraints sized to a predicted temperature swing, weld splice-plate templates, install the splice plate as the 100 percent load path, add a top strut plate against locked-up lateral forces, install lower longitudinal restraints as a secondary bypass, then cut the pin (the contractor used a wire saw, about 1.5 hours per pin face) [12:44][16:16]. The bridge was fully closed 11 weeks rather than keeping one lane open, to remove live-load vibration during the cut [11:13]. Across the girder case studies the controlling moves are shoring towers (one released with nearly 20 in of deflection), pier brackets for deep-water spans, strand jacking pre-assembled segments off barges, and a shoring tower mounted on a ballasted barge where the crew pumped roughly 100,000 lb of water out so the tower, not the crane, took the girder [60:34][66:08][81:25]. A consistent lesson is detailed, picture-driven, phone-coordinated release procedures so the crane operator knows exactly when it is safe to lower load [69:46].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The original 1940 detail failed by an unintended mechanism: pins were never meant to rotate, so high bearing stress grooved the pins (the weaker metal) and oblonged the gusset holes, keeper-plate welds sheared, and a prior 1995 contractor failed four times to reset a pin that had effectively seized [02:33][17:49]. Hidden defects only visible after disassembly included an existing pin misaligned 3/16 in per foot and a U29 cantilever gusset buckled out about 2 in from an erection force locked in during the original 1940s closure, which forced added splice plates and a jump from about 200 to about 300 A490 bolts for the eccentricity [22:27][24:31][26:02]. The named failure example is a Tennessee bridge that collapsed over 20 years ago after a cross frame was removed to fix a fit-up problem, increasing the unbraced length [58:01]. Common fit-up trouble on curved girders comes from differential deflection (1.25 in versus 2 in between adjacent girders) when cross frames are hung before the next girder lands, especially erecting inside-out [73:47].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Owners (MDOT plus Louisiana DOT) deliberately bypassed pure low-bid, using an RFQ to shortlist qualified specialty contractors so an unqualified bidder could not win the work, and planned to similarly prequalify painters to avoid finger-pointing [10:43][29:39]. Life-cycle economics drove the whole Natchez program: extending a bridge 75 years instead of replacing a Mississippi River crossing the state cannot afford, with the next phase bundling six remaining pin-and-link replacements into a 9-month painting closure [21:55][29:09]. The fabricator's cost message is that full assembly is expensive and usually unnecessary given modern metrology, and that the operationally efficient design (fewer bolts per partial splice, faster crew release) is what a good contractor actually pays for because crane and ironworker time is the number-one cost [34:56][59:33]. Catching detail errors in the shop versus the field is repeatedly framed as the big cost lever [51:21].
### Your Company Application
For Ivan's takeoff, the durable lesson is that bolt counts and splice plate sizes can grow once eccentricity and field reality are known (200 to 300 A490 here), so estimate connection material with margin and flag any partially-supported or staged-erection scope as an engineered line, not a unit rate. For Tekla detailing and the in-house PE stamp, the metrology and "validate with a second method" discipline maps directly: drive camber and geometry from one single-source model, hand-check the model with an independent calc, and remember that ironworkers want a stamp before they lower a crane [42:36][70:46]. For Mario's shop crew, the full-penetration stiffener fix and the "fix it in the shop, not the field" rule reinforce that a flagged fit-up issue is cheaper welded right at the bench. For Paul's safety program, reaming and large drill-bit bind-up are named as bridge-fab hazards and partially-erected stability is the dominant risk, both worth a toolbox-talk analogue even on building steel. Anchor-rod relevance is indirect (pin alignment and line-boring tolerance echo our anchor-tolerance discipline).
