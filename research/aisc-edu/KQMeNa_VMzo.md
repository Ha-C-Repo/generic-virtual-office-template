## Corrosion Protection Strategies for Bridges
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KQMeNa_VMzo | Duration: 1:35:33 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This is an AASHTO/NSBA bridge workshop with three presenters (Jeff Carlson of NSBA, Dr. Jennifer McConnell of the University of Delaware, and Ronnie Medlock of High Steel) covering the four corrosion-protection systems for steel bridges: paint, weathering steel, hot-dip galvanizing, and thermal-spray metallizing. The unifying idea is that corrosion protection is almost entirely about getting zinc onto the steel, the only exception being weathering steel which relies on a self-limiting patina [02:03]. Environment severity drives the choice: marine/salt is most severe, deicing-salt regions are moderate, and arid/rural is mild, with measured loss rates from 0.26 mils/year in Phoenix to 28 at Kure Beach NC [03:34]. The talk is bridge-DOT focused, not building structural steel, so AISC member-design content is absent.

### Spec References
The cited standards are bridge and coating standards, not AISC structural codes: FHWA Steel Bridge Design Handbook corrosion section [03:04][21:29]; AASHTO/NSBA Collaboration guide spec S8.1 for application of zinc-rich primers to steel bridges [12:17]; the new AASHTO/NSBA metallizing guide spec on the AASHTO SCOBS ballot for the June Spokane meeting, written as a supplement to SSPC/AWS/NACE CS 23 [22:00][24:33][49:00]; ISO 2063 as the international metallizing basis [24:03]; AASHTO NEPCOAT qualified-coating menu (ABC list, C = field) [08:42][66:04]; AASHTO 2013 Manual for Bridge Element Inspection condition-state scale 1 to 4 [43:23]; the FHWA 1989 Technical Advisory on uncoated weathering steel [35:43][57:52]; SSPC SP6 surface prep cited in an Iowa DOT spec [14:22]; ASTM A1010 high-chrome (about 11 percent) stainless bridge steel and ASTM A709 Grade 50W weathering steel [20:59][77:28]. ### Technical Details
Three-coat paint is an organic or inorganic zinc-rich primer, an epoxy moisture-barrier midcoat, and a polyurethane UV topcoat, with organic zinc preferred for fast cure (hours versus up to 24 hours or even a 3-month hold for inorganic, which needs ambient moisture to cure) [07:41][09:12][80:30]. Two-coat alternatives are a polyaspartic urethane over zinc (Sherwin-Williams Envirolastic 980 named, NEPCOAT approved) and a polysiloxane topcoat [09:43][82:34]. Metallizing is zinc/aluminum wire arc-sprayed onto cold steel (no preheat, no tank distortion), with material options of 99 percent zinc, 85/15 zinc-aluminum, or 99 percent aluminum, building 2 to 3 mils per pass to specs commonly 8 to 15 mils [18:56][26:05][76:58]. McConnell's research quantified weathering-steel performance using packing-tape particle samples scanned and analyzed by image recognition, correlating large-particle percentage to condition state, with worst corrosion on the top of the bottom flange of the first interior fascia girder [46:59][49:33].

### Fabrication Notes
The recurring fabricator message is that surface preparation governs every coated system: paint and metallizing both depend on cleanliness and an angular surface profile, and metallizing specifically requires hand blasting because the automated wheel blasters fabricators use for paint cannot produce the deep angular anchor pattern metallizing needs [19:26][76:27]. Metallizing is dusty and slow, demands dust collection, and is verified with pull-off adhesion tabs whose required pullout values and anchor patterns vary by state spec [25:34][85:37]. Carlson recommends applying all three paint coats in the shop for environmental control, accepting field touch-up of handling damage [06:39]. For weathering steel, run the steel through three wet-dry cycles in the yard (a hose, low skill) before shipping so the patina starts uniformly and most staining bleeds out before the steel reaches the piers [14:22]. Galvanizing thin-web large-flange plate girders risks distortion, and double-dipping long girders adds major cost [17:55][40:00].

### Erection Notes
Field-side content is limited. Metallizing can be done in the field by a third-party metallizer to convert an existing painted bridge, with the same surface-profile and thickness requirements as shop work [31:09][28:06]. The dominant field/detailing lesson is that leaking expansion joints are the single biggest cause of weathering-steel corrosion: 23 of 70 cluster-study bridges had worst corrosion at the joint, and water then travels longitudinally down the girder, so joint maintenance is the cheap fix for an expensive problem [58:22][59:24][61:58]. Detailing for water shedding matters, with wider overhangs acting as a roof and arch/frame geometries needing a planned drainage path to avoid ponding at bearings [60:26][61:27].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named failure modes: pre-1970 paint failures from ignoring surface prep, which created the lasting bad reputation of paint [05:37]; weathering-steel staining/bleeding onto piers and abutments and non-uniform patina in arid climates (Arizona, West Texas, Southern California) [13:20]; patina washing off in high-rainfall rural settings and streaking glass and walkways [62:28]; galvanizing distortion of flimsy plate girders [18:26]; metallizing inability to coat behind copes, crevices, and double-angle cross frames, where Carlson and Medlock both recommend hot-dip galvanizing the cross frames instead and Medlock recommends an RFI when a job calls for metallized frames [27:36][86:37][87:39]. Inspection ambiguity is itself a failure point: steel that fits none of the four condition states cleanly, which the tape-sample method was developed to resolve [46:28].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Medlock gave percent cost increases over a base weathering-steel grade-separation bridge: three-coat paint adds 24 percent, galvanizing 41 percent, metallizing 42 percent (galvanized cross frames assumed), and A1010 stainless 130 percent [74:54][84:04][90:42]. Adders: two-coat paint 19 percent, end paint 8 percent, end-plus-fascia paint 10 percent, paint-over-weathering 27 percent, galvanize-plus-fascia-paint 54 percent, metallize-plus-seal about 49 percent, metallized cross frames 54 percent [78:28][83:34][84:04]. On a more complicated bridge the metallize-and-galvanize premium dropped to 18 percent, so coating premium shrinks as base fabrication complexity rises [90:10]. Cure-wait drives schedule and shop capacity: inorganic zinc with a mandated 3-month cure forces girders outside and can make a job non-biddable on a tight schedule [80:30][81:31]. The key cost drivers a fabricator prices are cleaning, masking, coating application, and especially handling/girder moves [71:49].
