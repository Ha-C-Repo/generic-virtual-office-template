## Erection Engineering of High-Rise Buildings
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Gq1bmrn22Q8 | Duration: 1:33:34 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Timothy Nelson of Degenkolb Engineers covers erection engineering for steel high-rise structures, the temporary-condition analysis the erector and its engineer own before the permanent lateral system is fully in place [04:09]. The thesis is that as buildings go all-steel (no concrete core), use delayed mega-brace lateral systems, and push height and speed, more responsibility shifts from the structural engineer of record to the erector and its engineer for the in-between construction stages [06:42][77:46]. He organizes the talk into five topics: wind and seismic demands during construction, structural complexity and erection tolerances, pushing height limits with delayed lateral systems, erection aids and lifting lugs, and tower-crane support [07:13].

### Technical Details
The trust-tower wind analogy treats an unclad partial frame as exposed surface area on beams, columns, and braces; an unclad frame can pick up more wind than the clad finished building, and the audience poll confirmed wind base shear is "sometimes" higher during construction [09:47][81:18][82:21]. For a 60-story all-steel case study (13 to 15 foot stories, 250 by 80 foot plate), averaging five shielding standards gave about 70% of the ASCE 37 unshielded baseline north-south and 81% east-west, supporting roughly a 25% shielding reduction [15:31][22:47][23:48]. Seismic example for Degenkolb's San Francisco office: Ss 1.5g reduced to 0.30 (80%), Fa 1.0 on unreduced Ss, two-thirds of Sms, then R = 2.5 yields a coefficient near 0.08, low enough that judgment may override it back up to about 0.2 [29:32][30:04]. The R = 2.5 reflects that a partially connected ductile system (BRB, brace frames with collectors and diaphragm) may fracture before developing its full R = 6 to 8 ductility [27:26][27:56].

### Fabrication Notes
Box columns are increasingly common and very heavy, on the order of 50-plus tons over a few stories once gusset plates, stiffeners, and wall thickness are counted [54:03]. A complete-joint-penetration splice on a box column of 3 to 4 foot plan dimension can take days to over a week to weld off [54:35]. Lifting-lug demand requires an attentive unfactored weight takeoff capturing all supplemental plates and any consolidation rods or plates inside a concrete-filled box [61:20][62:23]. Erection-aid gravity load is carried in bearing through the PJP root face or by oversized backing bars placed above and below so they bear on each other, keeping gravity out of the bolted aid [58:46][59:18]. Bare metal-deck diaphragm capacity is governed by support attachments (welded stud, spot weld, powder-actuated fastener, screw) and side-lap connections (seam weld, button punch), with side-lap spacing of 24 versus 12 inches swinging total capacity significantly [48:50][49:21][51:57].

### Erection Notes
On 181 Fremont (800 feet, all steel, three-tier external mega-brace plus perimeter secondary moment frame) the erector measured building lean from differential column elastic shortening and building twist as the mega-braces engaged, and set certain columns intentionally out of plumb to land within the AISC Code of Standard Practice global envelope of 3 inches in and 2 inches out at the top [31:05][32:38][33:41][34:12][35:14]. A staged "dashboard" spreadsheet interpolated column XYZ positions at each level across three erection phases versus theoretical, shared with the GC; SAP2000 staged-construction was an alternative [34:43][35:44]. Mega-braces slid through intermediate floors (braced length only, no lateral transfer except at top and bottom nodes), leaving about 20 stories erected without the primary lateral system [42:30][43:33]. Temporary lateral support sources: secondary moment frames (with corner bottom flanges left unwelded to avoid Vierendeel action), gravity-connection partial fixity, slotted-hole brace connections that allow shortening slip before being tightened slip-critical or welded, and early welded-down deck diaphragms [44:06][45:06][47:10][48:14]. Heavy stick-built transfer trusses were false-worked off the perimeter excavation shoring (cutter-soil-mix wall with steel soldier beams at 4 feet on center) using sloped temporary columns to avoid touch points in the slab [37:19][38:21][38:51].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Crane-supplier tie-in demands are frequently undocumented as to ASD versus LRFD level, often generated from overseas codes, and have not always tracked the ASCE wind transition, so the speaker assumes ASD when unsure [68:03][68:34][69:04]. In one case the manufacturer-supplied tie-shoe holes could not pass even the largest bolts under the supplied demand, resolved with very high-strength bolts rather than reaming [70:38][71:08]. A box tube at a tower-crane tie-in was under-stiffened at the top and made audible noise as it distorted, alarming the site and requiring an OSHA conversation [75:11][75:42]. Wind-tacking deck is called out as inadequate to develop deck strength; an expedited proper weld-down is required [51:26]. Lifting-lug checks 5 and 6 (welds to the box wall and localized box-wall effects) are where problems most often arise, especially with thin walls that cannot develop the lug welds [64:58][65:29].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Heavier gauge deck or tighter connection spacing to gain temporary diaphragm strength carries a cost the erector must weigh, and the deck gauge is often left to the contractor rather than fixed on the structural drawings, so it must be confirmed early [51:57][52:27][50:23]. Knowing whether the decker works for the steel erector or the GC changes the schedule dynamics for how fast deck gets laid and welded after erection [50:54]. The load-spreading crane scheme demanded deck welded down across many floors right at the leading edge, a "huge obstacle" that had to be explained and bought into early [73:09][73:40]. Multi-day to multi-week CJP box-column splices keep elements on engineered erection aids far longer than a couple hours, raising the engineering and temporary-steel cost [54:35][55:05]. Lifting lugs use a factor of safety of about 5 (between ASME below-the-hook categories B and C) on nominal steel capacity given single-point lifts over streets and workers [62:23][63:25].
