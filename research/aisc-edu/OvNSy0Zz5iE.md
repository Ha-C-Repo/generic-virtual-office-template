## Design of Lateral Load Resisting Frames Using Steel Joists and Joist Girders
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OvNSy0Zz5iE | Duration: 52:13 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
Dr. James Fisher (SJI consulting engineer) presents the SJI Technical Digest on designing single-story moment-resisting frames where steel joists and joist girders carry lateral wind and seismic load. The central idea is that joists, joist girders, and roof trusses are far stiffer and stronger than the columns, so the usual strong-column weak-beam rule cannot be met; instead these frames run strong-beam weak-column, which the code permits only for one-story buildings [05:09][08:42][41:55]. In seismic terms the system is an Ordinary Moment Frame (OMF) with R = 3.5, and the column is the designated fuse that develops its plastic moment Mp while the joist girder is kept fully elastic [10:48][19:36][40:00]. Fisher repeatedly stresses that a moment frame is the least cost-effective lateral system and should be the last choice after a roof-diaphragm-plus-wall-bracing scheme, which only fails when the length-to-width ratio exceeds roughly 4 to 1 [08:07].

### Spec References
On-slide and spoken citations captured: 2005 AISC Specification (adopted by 2006 IBC) requiring second-order analysis on all steel structures [12:37]; 2005 AISC Seismic Provisions Section 5.1 for engineer-of-record drawing obligations (designate the seismic load resisting system, members and connections, connection configuration, connection material specs and sizes, locations of demand-critical welds, locations and dimensions of protected zones, welding per Appendix W Section W2.1) [39:03, frame 32]; AISC Seismic Provisions Section 7, with Section 7.3 Welded Joints and Section 7.3a General Requirements, governing chord splices and joist-to-column connections for SDC D/E/F and when R>3 in SDC B/C [40:30, frame 33]; chord splice requirement stated verbally as "appendix W or D1 point eight" (AISC Seismic Appendix W / AWS D1.8) [40:32]; bracing per the 2005 AISC Specification Appendix 6 ("appendix six") for joist chord lateral bracing [42:54]; ASCE 7-05 for wind and seismic loads [04:21][51:06]; IBC 2006 minimum roof slope of 1/4 in 12 [45:12]. The system R-value is OMF R = 3.5, with R = 3 recommended in SDC B and C to avoid special inspection and special connection requirements [10:48][41:00]. A forthcoming AISC Design Guide on second-order effects is mentioned by topic, no number given [13:21].

### Technical Details
The frame is modeled two ways. Model 1 takes the structure to mid-height of the joist or joist girder as a prismatic member with an equivalent moment of inertia and area for drift and initial sizing [14:46]; trial joist-girder stiffness comes from the SJI catalog equations (n panel points, P panel-point load, L length, D depth) with the moment of inertia reduced about 15 percent for web distortion [15:36]. Model 2 is the seismic fuse model: each column develops Mp, the connection demand is 1.1 x Ry x Mp extrapolated from bottom chord up to mid-height, applied to a continuous-beam model so end-span girders take the full moment and the center girder splits roughly half-Mp each way [19:36][20:54]. Fisher emphasizes leaning columns and P-Delta: every leaning column needs a brace, brace forces accumulate, and a 2D model uses a pseudo-column carrying the interior gravity and heavy-wall seismic loads [22:54][25:25]. Tilt-up and precast walls drive seismic forces up sharply, pushing designers toward lighter wall systems [26:09]. Worked seismic criteria from frame 27: R = 3.5 for OMF, SDS = 0.9297g, SD1 = 0.39g, rho = 1.0, QE = 49 kips, with Imin = 6790 in^4 exterior girders and 4570 in^4 interior girder, and minimum top-chord width 7.0 in for weld access.

### Fabrication Notes
The joist manufacturer designs the joists and joist girders in conformance with SJI specifications once the engineer of record supplies loads, load combinations (each combination separately because second-order analysis is nonlinear), stiffness data, minimum moment of inertia, and minimum top-chord width [13:53][32:38]. SJI has taken the position that it will design the entire girder, not just the connection, for 1.1 Ry Mp to guarantee the girder stays elastic [40:00]. A specified minimum 7.0 in top-chord width is a fabrication requirement so a downhand fillet weld of the connection plate is possible [34:31]. If the manufacturer returns a moment of inertia larger than the specified minimum, the columns still see no more than Mp, so the analysis usually needs no rerun; among friends, hold the delivered I to within about 10 percent of the specified value [33:00][36:00]. Web members are designed per SJI specifications, and chord splices meet AISC Seismic Section 7 / Appendix W [40:30].

### Erection Notes
The frames erect with no more difficulty than wide-flange beam-and-column systems; the one added requirement is heavy communication and information exchange between the engineer of record and the joist supplier [07:03]. The basic connection (frame 20) sets the joist girder on top of the column with a stabilizer plate, joist bolted to girder using (2) 3/4 in dia A325 bolts, with a 3/4 in stabilizer plate and an explicit "DO NOT WELD" note on the secondary seat. Bases are generally taken as pinned for one-story frames, occasionally fixed to control drift, though fixed exterior bases on piers cause moment-development problems [11:24]. Roof slope and ponding are handled with tapered insulation or camber plus crickets at drains to meet the 1/4 in 12 minimum [45:12].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The named failure modes are conceptual rather than weld-defect photos. Using the standard SJI seat connection in a moment frame is a mistake because it cannot develop meaningful moment capacity and is not recommended for these frames [27:00]. An eccentric force P at the seat creates a P x e moment the two-angle top chord cannot carry, so the eccentricity must be eliminated by welding a plate from the column cap to the top chord [28:17][30:28]. Slender chord elements (width-thickness near the slender limit) cannot reach yield in compression, so inelastic behavior cannot be relied on; the answer is to keep the girder elastic and let the column fuse [41:24]. Specifying R = 3.5 in SDC B or C needlessly triggers special inspection and special connection requirements, so R = 3 is recommended there [41:00]. Pin-base assumptions are acknowledged as rougher than the analysis precision being chased [36:37].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Seismic criteria dominate cost on identical buildings. In the worked comparison (frame 42), Charleston SC (SDC D, R=3.5) gave a seismic base shear of 49.0 kips per frame line versus a wind base shear of 22.9 kips per frame line, while the same building in Jackson MS (SDC B, R=3.0) gave only 14.3 kips seismic [48:01][50:02]. The Charleston seismic moments were nearly three times larger, forcing the interior columns into the moment frames, larger columns, more connections, and bigger girders: exterior columns W18x86, interior W18x97, total column weight 12,000 lb and girder weight 6,300 lb for about 18,500 lb per bay, versus roughly 12,000 lb per bay for the low-seismic case [49:02][50:16]. The estimating takeaway is that seismic design category and R-value, not span, set the steel tonnage on these frames, and a moment frame is the most expensive lateral choice to begin with.
