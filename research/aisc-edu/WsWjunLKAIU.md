### Core Concept
This is the opening session of AISC Night School "Fundamentals of Stability for Steel Design," presented by Ron Ziemian of Bucknell University for the Structural Stability Research Council, covering course introduction and the behavior of compression members [00:01]. The lecture builds the AISC column strength curve from first principles, starting with the 1744 Euler buckling solution and then progressively undoing each of Euler's idealizing assumptions: prismatic member, small post-buckling deflection, no bending before bifurcation, linear-elastic material, and pinned-pinned ends [11:24][24:11]. It distinguishes bifurcation (theoretical critical load) from instability (buckling preceded by growing deflection), and shows that initial out-of-straightness, partial yielding, and residual stresses each pull real column strength below the elastic Euler stress [04:07][45:14].

### Technical Details
Euler buckling load is n-squared times pi-squared EI over L-squared, controlled by the first mode, giving Euler stress of pi-squared E over (L/r)-squared once divided by area using radius of gyration [18:33][20:37]. Adding a sine-shaped initial out-of-straightness of about L/1000 produces a midspan amplification factor of 1/(1 - P/P_Euler), so deflections grow unbounded as load approaches the Euler load [29:51][31:26]. Residual stresses, on the order of 30 to 50 percent of yield in heavy jumbo shapes, arise from uneven cooling: flange tips cool first and end in compression while the web-flange junction ends in tension, and these compressive flange tips yield early and cut effective EI [50:20][54:02][62:13]. Minor-axis strength is consistently below major-axis strength because losing the flange tips to yielding hurts weak-axis stiffness far more [62:43]. K factors of 0.5, 0.7, 1.0, and 2.0 follow from the differential-equation solution and can be estimated from the spacing of inflection points on the buckled shape [75:29][76:00].
### Fabrication Notes
The most fabrication-relevant content is the residual-stress discussion. Built-in stresses come from uneven cooling after rolling, from welding built-up shapes (where the welded-shape residual pattern is more severe than rolled shapes), and from cold bending [50:20][54:02]. An audience exchange clarifies that mechanical straightening of a column (cold cambering or gagging past yield) often reduces rather than adds residual stress because it takes the section past yield and relaxes it, though cold-forming can introduce its own residual stresses [65:48][66:19]. No shop tolerance numbers, fit-up steps, or QC procedures are given; this is theory, not shop practice.

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
No inspection or rejection criteria are named. The implied design errors are conceptual: treating a real column as perfectly straight, ignoring residual stresses (which the speaker warns makes a finite-element column analysis unconservative), and misjudging the K factor when end restraint is uncertain [83:41][81:08]. He notes a single AISC curve cannot capture every shape, so it slightly over-predicts minor-axis strength and under-predicts major-axis strength relative to finite-element results, a modeling limitation rather than a field failure [70:25].
