## Fastener Fundamentals

### Core Concept
This is a fastener-engineering deep dive on structural bolting from a bolt-manufacturer's perspective: how high-strength bolts are made, what threads and coatings actually do to performance, how torque, tension, and angle relate, and what goes wrong on real jobsites. The unifying idea is the torque-tension-angle curve [35:00, 64:00]: torque and tension are linear only in the elastic range, and over 50 variables (surface roughness, temperature, install rate, helix angle, stiffness, humidity) move that relationship [61:00]. Because structural bolts are intentionally tensioned well into yield, a practice the speaker calls a "no-no in every other engineered fastener application," our industry designs many of a good fastener's best traits (long threads, matched sets, controlled K factor) out of the product to meet code, which is why field problems concentrate in large-diameter, high-strength, coated bolts [73:00, 90:55].

### Spec References
RCSC Specification, with the snug-tight definition quoted verbatim from both the 2000-2004 revision ("the tightness that is attained with a few impacts of an impact wrench or the full effort of an ironworker using an ordinary spud wrench to bring the plies into firm contact") and the 2009 revision ("the condition that exists when all of the plies in a connection have been pulled into firm contact ... and all of the bolts ... tightened sufficiently to prevent the removal of the nuts without the use of a wrench") [92:00]. RCSC's four approved pretensioning methods: turn-of-nut, calibrated wrench, DTI (direct tension indicator) washers, and twist-off (tension-control) bolts [70:00-72:00]. RCSC rule that a fastener altered from its tested condition (e.g. rust) must be re-qualified [82:17], the 1-in-20 ply slope limit before a bevel washer is required [90:33], and the RCSC determination that a shear plane in the thread transition zone may be treated as full body [84:30]. ASTM bolt specs by number from the slides: A325 / A325M and F1852 (Group A), A490 / A490M and F2280 (Group B), and A354 Grade BD, with A354 quoted directly that pretensioned use above 50% of tensile strength should carefully consider the head, tensile, nut, washer, test, and inspection requirements of A490 [75:00]. Coating specs cited on the Group-A/B matrix: B695 zinc mechanically deposited (Group A only), F2329 zinc hot dip (Group A except twist-off), F1136 zinc/aluminum (Group A or B except twist-off), F2833 zinc-rich base with aluminum organic/inorganic (Group A or B except twist-off), and F1941 electrodeposited (Group A hex only) [77:00]. AISC manual and AISC minimum design tension are referenced; Dacromet and Geomet named as the proprietary processes the F1136 spec was written around. The AWS D1.1 welding code is not discussed in this video.

### Technical Details
Group A bolts run 120 ksi tensile, Group B run 150 ksi [72:00]. Group A is medium-carbon, oil-quenched (moderate quench) with low quench-crack risk and better ductility; Group B is the higher-risk family, and the speaker has seen more quench cracks there, especially as A490 began getting coated [89:00]. Thread terms shown: root, flank, crest, pitch, major and minor diameter, 60-degree thread angle, with the internal/external thread geometry (H/8, 3H/8, 5H/8, H/4, P/2, P/4) [43:00, 46:00]. K-factor reference values: K=0.20 for as-received bolts and nuts with no supplemental lube (highly variable), K=0.10 to 0.17 with wax or other lubricant, and K=0.28 for HDG-coated bolts with no lubricant [61:00]. Turn-of-nut works because it pushes the bolt up onto the plastic plateau where tension varies little even as degrees change a lot; without lubrication a 180-degree install spec plus the allowed +60-degree tolerance can get close to the fracture point [69:00-70:00]. Snug tension is highly variable, ranging from roughly 10 to 300 degrees of rotation; even a spud wrench at ~60 degrees can develop 20,000 to 25,000 lb on a 7/8-in bolt [68:00].

### Fabrication Notes
Bolts are made by cold forming or hot forming; the hot-form progression is steel bars, hot-form nut blank, descale (sometimes), quench-and-temper heat treat, tapping, plating/coating if required, lubrication if required, inspection, packaging [24:00, 31:00]. Large-diameter and long parts are the hard cases: harder to heat-treat uniformly, prone to die fins and body swell that affect hole size, and hard to keep straight through many thick plies [37:00]. Most stress sits on the first few nut threads nearest the grip, and many quality problems trace to poor heat treatment or thread quality and fit [37:00]. For coatings the nut threads must be oversized for clearance, which raises the nut minor diameter, lowers proof-load capacity, and can shift the failure mode from bolt tensile to thread stripping; bolt-tensile and nut-proof-load tests are run on fixtures and do not correlate to assembled-fastener behavior [80:00]. The speaker's own QC caught a quench crack in a coated A490 on receipt inspection and scrapped the entire lot [78:00]. He stresses matched assemblies, controlled K factor, and not over-tensioning coated fasteners.

### Erection Notes
All four tensioning methods depend on first reaching a true snug-tight condition, which is the weak link because snug is so variable in the field [92:00]. Twist-off bolts give single-side install and shear the spline when the calibrated torque is reached; DTI washers measure preload by compression and come as gap-measuring or silicone "squirter" types, placed under the head or nut [71:00]. The strongest field recommendation is to move away from impacting (impact-wrench) tools toward non-impacting, non-reacting electric or pneumatic tools: they are quieter, preserve coating integrity, and prevent driving coating off corners and threads, which can wreck an otherwise good install [66:00, 67:00]. A practical failure shown was a bolt that slipped into bearing and bound in the steel so badly the crew could not turn it to develop tension, fixed by attention to hole size; another was installing from the head side, which twists off bolt heads from body friction in the hole [79:00, 80:00]. Match-marking, witnessing, and sign-off can still pass a bolt that has already partially stripped its first thread and a half on slick coated fasteners [81:00].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named defects and rejections: quench cracks (described as potentially horrible, running through the thread root, and rising with coated A490) versus seams and bursts (usually cosmetic forging defects with ASTM permissible limits) that often cause unjustified project shutdowns [76:00-78:00, 123]. Also: improperly mated components and missing engagement reported as thread stripping (counterfeit or defective material that is really just too little engagement), head bursts, fatigue, torsional and tension failures, lack of installer training, and head twist-off from head-side install [74:00-80:00]. Mixing inch-series washers onto metric fasteners cracked the washers because the under-head radius forced them apart [79:44]. Over-tension is now common on slick coated fasteners and has produced limited stress-corrosion / delayed-fracture cases in coated A490 from micro stress cracks in the thread roots [81:00]. Improper storage (rust-through covers, water-filled buckets) and improper handling (bolts dumped on the ground, lots mixed) appear as field failures [85:00]. On rust, RCSC requires re-qualification of any altered fastener; the speaker won't put a number on acceptable rust [82:00].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The opening thesis is explicitly commercial: "the difference between panic and understanding is slight, but can mean thousands of dollars," and informed bolt decisions cut downstream problems and project compromise [15:00]. Quench cracks force scrapping and segregating whole lots, and burst/seam panic shuts down projects for cosmetic defects, both of which are avoidable schedule and cost hits with the right receipt-inspection knowledge [76:00, 78:00]. The Group-A versus Group-B trade is a sourcing decision: Group A buys availability, ductility, easier coatings, and "less risk" while Group B buys strength, and the 80/20 rule is that 80% of problems land in the 20% of bolts that are large-diameter, high-strength, and coated, so the speaker and the engineer should both get nervous about a 4140 2-in high-hardness coated bolt [72:00, 90:55]. Specifying A354BD to reach sizes above A490's 1.5-in cap means calling out heavy-hex dimensions and invoking A490 test requirements (mag particle, carb/decarb, max tensile) with the supplier, which is a procurement and QC scope item [75:00, 88:00].

Anchor rods and in-house PE stamp: the A325/A490/A354BD grade logic, the 1-in-20 bevel-washer rule, and the A354 "invoke A490 testing" guidance map onto anchor-rod and large-diameter connection callouts our PE produces and onto setting tolerance and hole-size coordination. Ivan's takeoff: bolt grade, coating spec, and matched-assembly requirements are real cost drivers, so bolt and coating selection (Group A default, Group B only when strength demands it) should be a flagged line in estimating rather than a silent assumption, consistent with verify-don't-generate. Receipt inspection at the shop should screen for quench cracks on coated Group-B lots and not panic-reject cosmetic bursts/seams, a concrete QC routine for Mario and our incoming-material check.
