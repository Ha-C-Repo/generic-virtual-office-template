### Core Concept
This Lawrence Kloiber (Clober) session covers field fixes for column-erection problems and the economics and rules of beam camber. The first half walks through column-splice gaps, columns set too short or too long, and out-of-plumb columns, with the recurring theme that the erector must give the fabricator and engineer of record early notice the moment something hangs up on fit-up [05:41][06:13]. The second half is a detailed treatment of when to camber, when not to, and how camber is physically produced and checked.
### Spec References
The speaker cites the AISC splice-gap rule by value: a gap of one sixteenth inch or less needs no action, and a gap over one sixteenth up to roughly a quarter inch may be shimmed with flat mild-steel shims [01:34][02:06]. He cites the AISC column out-of-plumb tolerance of L/500 from the AISC Code of Standard Practice [12:21][12:51], and returns to the Code of Standard Practice for the rule that camber shall be checked in the shop in the unstressed condition [29:45]. He cites AWS D1.1 for moment-weld buildout, prequalified joint configurations, root-dimension limits, and the EOR-accepted larger correction with a developed procedure [14:22][16:24][17:25]. He references an American Iron and Steel Engineers recommendation for cambering crane girders over 75 feet [20:30]. No table numbers or Design Guide numbers were stated.
### Technical Details
Column splices should sit at least 48 inches above top of concrete so a shop-punched hole can serve as both the lifting hole (pin and shackle, lanyard to pull the pin from the ground) and the OSHA perimeter safety-cable hole at 42 inches above floor [03:39][04:09][04:39]. Splice gaps arise from band-saw drift on heavy columns, slight out-of-square ends, or joints opening during plumbing [05:10]. Out-of-plumb causes to diagnose first are misplaced anchor rods or mis-detailed framing beams [11:50][12:21]; a column is a compression member and tolerates more than L/500 if braced for the eccentricity and there is no cladding or fit-up conflict [12:51]. Welded CJP moment joints shrink roughly an eighth inch each; AWS requires a buildout pass when the root exceeds about three eighths inch so a single pass does not crack [14:22][16:54].
### Fabrication Notes
Fabricators prefer bolted-bolted splice details made on CNC punches and drill lines because holes are cheap and a loose bolted upper shaft adjusts more easily on fit-up [02:37][03:08]. Base-plate elevations should be uniform with exceptions clearly flagged, and top-of-column elevations and roof slopes clearly shown, with shop QC verifying column dimensions [07:14][07:44]. Camber is produced in a mechanical press with reaction points about 25 feet apart and rams about 6 to 8 feet apart that push the beam past yield to a permanent set, so beams under 25 feet will not fit the machine [23:40][24:11]. Members are sawn, squared, and punched uncambered, then cambered last, which leaves an end slope; webs of a quarter inch or less can buckle when forced to yield, and heat cambering costs four to five times mechanical cambering [24:41][25:11][25:41].
### Erection Notes
The dominant erection lesson is timely notice: examples include 50 columns decked and ready for concrete before a splice gap was reported [05:41], a brace bent poured into concrete with diagonals that would not fit because one column was an inch low [10:18], and a 165-foot welded moment line drawn 2.5 inches short because the crew did not compensate for weld shrinkage [15:23][16:24]. The fix posture is to plumb the center column and lean columns outward as welds shrink, or to plan shrinkage if starting from one end [17:25]. Big-box joist and joist-girder jobs with slotted end connections can run several inches out of plumb across five or six bays, so OSHA requires bracing and plumbing every couple of days as erection proceeds [13:22][13:52].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named failures are column splices hanging up on splice plates with unreported gaps [06:13], columns erected an inch too short or too long [06:43][11:19], six columns set an inch low from a base-plate grout-thickness detail error [09:48], out-of-plumb beyond L/500 [12:21], welded and end-plated beam lines running too short from uncompensated weld shrinkage [14:22][16:24], and AWS root dimensions exceeded leading to single-pass cracking [16:54]. Detailing too-light beams from RAM-Steel output and then cambering them is flagged as a buckling failure [25:11].
### Bid / Cost Implications
End-plated and welded moment connections should be detailed short (an eighth inch each end on end plates) so finger shims absorb error, because a too-long member goes back to the shop for a cut-off and new end plate [18:26][18:58]. Camber only what pays: skip camber under 1 inch, under 25 feet, and on stiff girders loaded at third points, and camber filler beams rather than girders for real savings [25:41][26:11][26:41]. Heat cambering costs four to five times mechanical cambering, so upsizing a beam a size or two can be cheaper than a heat-camber requirement [24:41]. The cost of a field fix scales with bay count and end tolerance: a one- or two-bay job tolerates being slightly short or long, but multi-bay or tight-tolerance lines need designed-in adjustment [18:58].
