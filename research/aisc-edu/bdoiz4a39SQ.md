## Alternate Methods of Connection Design
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bdoiz4a39SQ | Duration: 1:28:34 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
This 2015 AISC webinar, by Larry Muir (AISC Steel Solutions Center) and Dr Patrick McManus (Martin and Martin), teaches connection-design methods that go beyond what is tabulated in the AISC Manual while still satisfying the AISC Specification [01:02]. Muir draws the line between the Specification (adopted into law by reference in the building codes, written as requirements) and the Manual (guidance only, not a contract requirement, so calling out "design shall conform to the AISC Manual" is improper) [02:36]. Part one covers shear-connection considerations beyond strength, mainly rotational ductility and torsional restraint [03:37]. Part two covers built-up PJP welds, bolt-group analysis by alternate methods, and prying action [37:26].

### Spec References
Specification Section B3.6a defines a simple shear connection and requires sufficient rotation capacity for the analysis-required rotation [05:38]. Section J1.2 permits inelastic but self-limiting deformation to accommodate simple-beam end rotation [06:09]. Section J2.2b limits weld returns to allow weak-axis flexure [07:11]. Chapter F (Section F1) carries the assumption that beam supports are restrained against rotation about the longitudinal axis [24:40]. Seismic Provisions Section F2.6b requires connections to accommodate 0.025 radians of rotation [11:20]. Chapter N requires UT on CJP welds with frequency by risk category [40:35]. Table J2.5 gives weld available stresses [45:20]. Specification Section J3.6 states prying action must be considered when computing bolt tension but does not dictate the method [72:38]. Manual Part 7 (instantaneous center of rotation and elastic bolt-group methods, C-prime, Delta-max of 0.34 in), Part 9 (weld and bolt minimum-size ductility checks), Part 10 (single-plate, shear-tab, and extended-tab design tables), and Part 2 (bearing-support stability) are cited throughout. Referenced studies: McGill University tests on welded shear tabs [21:38], Jim Swanson University of Cincinnati 2002 prying-action summary, and Bill Thornton's prying work that underlies the Manual method [73:10].

### Technical Details
The de-facto test target for simple shear connections is 0.03 radians, but Muir shows a serviceable beam almost never reaches it: 0.003 rad occurs near span = 36x depth, and a beam is usually unserviceable before then [14:57]. Rotation is accommodated by weak-axis flexing of angles, end plates, and WT elements, by bolt plowing (localized bearing), by hole elongation, and by support flexibility; the conventional shear tab gets a max plate-thickness limit and a weld sized at 5/8 of plate thickness so the bolts plow or the plate yields before the weld fractures [10:18]. For built-up PJP welds, a reinforcing fillet lengthens the effective throat: equal fillet and PJP gives roughly a 40 percent (square root of 2) gain, and maximizing the PJP in thick steel with a smaller reinforcing fillet drives the throat to the hypotenuse [43:43]. PJP welds get only a small directionality increase (phi 0.75 to 0.80, about 6 percent) versus 50 percent for fillets [47:53]. Prying action resolves into three limit states (flange hinging, combined mode, bolt tension) selected by the alpha term; the Manual uses Fu not Fy because weak-axis bending and strain hardening reliably reach above-yield strengths [74:42].

### Fabrication Notes
CJP welds are expensive: they need backer bars or back-gouging and rewelding, often weld access holes, and Chapter N ultrasonic testing keyed to risk category [40:03]. Built-up PJP welds avoid UT (Chapter N requires it on neither PJP nor fillet welds), usually avoid weld access holes and backing, and can be the cheaper detail despite needing more weld metal for the same capacity [41:06]. McManus's office tabulates, by plate thickness, double-sided fillet or single-sided built-up-PJP-plus-fillet combinations that match the grade-50 plate strength so the fabricator can choose the economical option in place of a CJP [50:55]. He notes the reinforcing fillet on the back side keeps the backer-bar pseudo-crack internal, which matters for fatigue or high-seismic where the backing would otherwise have to be removed [49:54]. The single-plate 5/8-thickness rule applies to both A36 and A572 grade 50 [52:58].

### Erection Notes
Beams that run continuous over columns can violate the Chapter F torsional-restraint assumption and the pinned-column assumption at once, so stabilizer plates are detailed at the support [31:20]. Beams on bearing plates need stability by building into concrete or masonry with anchorage, by a top-flange diaphragm connection, by a top stability angle, or by an end plate or transverse stiffeners, plus a tie-down to the bearing plate [30:50]. A diaphragm or deck stabilizes the top flange, but during erection that deck is not yet in place, so the erection sequence must keep the beam stable until the floor system is on [30:20]. A common field fix when bolt holes will not make up is to weld the shear tab to the beam web; stopping the horizontal welds short of the holes preserves a free plate length to hinge and accommodate rotation, a behavior the McGill tests support [20:36].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Long or double copes leave a thin web plate with little torsional restraint, and pairing a long double cope with a torsionally stiff double-angle connection is a worse detail than an extended tab because the weak cope section governs anyway [34:55]. Field changes that depart from the Part 10 table limits leave a connection that may fail the Part 9 ductility checks, forcing a search for other rotation sources [12:51]. CJP welds are abused as a default crutch to develop the base metal, carrying needless cost and the heavier Chapter N inspection burden [40:03]. One-sided welding of a built-up PJP without the back-side reinforcing fillet eccentrically loads the weld [49:23]. A failed-UT CJP being patched with a fillet and recalled a PJP is questionable because the inclusion's location makes the PJP effective throat uncertain [87:27].

### Bid / Cost Implications
CJP welds cost more in labor (backing, back-gouging, access holes) and add mandatory UT under Chapter N, so substituting a built-up PJP plus reinforcing fillet, or double-sided fillets, can cut weld cost while matching plate strength [50:55]. The trade is more deposited weld metal against a higher factor of safety with no UT, which is often net cheaper [41:39]. Alternate bolt-group methods (the separation method for drag and collector connections, and the interaction method) let an estimator tabulate a reliable shear-plus-axial capacity rather than re-running the iterative instantaneous-center method per condition, which is faster for repetitive industrial connections [57:37]. The separation method runs about 9 to 17 percent conservative versus the IC method, so it trades a small capacity penalty for predictable, codeable numbers [61:42].
