## Lean on Bracing for Steel I Shaped Girders
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ABoxRE5o9bs | Duration: 1:26:08 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This NSBA webinar by Jamie Ferris of TxDOT teaches lean-on bracing, a method of preventing lateral torsional buckling in steel I-girder bridges by letting several girders lean on one or two cross frames at each brace location through top and bottom struts, rather than placing a full cross frame in every bay [06:43][07:14]. The controlling failure mode is lateral torsional buckling, a lateral translation accompanied by twisting of the cross section, and the critical stage is deck placement when the bare steel must carry the entire construction load [02:35][03:05]. Cross frames and diaphragms act as torsional bracing because they control twist of the cross section, and a designed lean-on layout cuts the number of those braces while still meeting stability requirements [03:35][07:45]. The method derives from TxDOT research project 1772 at the University of Texas at Austin and applies to straight bridges with or without skew [10:23][07:14].

Background strength work cites Yura report 1239 "Bracing of Steel Beams and Bridges," 1992 [46:24].
### Technical Details
Total torsional system stiffness combines cross frame stiffness, cross-sectional (web distortion) stiffness, and in-plane girder stiffness as springs in series, so the total is always smaller than the smallest component, and required stiffness is twice the ideal stiffness divided by a 0.75 resistance factor for an LRFD-compliant value [30:42][32:12]. Web distortion is the dominant reduction term: the portion of web outside the brace depth can distort, an effective web width of N plus 1.5H is used with the web spreading at a 0.75-to-1 slope, and for slender built-up webs (h over tw greater than 58) the web is split above and below the brace and recombined in series [35:14][36:25][36:55]. A worked example with a 48-inch web showed the 24-inch lower region contributing 185,400 kip-in/rad versus 1,459,400 for the 12-inch upper region, proving distortion is non-linear with depth [40:07]. Brace strength uses an initial imperfection of Lb/500 times girder height, with brace moment equal to brace force times brace height equal to required stiffness times the imperfection [47:33][48:37]. The design example was a three-span continuous straight I-girder bridge, zero skew, 46 ft wide, five girders at 10 ft spacing, 92-inch web at 0.6875 inch, 24-inch flange at 1.5 inch [50:29][57:46][58:16].
### Fabrication Notes
Cross frames are called out as one of the more expensive parts of a steel structure, so cutting their count directly reduces shop fabrication cost [17:30][08:15]. The tension-only cross frame configuration uses four angles with one diagonal in compression and one in tension; the slender angles do not perform well in compression so designers neglect the compression member and design only for tension, and the same angle size is typically used for all struts and cross frame members to simplify procurement (areas A_d and A_c treated as equal A_b) [43:11][44:12]. Connection plates carry holes for fit-up bolts at each corner of the cross frame [85:10]. Fewer and right-sized braces also reduce fatigue-prone details, since oversized standard cross frames attract larger live load forces that drive fatigue cracking near brace locations, especially on skewed supports [08:47][09:18].
### Erection Notes
Lean-on bracing reduced fit-up problems and let contractors erect girders faster than typical plate girders, saving schedule [08:15][17:00]. The framing plan must be built around the actual girder erection sequence, ideally after talking to contractors about lifting order, and TxDOT lays full cross frame lines at bearing and at field splice locations to prevent twist while the bridge is partially installed [52:00][55:12]. When erection side is known the cross frame layout follows a natural S-shape; when either side may start it becomes a mirror image with more cross frames [55:42][81:34]. On the 19th Street underpass, heavy struts were tied together and lifted with the girder, then untied and attached to the adjacent girder by ironworkers once set, and girders were left at the second splice point for about 18 months for traffic control with no stability issue [21:04][22:34][17:00]. Conventional bracing would have needed 80 cross frames there versus 28 with lean-on [19:02]; the design example dropped 148 conventional cross frames to 80 [68:33].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The recurring field problem is brace fit-up difficulty, worst near skewed supports, which lean-on bracing mitigates by replacing hard-to-fit cross frames with struts [14:55][70:36]. Fatigue cracking found during routine maintenance inspections around cross frames and diaphragms is the named in-service failure, caused by large stress concentrations from cross frame forces under truck traffic and aggravated by skew and by oversized standard braces [08:47][09:18]. No weld rejections or bolt inspection failures are named; TxDOT field-welds cross frames after a fit-up erection bolt and reports good results because it has good field welders and steel inspectors [04:06][04:37].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The core economic argument is fewer cross frames, which lowers fabrication cost and erection cost, reduces fit-up labor and construction timeline, and simplifies future inspection and maintenance [08:15][73:09]. TxDOT explicitly endorses the method for the right straight-girder projects in its design manual chapter 3 section 14 because of fabrication and erection savings [72:07]. For heavily skewed bridges, a partial application that removes cross frames only in problematic areas (the Lake Conroe SH 105 example) captures savings without redesigning the whole bridge [69:04][70:36]. The savings scale per structure, so for a multi-line bridge the stiffness, strength, tension, compression, and bolt checks are run for each line of braces across the width [68:33].
