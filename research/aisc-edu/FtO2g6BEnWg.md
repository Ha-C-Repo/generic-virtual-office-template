## Designing Structural Stainless Steel - Part 2
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FtO2g6BEnWg | Duration: 1:32:22 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This webinar, presented by Nancy Baddoo of the Steel Construction Institute (UK), author of AISC Design Guide 27, covers the structural-engineering design rules for stainless steel members and connections [00:33][02:35]. The controlling idea is that stainless steel has a nonlinear (rounded) stress-strain curve with no sharply defined yield point and high strain hardening, unlike carbon steel which has a defined yield plateau [09:53]. Because of this nonlinearity, design uses the 0.2% offset yield strength: travel to 0.2% permanent strain, draw a line parallel to the initial elastic modulus, and read the stress where it intersects the curve [10:23]. The design approach mirrors AISC 360 carbon-steel methods but with modified coefficients, lower width-to-thickness limits, and lower buckling curves [36:08].

### Technical Details
Initial elastic modulus is 28,000 ksi for austenitic versus 29,000 ksi for carbon and duplex; density runs 485 to 500 lb/ft3 [12:29]. Austenitic 304/316 design strength is generally FY = 30 ksi; duplex runs 58 to 67 ksi; precipitation-hardening grades are very high strength for tension rods and fasteners [37:15][28:22]. Local-buckling limits are lower for stainless: unstiffened I-shape flanges 0.47 vs 0.56 root(E/FY), stiffened webs of W and channel sections 1.24 vs 1.49, round HSS 0.10 vs 0.11 [38:48]. Compression equation coefficient 4.71 is reduced to 3.77 [42:34]. Deflection uses the secant modulus from a Ramberg-Osgood model (n around 5 for austenitic, 7 to 8 for duplex); at a stress ratio of 0.6 the austenitic secant modulus drops about 15% to 23 to 24,000 ksi, duplex only about 5% [49:32][52:11]. Scope excludes flexural angles and tees, slender webs in flexure, unequal-leg angles, round-HSS flexure, tension-field action, composite members, and plastic analysis [30:58][37:48].

### Fabrication Notes
Strain hardening from cold forming, roller leveling, and other fabrication operations can raise the 0.2% offset yield strength by about 50% in the highly worked corner regions of a hollow structural section [15:40]. This is not free: working strain-hardened stainless requires more powerful equipment, stronger tools, and greater forces, and it can reduce ductility (though stainless ductility is high enough that this is rarely a problem) [16:12]. Welding stainless to carbon steel is common and acceptable provided the correct filler metals and welding procedures are used [56:52]. Because stainless has low thermal conductivity and high thermal expansion, weld distortion is a concern; DG27 recommends symmetrical joints, good fit-up, and reducing the extent of welding [73:30]. Scope applies to sections thicker than 0.125 inch [27:19].

### Erection Notes
The webinar is design-focused and does not cover field erection sequencing, crane, rigging, or bracing. The closest erection-adjacent guidance is detailing connections to accommodate the higher thermal expansion of stainless in a restrained frame, since trapped expansion forces can cause premature failure at elevated temperature [72:58]. Galvanic separation between dissimilar metals was addressed in Q&A: a small stainless canopy against a large aluminum standing-seam roof may not have a galvanic issue because relative surface areas govern [89:29].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The headline failure mode for stainless bolting is galling (also called cold welding), a local adhesion and seizure that occurs when underloaded surfaces are in relative motion under high torque; it is distinct from thread stripping and also affects aluminum and other oxide-film alloys [63:32]. DG27 mitigations are lubricating internal or external threads, smoothing the threads, or pairing bolt and nut of different stainless types with different hardness [64:33]. A carbon-steel DTI will not correctly predict tension in a stainless bolt and should not be used [65:05]. Other named pitfalls: time-dependent stress relaxation in pretensioned austenitic and duplex bolts [64:33], using carbon-steel bolts in stainless members [58:25], and the dual-certified penalty where 304/304L material must be designed to the lower FY per ASTM [82:29]. Chloride stress-corrosion cracking is an ambient-temperature risk noted for swimming-pool environments [90:36].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Stainless is specified for corrosion resistance and long, low-maintenance life, plus aesthetic exposed finishes, blast and impact resistance, and hygiene; the payback is in avoided coating and maintenance, not first cost [04:11]. Lean duplex grades are cheaper than standard duplex because they are less highly alloyed, while offering strengths well above austenitic [28:52]. The higher strength of duplex cannot always be fully used when deflection or local buckling governs, which caps the tonnage savings [37:15]. There are no AISC-manual-style member capacity tables for stainless; some producers publish section properties but not full capacity tables, so design effort per member is higher [83:36]. The dual-certification rule (design to the lower yield) is a real cost penalty on material that is physically stronger than its stamp [82:29].
