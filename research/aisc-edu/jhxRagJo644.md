## Weld Inspection: What Matters and What Doesn't
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jhxRagJo644 | Duration: 1:34:48 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
This is a Duane K. Miller (Lincoln Electric, Manager of Engineering Services) lecture for AISC on separating weld discontinuities that actually impair structural performance from cosmetic features that do not, so that inspection effort and repair money go where they matter. The thesis runs through the W. Edwards Deming "Out of the Crisis" frame at [07:54] (the system belongs to management, not the welder) and the James Cameron "rightist" quote at [03:57]: weld it correctly for the loading and service condition, then stop. The talk works from AWS A3.0 discontinuity definitions through the five NDT methods, the AISC 360 and AISC 341 inspection-task tables, and into fitness-for-service and fatigue, arguing that acceptance criteria are workmanship limits set by what a qualified welder can produce, not a hard boundary of suitability for service.

### Spec References

Table N5.4-3 footnote cites visual inspection of the web k-area within 3 in. (75 mm) of the weld.

### Technical Details
The five NDT methods (the "Big Five" at [1:26:53]) are visual (VT), dye penetrant (PT), magnetic particle (MT), radiographic (RT), and ultrasonic (UT). PT greatly enhances visual inspection, is low cost, works on non-magnetic material, and is very effective on surface-breaking cracks but cannot see subsurface flaws (slide at [31:36]). RT principles are shown via the film-density model at [35:33]: discontinuities read darker, thicker sections lighter, with edge-effect transitions. UT acceptance under AWS D1.1 uses an indication rating or "dB" value, and the SAC round-robin found broad scatter in that rating and that on average 16% of rejectable indications were false calls at locations with no implanted discontinuity [55:17]. Fatigue and fracture mechanics close the talk: a single-edge crack has a stress-intensity K-I about 1.58 times that of a through-thickness crack of equal length [1:11:05], and a worked fatigue example predicts 281,765 cycles for a crack to grow from 0.25 in. to 2.25 in. [1:07:08].

### Fabrication Notes
Table N5.4-1 (prior to welding) is the shop's first gate at [23:42]: WPSs must be available (P for both QC and QA), and fit-up of groove welds must be checked for joint preparation, dimensions (alignment, root opening, root face, bevel), cleanliness of the steel surfaces, tack weld quality and location, and backing type and fit. Table N5.4-3 (after welding) at [1:30:50] makes QC and QA both Perform welds cleaned, size/length/location, visual acceptance (crack prohibition, weld/base-metal fusion, crater cross section, weld profiles, weld size, undercut, porosity), arc strikes, k-area, backing and weld tab removal where required, and documentation of acceptance or rejection. The fitness-for-service argument at [1:03:11] is the core fab message: Clause 5 criteria reflect what a qualified welder can produce, not a structural failure boundary, so not every flagged discontinuity needs a repair. Jumbo sections warrant extra inspection for low toughness, thermal cutting, richer composition steel, high restraint, high residual stress, and reduced redundancy [1:18:59].

### Erection Notes
The repair-cost curve at [1:07:08] is the erection lesson: complication of repairs rises sharply from fab to erection to completion, so a discontinuity caught and dispositioned in the shop is far cheaper than one chased in the field after the member is set and loaded. The k-area MT requirement under AISC 341 J5 applies where continuity plates, doubler plates, and stiffeners are welded into the column k-area, which is field-relevant on seismic moment-frame connections [1:22:56]. The talk does not cover crane, rigging, bracing, or sequencing in detail; its erection content is about where repairs happen and why earlier is cheaper.

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named discontinuities and failures: incomplete fusion (IF), defined per AWS A3.0 as fusion that did not occur between weld metal and the fusion faces or adjoining beads, illustrated at [15:48] and again as a fillet-weld root condition at [43:27]; misalignment (high-low) at [19:45], shown as a profile defect with associated radiographic indication; and the full visual reject list from Table N5.4-3 (cracks, lack of fusion, bad crater cross section, out-of-tolerance profiles and size, undercut, porosity). The most important inspection failure named is the false call: the SAC UT round-robin found 16% of rejectable indications were at locations with no real discontinuity [55:17], which means UT scatter itself drives unnecessary repairs. The Tub Girder case at [1:15:02] makes the point that an unrepaired weld can be left in service when the loading is cyclic shear at low stress range with adequate throat and defined toughness.

### Bid / Cost Implications
NDT scope scales with Risk Category and is a direct cost line: 100% UT on tension CJP welds for Risk Category III or IV versus 10% for Risk Category II is a measurable difference in QA hours and schedule per the Section 5b slide at [11:51], so the bid must price NDT to the building's risk category, not a flat assumption. The repair-complication curve at [1:07:08] argues for shop QC investment over field rework, since the cost of a given repair multiplies through erection and completion. False-call rejections (16% in the SAC study) are pure waste, so an estimate that buys disciplined VT and right-sized NDT avoids paying for repairs that fix nothing. Jumbo sections and seismic k-area MT add inspection scope that must be carried where those conditions exist.
