## Design of Facade Attachments, Session L3: Lateral Drifts and Facade Attachments
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=i26hOfUImi0 | Duration: 1:27:53 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This is the fourth and final session of the AISC facade-attachments series presented by Alex Zimmer of Simpson Gumpertz and Heger, a co-author of AISC Design Guide 22 on facade attachments to steel-framed buildings [00:35]. The controlling idea is that in modern skinned construction the steel frame carries all gravity and lateral load while the facade is decoupled and goes along for the ride, so connections must allow relative movement between frame and skin or the facade picks up unintended loads and can fall off the building [05:11][07:14]. The session focuses on accommodating inter-storey drift, meaning how much one floor displaces relative to its upper and lower neighbors, both in-plane and out-of-plane, plus corners and designing facade joints for less than full code-level drift using mean recurrence intervals [01:36][14:27].

### Technical Details
Three mechanisms accommodate inter-storey drift for both out-of-plane and in-plane movement: rigid body translation, rigid body rotation, and distortion / racking [14:59][17:34]. Rigid body rotation is generally preferred for rigid facade systems; distortion is rarely done intentionally because it can damage brittle cladding and is mainly seen in aluminum glass curtain wall that racks into a parallelogram [16:33][19:08]. For metal stud and precast back-up the recommendation is rigid body translation: restrain in-plane horizontal, vertical, and out-of-plane at the bottom gravity connection, but allow sliding at the top so the floor above moves freely [21:11][22:16]. Precast panels restrain in-plane motion at only one of two anchors to allow for shrinkage [22:50]. Inter-storey deformation combines frame shear racking and flexural cantilever bending; shear deformation governs joint movement far more than flexure, and for buildings under about 10 storeys all drift is treated as shear [25:56][26:56]. Typical drift limit used in practice is storey height over 400 or 450 [44:12].

### Fabrication Notes
This webinar is about facade-to-frame connection detailing, not steel shop fabrication, so there are no welding, bolting, fit-up, or shop-QC procedures stated. The relevant fabrication-adjacent point is that tie-back anchors restraining out-of-plane motion at corners are often made as rods or relatively light sections so they can flex perpendicular to their axis without permanent deformation, and code language governs the diameter-to-length ratio of those rods so they stay flexible parallel to the panel plane [36:55][54:54]. Panelized facades assembled in a fabricator shop (precast) are contrasted with field-built back-up walls, which can take wider waterproofing joints [80:15].

### Erection Notes
Field coordination items dominate over erection sequencing. The back-up wall position relative to columns must be detailed so a swaying column does not hang up on the back-up; where the slab edge is narrow the back-up depth is reduced locally at the column [29:04][29:34]. Masonry or stud back-up built tight to the frame inadvertently behaves as an unintended shear wall and gets damaged [30:06]. Top-of-wall connections use clips or a slip track that capture the wall for out-of-plane load but let the spandrel beam deflect vertically and slide horizontally with no through-bolts or anchors restraining in-plane motion [31:44][32:15]. The structural engineer of record must communicate inter-storey lateral drifts to the specialty engineer who designs curtain wall or metal panel, usually through the architect's performance specification [52:23][53:24].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named failures: precast facade panels falling off a building because stiff connections did not allow the frame to drift relative to the panels, creating a life-safety hazard to people below [07:14][07:45]. Gypsum-sheathed metal stud back-up forced to rack acts as a mini shear wall, damaging screws, board, and the air and water barrier behind the veneer where it cannot be seen [19:39][20:41]. Brick built tight to the steel frame on transitional buildings (1890s through about 1950) traps moisture against the steel, corroding embedded items whose rust expands eight to ten times original volume and dislodges the masonry [75:37][76:39]. Corner joints are the most overlooked condition, where one facade moves perpendicular to its neighbor and can break off panel corners or rupture concealed air and water barriers [37:25][83:28]. Brittle finishes like stucco show moderate to extensive damage near 1 percent drift while curtain wall tolerates about 0.5 percent with only gasket damage [63:39][64:10].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Designing for tighter seismic drift than code limits reduces facade damage but requires a heavier, more expensive building frame, so there is a direct trade-off between frame steel tonnage and facade repair risk [59:03][59:33]. Choosing a shorter mean recurrence interval for serviceability drift (for example 25 years for a low-risk warehouse versus 100 years for a hospital) lowers the design wind speed and pressure and can save steel tonnage in the frame [67:46][85:03][85:33]. Designing facade joints for a reduced MRI yields a 30 to 50 percent reduction in design force (69 percent of strength wind at 100-year, 58 percent at 50-year, 50 percent at 25-year for the Boston example) [45:44][46:15]. The owner discussion about repair tolerance directly sizes the frame.
