### Core Concept
This session covers field remediation for rooftop-unit (RTU) loads on steel framing and steel joists, with most attention on the recurring problem of HVAC units that arrive late, vibrate, or land in the wrong location relative to the framing the structural engineer designed [00:01], [03:34]. The teaching thread is how to support, reinforce, or pre-engineer joist and beam framing so a misplaced or heavier-than-planned RTU does not require destructive after-the-fact fixes [09:42]. The presenter, a steel joist authority, also previews a Steel Joist Institute technical digest he is writing on joist reinforcement [05:36].

### Technical Details
For RTU vibration the rule of thumb given is that supporting beams or joists should have a natural frequency either 50 percent above or 50 percent below the unit operating frequency [01:33]. Two textbook frequency equations were stated in cycles per minute, not cycles per second: 188 divided by the square root of the deflection for a concentrated load at midspan, and 213 divided by the square root of the deflection for uniform load [02:33], [03:04]. K series joists are designed to the exact tabulated load with no spare capacity, the older H series carried an extra 300 lb concentrated load anywhere, and the KCS series carries a constant moment and constant shear capacity along its full length with all diagonals except the end diagonal designed for stress reversal (the end diagonal is 100 percent tension only unless uplift is specified) [06:38], [12:47], [13:48]. A concentrated load that does not land at a top-cord panel point cannot be carried by the top cord and must be transferred to a panel point, typically via an inverted channel across the deck ribs plus an angle web member down to the panel point [06:06].
### Fabrication Notes
The classic fabrication detail for slipping a reinforcing beam under an existing K series joist is to make the beam seat shallower than the 2-1/2 inch joist seat, about 2 inches deep, then shim about 1/2 inch to set it [08:11], [26:11]. A web stiffener is added at that shortened seat so the thin remaining web is not overstressed in shear and bending [08:41]. Using all 50 ksi yield steel gives better web shear strength, sometimes requiring only a slightly thicker web than otherwise [09:12]. For a special reinforcing joist, order it with no camber and a 2 inch deep seat, since every manufacturer cambers joists by default and a cambered joist will not stuff into an existing facility where the surrounding deflection is already locked in [25:08], [25:39].
### Erection Notes
Field installation of reinforcement is driven by access and interference. Before adding a joist or beam, identify existing piping, mechanical, electrical, and gas interference, because the cost of relocating it can make the in-fill solution prohibitive [24:37], [25:08]. A no-camber, shallow-seat member is what lets the erector shove the new piece into place and shim it [26:11]. The presenter's preferred field trick is to specify a joist with no end seat so the bare joist is short enough to lift and set, then field-weld a two-angle seat back to the main panel point, with the seat designed to carry the moment from the offset vertical load [27:13], [28:14]. Lateral support is mandatory: a joist is designed assuming top-cord lateral support every 36 inches normally provided by the deck and its welds, screws, or pins, so any added joist needs equivalent bracing, and you must not shoot nails vertically through the roof membrane to get it [26:42], [27:13].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Field damage examples shown include a plumber who cut most of a joist top cord (stopping only because he ran out of drill) and a tension cord with a large hump from an erector dropping the joist and re-erecting it without flagging it [18:26], [18:59]. A flagged unstable condition: a joist bearing on an HSS column with a beam on top and no stiffener, joist extension, or other stabilizer, which leaves a thin-web column with a poor L over R [16:53], [17:24]. Sprinkler mains, the 8 inch main specifically, must be hung from more than one joist or they overstress a single joist [22:03]. The curb and support-frame weight is frequently omitted from the unit weight the mechanical engineer gives you [16:23].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The cheapest prevention named is providing two extra joists in a bay (roughly 500 dollars each) so a heavier or relocated unit can be accommodated without later reinforcing, though owners resist paying for spare joists on a large job and then pay more for field rework [10:12]. Adding a new joist is generally the most cost-effective fix when schedule allows, but joist-manufacturer lead time often forces a faster beam-with-special-seat solution instead [19:29]. Load redistribution with a stiff spreader beam can carry a concentrated load across multiple joists and avoid reinforcement entirely; a 10 to 15 percent joist overstress is small enough that this often works [22:34], [23:35]. The presenter endorses modest overdesign for mechanical equipment and collateral loads when you already hold the job, and notes simplifying RTU reactions by taking total weight divided by two saves significant engineering time at no material cost [16:23], [20:30].
