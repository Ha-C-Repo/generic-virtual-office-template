## Working with Large Trusses
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B2zTYoGWhvo | Duration: 1:14:50 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This webinar (PDH code 5669) by Colin Hughes of a Southeast/Texas fabricator and Sylvie Boulanger of Super Metal (Montreal) is a fabricator-and-erector view of large trusses, not a connection-design lecture [00:34]. The governing theme is that the optimal number of splices in a truss is zero, fabricated in one shop piece when shipping and crane capacity allow, and that every downstream decision (splice type, camber method, member shape, erection sequence) is driven by transport limits, shop and field crane capacity, and site access [04:42][05:12]. The recurring engineering message is that fabricators should engineer camber themselves and look early for opportunities to size members to their advantage, because "if the engineer is off it is oops, if we are off it is ouch" when pieces will not fit [38:31].

### Spec References
No AISC 360 section numbers, table numbers, AWS D1.1 clauses, RCSC sections, or Design Guide numbers were spoken aloud. General principles, no section cited. The only named references are bolt grade A490 and bolt grade A449 (1 and 1/8 inch) [09:46][35:55], a "75% bearing contact, no more than 2/100 inch separation" machined-fit acceptance criterion stated as a project requirement [42:38], steel at 65 ksi [11:48][44:43], the turn-of-nut bolt method [74:10], and a reference to "Carol Drucker load-path recommendations" for keeping plate bending and eccentricity low [40:34].

### Technical Details
Truss types covered: a 70 foot HSS 3D architectural truss in Charlotte [03:40], a fabricated-in-one-piece pedestrian bridge at the NASCAR Hall of Fame [07:14], a multi-splice bolted truss over the RSA judicial building in Montgomery supporting eight floors [09:46], an 8th Avenue Place transfer truss (74 ft by 36 ft, 136 short tons, carrying two 45-story columns, 8,500 kips compression and 3,500 kips tension, members to W14x730) [34:19][35:55], a cantilever conveyor truss in Labrador launched like a bridge [43:40], stacked full-story student-residence trusses in Manitoba with one room per diagonal [51:22], and ASS welded 3D trusses at Calgary airport (largest 45 tons, shortest 14) [62:10]. Chord member choices: WT (web doubles as connection material for lapped HSS or angle webs), wide flange for larger loads, HSS for less horizontal bracing, with the "bird beak" mitered HSS node rotated 90 degrees and welded all around [16:54][20:26][20:57]. A W6x26 coped at both flanges could not be made to work and needed cope reinforcement plus web doublers; a heavier W16 with a thicker web would have avoided that [19:26].

### Fabrication Notes
Camber is built two ways: rolled chord members (smooth, no extra shop grinding, but often from a specialty plant with long lead time) or straight segmented members cut and angled to hit specific deflection callouts (uses mill stock but adds shop labor) [13:49][14:49]. Cambered chords are detailed twice, once in the full building model with infill, columns and posts, and once as the truss alone in its own model [15:51]. Heavy bolted splices were match-drilled in the shop so plates fit in the field [10:47]. On 8th Avenue Place, preassembly used fill-in-the-blank checks (staff not allowed to pre-check the box) and total stations for accuracy, and heavy bearing plates were machined to the 75% contact, 2/100 inch flatness criterion [39:33][42:38]. Calgary used jigs and a rotator so welding always sat in a good position for both quality and appearance, with a two-tier AESS standard: distant steel gets a good weld with no grinding, close-up steel gets grinding [63:42][64:43].

### Erection Notes
Large trusses are often erected twice, once in the fabricator's own yard for fit-up verification (RSA truss assembled in the backyard, a 3D pedestrian bridge laser-measured against detailing drawings) and once on site [22:27][23:27]. Field-welded splices need shoring towers and alignment aids that are later removed and scrapped (Duke Energy building) [12:49]. The Manitoba stacked truss came up crooked because it was rigged at two pickup points when four were required, corrected once the rig was fixed [58:00]. Calgary erection assembled pairs on the ground, set them on an adjustable "Swiss knife" temporary tower, installed four struts between them, then removed the tower [67:21]. Temperature mattered: some Manitoba trusses would not fit until crews retried in the cooler early morning [60:38].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The Labrador cantilever truss is the cautionary tale: at launch none of the base plates fit, so every hole was field-oversized; then the GC delayed grouting and the cantilever load crushed the unsupported column shims "like butter," tilting the truss onto the existing building [46:17][47:18]. The fix was a same-day-engineered jacking and shimming rig (supporting beam, lifting beam, hydraulic jacks, repeated jack-and-shim) that recovered position while keeping the original anchor rods, after which the contractor grouted immediately [49:51][50:51]. Other named misses: the W6x26 double-cope that could not be designed, the Manitoba two-point pick, and Manitoba trusses quietly retried at dawn because of thermal fit. Slip-critical oversized holes were called out as a strength penalty that forces more bolts (a 200-bolt splice would balloon to 300 to 400) [10:47][11:17].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Field-welded CJP splices were quoted at roughly 40 man-hours of welding per location (about 20 hours per flange) on the RSA chords (W14x730 and W14x65, 65 ksi), versus faster, cheaper bolted snug-tight connections [11:48][12:19]. The over-200 A490 bolts at 1 inch and over a foot long were special-order long-lead and expensive [09:46]. Spending more up front on a slightly larger WT, W16, or full-square HSS section repeatedly avoided larger downstream fabrication cost for cope reinforcement, web doublers, and gusseting [17:24][19:26][68:23]. Specifying the launch-and-engineer scope on the Labrador job thinned the bidder field. Field-oversizing every hole "made a real difference in how we managed the project" budget [47:18].
