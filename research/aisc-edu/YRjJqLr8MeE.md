## Robust Structures
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YRjJqLr8MeE | Duration: 1:30:40 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
This webinar (session E22, two speakers: Prof. Chris Foley of Marquette and Kirk Marchand of Protection Engineering Consultants) is about structural robustness and resistance to disproportionate (progressive) collapse in steel framing after a key member is lost to blast, vehicle impact, gas explosion, or construction defect [01:37][02:08][45:33]. The central idea is that a conventional steel frame is inherently robust "out of the box" and the engineering task is to quantify that latent capacity, not to add exotic detailing [05:08][36:48]. Robustness is framed three ways: acceptable probability of loss, acceptable probability of collapse, or acceptable risk and consequence, and a robust system is characterized by redundancy, reliable ductility, and the ability to accommodate load reversals [10:09][10:40]. The mechanics center on a transition from a flexural (plastic-hinge) mechanism to a geometric-stiffness or catenary/membrane mechanism as a damaged span deforms vertically, with both mechanisms linked and active together [11:10][19:53].

### Technical Details
Foley's analysis manipulates connection moment strength (term beta) and stiffness to show that partially restrained (PR) connections accumulate vertical deformation faster, which activates geometric stiffness earlier and generates large horizontal tension forces sooner, raising the risk of tensile rupture at the connection if those forces cannot be carried [13:15][15:16][15:48]. Strong-column-weak-beam behavior caps the moment funneled into columns and is favorable here as it is in seismic design [23:29][24:59]. The floor slab and deck matter: ACI-style continuity reinforcement, welded-wire mesh membrane action, and the steel deck acting as one-way tension reinforcement (deck spans one direction only, unlike a two-way cast-in-place slab) provide catenary capacity, but only with adequate anchorage at panel ends [26:01][27:02][28:34]. Marchand's DTRA test program ran four phases on exterior perimeter-bay specimens: base-plate and connection blast response, column blast response, full beam-girder-column system blast, and a phase-four ram push-down test on the damaged (or pristine "immaculate removal") double-span condition [52:21][53:21][54:25]. Tests measured ram force, axial force via load pins, and beam strain, then derived shear, moment, chord rotation, and flexural-versus-axial energy split [65:47][66:18].

### Fabrication Notes
The webinar is research-oriented and does not give shop fabrication tolerances or fit-up procedures. The fabrication-relevant takeaway is connection type selection: the connections tested were simple shear tabs, double-angle (web) connections, WTs, bolted cover-plate ("bolt wind moment"), bolted split-T, side plates, and welded-flange ("woof"/seismic moment) connections [35:46][54:55][59:35]. Foley's MSOE connection tests ran 27 specimens of three-, four-, and five-bolt simple shear connections and showed 17 inches of deformation over a 14-inch span with no fracture, with angles flattening and pulling off bolt heads as the limit state [36:16][36:48]. The practical fabrication point is that a few extra bolts per connection (the ACI-style "minimum two or three bolts per connection" idea) can materially raise integrity [06:38].

### Erection Notes
There is little direct erection or rigging content. The relevant systems-level point for the field is that anchorage at the ends of damaged spans, the in-plane shear stiffness of the deck diaphragm, and continuity of tie members govern whether catenary action can develop, so deck attachment (puddle welds, headed studs) and connection completeness in the as-erected condition carry real structural-integrity weight [30:37][39:53]. Marchand stresses that corner and perimeter bays often cannot develop catenary action because there is nothing to react the membrane force against, so the as-built tying scheme around the building perimeter matters [45:02][45:33].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
No code rejections or QC inspection failures are named since this is research, not a shop or field QA talk. The named failure modes from testing are the connection degradation sequences: bottom-flange failure, then sequential bolt failures, then top-flange failure for welded-flange connections, and web tear-out and angle failure for cover-plate connections at roughly 12 percent rotation [71:28][74:00][75:06]. The cover-plate connection behaved unpredictably and is flagged as the "oddball" that should give pause in setting acceptance criteria [83:27][84:29]. A recurring analyst error called out is discounting residual capacity: a blast-damaged column can retain up to about 30 percent of its vertical load capacity, contrary to the assumption that a visibly deformed column is finished [60:06][60:37].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The cost theme is that imposing full seismic requirements purely to chase robustness makes buildings much more expensive without true equivalency to a disproportionate-collapse event [88:07][89:10][89:41]. The research lets a practitioner credit the inherent capacity of conventional connections through nonlinear inelastic analysis instead of buying seismic detailing wholesale [89:41]. A second cost-relevant result: the dynamic increase factor for alternate-path analysis drops from a factor of 2 (elastic worst case) to about 1.2 when inelastic connection behavior is allowed, which reduces demand and therefore steel weight in integrity design [62:41][63:45][64:15].
