## High Strength Bolting - Why isn't it Simple, the Way it Used to Be?
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kxitGn8zLJ0 | Duration: 1:01:20 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Dr Peter Birkemoe (University of Toronto) traces high strength structural bolting from the riveting era to the present and explains why bolt design grew complicated as research drove bolt counts down. The central technical lesson is prying action in bolts loaded in tension: how flange flexibility amplifies the bolt force by a prying ratio Q/P, and why pretension, joint fit, and shimming control whether a tension joint survives fatigue [13:55][18:36]. The recurring theme is that fewer, stronger bolts create new failure mechanisms (prying, block shear) that simpler riveted joints never exposed [14:25].
### Spec References
The talk is historical and conceptual, so few clause numbers are spoken. Named documents: the RCSC (Research Council on Structural Connections, formed 1947, first specification 1951, then the Specification for Structural Joints Using ASTM A325 and A490, last edition cited as 2004 with another pending) [08:48][09:19][60:18]; the 1912 American Bridge Company specification from the Carnegie Steel pocket companion [04:07]; and the Guide to Design Criteria for Bolted and Riveted Joints (the "bolt guide" / RCSC Council guide) [28:58][30:30]. The speaker references "the new AISC" requiring a Whitmore-section check on brace ends but cites no section number [51:12]. Fastener grades named: A325 and A490 [09:19].
### Technical Details
Pretension is set near 70 percent of the bolt minimum tensile strength, and a key principle is that pretension does not change ultimate bolt strength; a rigid flange means the bolt does not "see" load until applied tension exceeds pretension [18:36][22:14]. With a real flexible flange the bolt tension rises from first load and at ultimate the relation is applied load plus prying force equals ultimate tension [23:50]. Several prying formulas exist (Douty, DeWolf, Struik/Delft, Kulak/Fisher, Nair, Birkemoe, Kennedy and others), all derived from similar mechanics; the speaker prefers the Delft equation for being intuitive and semi-empirical [29:29][30:00]. Block shear, the square-over-4G rule, shear-tearing models, and Whitmore section (a shear-lag effective-area reduction, distinct from block tear-out) are discussed [39:53][51:44].
### Fabrication Notes
The shift away from "all surfaces shall be painted" to assembling clean or rusty mill-scale steel is central; dry mill-scale or wheel-abraded faying surfaces affect slip behavior [55:04][47:46]. Shimming at the bolt line is required where flanges do not contact, and galvanized shims add zinc thickness inside the grip, which can cause loss of tension, so shim choice matters [37:49][38:19]. The speaker notes that turned bolts (within six thousandths fit, reamed hole) were the old secondary-rivet equivalent [48:16]. A325/A490 pretension target values appear in the RCSC manual by diameter [58:42].
### Erection Notes
Field practice and detailing are decisive for tension joints. Two case studies: a crane girder where a dished bottom flange left gaps at the bolt line, so pretension was lost and bolts fell out under crane load because the flange compressed [33:37][34:39]; and a galvanized HSS bridge expansion strut where bolts torqued in the morning could be loosened by hand in the afternoon, because afternoon sun expanded the member (added compressive P) and erased the induced tension [35:11][36:45]. The cure in both was careful shimming or a better detail. Snug-tight bolts may permit slight slip and are installed mainly to draw plies together and keep nuts on [54:32].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Loss of pretension from soft material in the grip (insulation, excess galvanizing) is flagged as a fatigue-killer; the Hartford arena roof collapse is cited where tension hangers had an insulating layer in the joint leading to low or no tension and poor threaded-fastener fatigue behavior [50:07][50:40]. The "banging bolt" phenomenon: snug-tight joints with fewer bolts than a slip-critical design, on dry mill-scale or wheel-abraded surfaces, slip under service load and emit a loud bang [54:32][55:04]. A field-malpractice warning: torque "cheater" conversion tables (torque to tension) are unreliable and should not be used; follow the RCSC installation and verification methods instead [58:42][60:18].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The historical arc is a cost story: research that recognized true bolt strength cut the number of bolts required for shear and bearing, shrinking connections and reducing fabrication and inspection cost [11:21][13:25]. There is "always pressure to keep connection costs down and reduce inspection requirements" [13:25]. The flip side is that slip-critical designs carry extra bolts that function as a safety and cost factor versus bearing-only designs, and that snug-tight versus pretensioned choice has cost consequences in the shop now that tension-control bolt guns pretension nearly everything anyway [57:08][52:48].
