## 7_Seismic Design in Steel_Concepts and Examples_Part 7

### Core Concept
This is the third live session of Rafael Sabelli's eight-part AISC seismic-design series, covering the full design of a steel special moment frame (SMF) on a four-story example building [00:02][01:04]. The governing idea is that an SMF is controlled by drift, not strength, so members are sized to the drift limit first and strength checks (which run well below a demand-capacity ratio of one) come last [03:39][04:10][75:23]. The worked example uses a prequalified reduced beam section (RBS) connection and carries the design from member selection through the panel zone, the beam and column strength, the base plate, and the anchor rods [02:37][57:01].

### Spec References
Cited verbatim by the speaker: AISC 358 (prequalified connections), specifically Section 5.3 for RBS member restrictions [10:50]; AISC 341 seismic provisions, with highly ductile member requirements in Section E3, demand-critical welds, protected zones, and the 2010-vs-2016 minimum column flange thickness rule [05:12][20:32][45:11][93:00]; AISC 360 for strong-column-weak-beam, web local yielding, web crippling, flange local bending, panel zone shear, the B2 second-order factor, and the C-sub-M / B1 rule [23:38][37:29][67:00]; AISC Design Guide 1 base-plate-with-large-moment method [58:02][62:07]; ACI bearing equation with the square root of A2-over-A1 spread for grout [61:06]; AISC manual Table 14-2 (anchor-rod washers) and Supplement S1 (weldable anchor material) [68:15]; ASCE 7 for drift limits, the stability factor theta, and Section 12.8.7 P-delta load factors [31:52][34:26][83:44]; NIST/NEHRP Seismic Tech Brief No. 2 on steel SMF and Farzad Naeim's Seismic Design Handbook as references [18:28][75:54].

### Technical Details
The RBS beam is limited to a rolled or built-up I-shape, 36 in deep max, 300 lb/ft max, flange thickness 1.75 in max, span-to-depth ratio at least seven, with seismically compact flanges checked at the center two-thirds of the reduced section [10:50][11:22]. Flange reduction (dimension C) runs 10 to 25 percent per side, dropping flexural strength roughly 15 to 30 percent; the example took a 27 percent cut of the plastic section modulus [12:22][38:32]. Strong-column-weak-beam is screened with a plastic-section-modulus ratio kept above 1.5, and the example pair (W14x132 column, W24x62 beam) finally cleared it at 1.44 against a Z-ratio of 2.1 [23:38][43:39]. Drift design landed at a period near 1.5 seconds and a base shear about 60 percent of the strength-design shear, with the bottom-story B2 at 1.06 and theta at 0.07, both inside limits so K equals one [29:44][33:24][34:26].

### Fabrication Notes
The shop cuts the RBS radius into both flanges per the A, B, C dimensions from AISC 358, and compactness is verified at the reduced center two-thirds width, not the full flange [13:22][14:53]. The column required continuity plates (driven by the minimum-flange-thickness check, 1.17 in needed vs 1.03 in available) and a roughly half-inch doubler plate from the 937-kip panel zone demand [45:41][46:43][47:14]. A key optimization message is that going to a heavier column (W14x176) and a lighter beam eliminated both the continuity plates and the doubler, removing significant shop labor at the cost of a little added steel [49:49][50:53]. Connection completion (not detailed in the example) includes removing weld tabs and backing bars at the demand-critical CJP welds [73:52].

### Erection Notes
RBS keeps inelastic strain in a protected zone away from the column face, with lateral bracing required just outside the hinge region; the example needed bottom-flange braces about every 5 ft (50 times an r-sub-y of about 1.4) while the top flange was braced continuously by the composite deck [11:52][51:54][52:25]. At the column base the design uses double-nutted anchor rods top and bottom, four per side on a 32 in by 32 in plate, with debonding of the rods from the concrete so the rods can elongate and the connection can rotate for ductility [59:33][68:15][73:22]. Shear at the base is best taken out by bearing of the base plate edge or by friction (a 0.4 coefficient on the flexural compression gave 102 kips), not through the anchor rods, which makes for poor bending elements [66:10][67:13].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The session frames "failures" as design rejections rather than weld defects: selecting a beam too deep or too heavy for RBS prequalification, or one that is not seismically compact, only to have it rejected at the prequalification check [05:12]. Web local yielding came out "just barely no good," driving the continuity-plate requirement, and the panel zone failed without a doubler [45:11][46:43]. Treating the moment-frame column base as a true pin is called unrealistic because the connection cannot actually accommodate the large inelastic rotation; assuming a fully rigid base can be unconservative for member selection because real foundation flexibility adds to first-story drift [85:18][86:19]. The speaker also warns that the AISC 358 blanket 10-percent drift increase for RBS is overly conservative (real effect is 2 to 5 percent if modeled directly) [20:00].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Continuity plates and doubler plates are the cost levers: tighter lateral brace spacing (every 5 ft vs every 7.5 ft) adds a few braces, but spending on a heavier column to delete continuity plates and doublers usually nets a cheaper, more economical frame [48:15][50:53][75:54]. Connection-type choice carries direct cost trade-offs: WUF-W is cheap and easy for small buildings, RBS adds flange-cutting, and bolted flange-plate or bolted end-plate connections appeal to erectors who want to avoid field welding and the inspection/NDE that comes with it [07:14][08:14][08:44]. Anchor-rod sizing has cost weight too: tension-only design allowed 1.5 in grade 55 rods, while forcing shear and bending through the rods would have pushed all eight to 2 in or even 2.25 in diameter [65:10][66:10].
