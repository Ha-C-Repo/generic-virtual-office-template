## Field Fixes - Part 3

### Core Concept
This webinar is a practitioner walkthrough of common field-fix situations on structural steel jobs and how to repair or design around them, presented by an engineer (Jim, with co-author Larry) drawing on roughly 40 years of practice. The recurring theme is that most field problems trace to detailing, mill tolerances, and concrete-to-steel coordination rather than bad steel, and that material is cheap while labor is expensive, so reinforce or adjust rather than scrap [22:57]. A second theme: design connections for the completed structure, and visit the job site because, in the speaker's words, he has never gone to a site in 40 years without finding something wrong [22:25].
### Spec References
The speaker cites the 2005 AISC Specification, specifically Section F13 covering holes in beams, noting those requirements were liberalized versus the prior spec [01:31]. He references ASTM A6 for mill tolerances on rolled shapes (web off-center, tilted flanges, depth variation) [06:46]. He cites ASTM A992 (50 ksi yield, flange-sampled mill reports) versus legacy A36 [03:35, 04:39], ACI 117 for concrete vertical and lateral alignment tolerances [12:33], OSHA four-anchor-rod erection-stability rules [27:34], and AWS for column-to-base-plate welding [31:15]. He also mentions the 2005 spec contains new combined bolt-plus-weld strength requirements [25:32].
### Technical Details
Mill reports can hint at higher actual yield (old A36 often ran 42 to 44 ksi) but should not be used directly to justify added capacity; legacy reports were sampled from the stronger web, and A992 now mandates flange sampling, so an old web-based report overstates flange strength [03:02, 04:39]. Steel is not homogeneous or isotropic; webs can run 10 percent higher yield than flanges due to more rolling [04:06]. A6 tolerances (off-center webs, tilted flanges, depth deviation) accumulate and can make a beam-to-column-web weld impossible to land [06:46]. Continuity plates must match the A992 50 ksi material; a real project used 36 ksi plates against A992 members and the moment connections were invalid until field-welded new plates and beam stubs fixed them [07:49, 08:20].
### Fabrication Notes
Oversize embeds and weld plates because they will not land where the form drawings show; make a wall weld plate roughly the size of the wall, and provide nail holes in embeds so the concrete crew can set them [16:08, 13:34]. Use generously oversized continuity plates so a backup bar and full-penetration weld can still be made despite tolerance stackup [07:17]. For bolted flange-plate connections, leave plenty of shim room (at least a 3/8 inch shim stack on the bottom) and consider weld detail and sequence to control distortion, with heat straightening after fab if needed [11:01, 11:31]. Deck does not seat over bolted moment connections where nuts stick up an inch; fix the detail ahead of time so the deck bears and the required concrete cover is achieved [00:01].
### Erection Notes
Field-fit problems are routine: beams too long or short at concrete walls get cut and rewelded, caught on larger angles, or field-measured and replaced, with adjustable and slotted connections designed in to absorb concrete tolerance [11:31, 14:35]. Bolted flange-plate beams can often be jacked into place and the bolts snugged to pull them in, but a mismatched two-fabricator job required air-arcing out all the top-plate CJP welds and repositioning, which was very expensive [09:57, 10:30]. OSHA requires four anchor rods per column for erection stability with limited exceptions (light posts around 300 lb); fewer than four needs special guided-erection procedures [27:34, 28:05]. Avoid weak-axis moment connections entirely where possible; rotate columns or add bracing to frame into the strong axis [05:45].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named failures include 36 ksi continuity plates installed in A992 moment frames (connection invalid) [08:20], two-fabricator beam-and-column splits that did not fit despite shim allowance [10:30], a torched beam hole oversized past the recommended limit in a building that partially collapsed under snow load when the flanges buckled [20:17], and a crew cutting girt flanges out at midspan to thread bracing rod instead of drilling the web, hidden behind liner panel [21:22]. Epoxy field-fix bolts melt above 200 degrees, so they fail in a fire unless fire-protected [17:12]. Welding to embeds risks concrete spalling, mitigated with small fillet welds, controlled sequence, and thick enough embed heat sinks (1/2 inch for deck bearing, 3/4 inch for beam bearing) [18:13, 18:45].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The standing cost rule stated is material is cheap and labor is expensive, so reinforce existing members (cover plates, bent plates, added members below, boxing with channels) rather than replace when analysis supports it [22:57, 23:28]. The two-fabricator flange-plate mismatch is a cautionary cost case: splitting columns and beams between fabricators removed any shop fit check and forced full-penetration weld removal and rework in the field, described as very expensive [10:30, 11:01]. Job-site visits are framed as cheap insurance; a half-hour walk catches problems, and the speaker urges negotiating a site-visit fee or going anyway [21:52, 22:25].
