### Core Concept
This webinar segment (slides 79 onward) covers composite beam design strength and serviceability under the 2005 AISC specification, with the bulk of the time spent on camber and floor levelness as the dominant real-world problem in composite floor construction [00:01], [07:13]. The speaker frames composite design around a plastic stress distribution model that lets you tune shear connectors to less than full composite action for economy [02:05], [02:35], and warns repeatedly that the precomposite construction stage, not the composite stage, often controls beam size [01:34].
### Spec References
The speaker cites AISC specification Chapter F for bare-steel and precomposite strength provisions [00:32], [05:11]; Chapter I and its commentary for composite moment-of-inertia options [27:32], [28:33]; the AISC Code of Standard Practice for camber tolerances [17:12], [19:48]; Design Guide 11 for floor vibration [22:56], Design Guide 5 (low and medium high-rise buildings) for a camber section [23:29], and Design Guide 3 for serviceability [26:31]. Load references are ASCE 7 load combinations including the 1.4 dead-alone case [06:43] and ASCE 7-02 Appendix B serviceability combinations [26:31]. The 25% minimum composite action is described as a suggestion, not a specification requirement [01:02].
### Technical Details
Plastic stress distribution requires a compact web meeting the h/tw limit; a non-compact web forces design by superposition of elastic stresses, an issue mainly for thin-web plate girders [00:32]. Effective flange width remains 1/8 of the span, half the distance to the adjacent beam centerline, or for spandrels the distance to the slab edge [03:38]. Short-term deflection uses an effective I equal to 75% of the transformed moment of inertia, the 25% reduction accounting for creep; long-term creep is sometimes estimated using 50% of E [28:33], [29:34]. Partially composite I is reduced by a summation-Q term, and the manual offers a lower-bound moment of inertia as a conservative simplified check [29:34], [30:37]. Total deflection is Delta 1 (precomposite bare beam under wet concrete) plus Delta 2 (composite section, superimposed dead and long-term live) plus Delta 3 (composite section, transient live) minus camber [31:07].
### Fabrication Notes
Camber is an imperfect hydraulic-ram process applied by trial and error, and identical beams can come out level, high, or low [19:48]. The speaker insists camber be measured and inspected at the fabrication plant in the unstressed condition, never in the erected field condition, because beams lose camber in transit and it is unfair to hold the fabricator to a field check; the Code of Standard Practice states this unstressed basis [17:43], [19:16]. Practical limits: round camber down to 1/4-inch increments, hold a 3/4-inch minimum (fabricators will not give a 1/2-inch camber), and treat anything over about 2 to 2-1/4 inches with caution [22:24], [10:54]. Welded cover plates and shop-welded edge angles change camber because welding heat alters it [08:14], [23:59].
### Erection Notes
The metal deck braces the top flange only when its flutes run perpendicular to the beam, so girders should be taken as unbraced over the distance between framing composite beams unless special construction bracing is provided [05:11], [05:41]. The pour method is an erection-stage decision the engineer controls by specification: pour to constant thickness (floor follows deck and beam deflection profile) or pour to a level floor with extra concrete as needed; multi-story contractors will give only the specified thickness and no more unless told otherwise, because extra concrete on every floor of a 60-plus-story building adds up [14:36], [15:38], [16:11].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Over-camber is called the worst case: studs can end up exposed above the floor, which an inspector flags as a missing 2-hour fire rating, and a thinner-than-designed cover (2+3 instead of 2+4-1/2) can erode the composite flange strength you calculated [20:19], [21:22]. A real spandrel-beam case is described where a tube post and continuous sill turned the cambered beam into a vierendeel truss that stiffened it, the camber never came out, the curtain wall would not meet tolerance, and the posts had to be cut off and releveled at cost [09:18], [09:51]. A separate lawsuit involved floor vapor emission attacking reformulated low-VOC adhesives on structural floors [24:29], [25:30]. Common design oversights named: forgetting that chases and floor openings in hotels and condos cut effective flange, and forgetting that a bent-plate deck stop adds moment of inertia and kills the camber [04:41], [10:23].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The composite model lets cost track the client: developers wanting code-minimum office buildings get minimum composite action and the fewest studs, while an institutional owner planning future load increase may pay for full composite action and reserve capacity [02:35], [03:07]. Designing non-composite-and-heavier to avoid camber is a valid but more expensive option for clients willing to pay for a stiffer floor [12:28]. Shoring is rarely the most economical choice per contractors and adds long-term creep deflection [13:01]. Floor vibration design (Design Guide 11) for retail, convention, or ballroom floors can control and force upsizing of the whole framing, a direct cost driver [22:56].
### Your Company Application
For Mario's shop crew, the load-bearing takeaway is that camber must be checked at the plant in the unstressed condition with someone measuring each beam, since field camber checks are off the table once a beam ships; on any composite-deck job Your Company fabricates, build a shop camber-inspection step into the traveler and round camber to 1/4-inch with a 3/4-inch floor. For Ivan's takeoff, composite action percentage and stud count are a cost lever to confirm against the EOR drawings, not to assume, and Chapter F precomposite strength often sizes the beam, so the bare-steel weight is the governing tonnage input. For Tekla detailing, cover plates, bent-plate deck stops, and edge angles welded in the shop alter camber and must be modeled before the camber call is finalized.
