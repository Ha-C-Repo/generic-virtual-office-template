## Steel Design After College - Part 12

### Core Concept
This webinar segment covers three practical topics: anchor-rod placement tolerances and field-fix options at column bases, the design and connection economics of steel trusses, and computer-model verification practice. The unifying theme is closing the loop between analysis, design, and what actually gets built, plus controlling cost by controlling connections and field problems before they happen [00:01], [06:39], [27:45].
### Spec References
The speaker cites the AISC Code of Standard Practice anchor-rod placement tolerances versus ACI 117 (stated as "112 over 114," the concrete placement tolerance standard) and stresses they do not agree, with the AISC steel tolerances being much tighter [01:33], [02:03]. He references weldable anchor-rod material as "grade 55" and A36 as weldable grades [05:05], grade 50 and grade 65 steel for long-span truss chords [25:42], and the slenderness provisions of Chapter F for slender tee compression elements [18:06]. No AISC 360 section numbers, AISC 341 seismic sections, AWS D1.1 clauses, RCSC sections, or Design Guide numbers were stated by number; treat the rest as general principles, no section cited.
### Technical Details
A truss is framed as a beam: diagonals and posts carry shear, chords carry the flexural couple, so chord force equals the moment diagram divided by truss depth (M/D) [07:42], [10:49]. Economical truss depth runs 1/10 to 1/12 of span, with a flat cost curve in that range [11:51]. Truss types named: king post, scissors, bowstring, and a "Brunel" teardrop catenary truss after engineer I. K. Brunel [08:44], [09:14]. Panel points should be set so loads frame in at the joints, keeping chords as axial-only columns rather than beam-columns, and uniform load on a top chord should be avoided because it adds moment to an already heavily loaded compression chord [13:26], [13:58]. Secondary bending moments in pin-modeled chords are generally negligible and may be ignored, but if chords are modeled continuous, the connections must be designed continuous to match [15:30], [16:02].
### Fabrication Notes
The shop-versus-field rule: if the truss is 16 feet deep or less it can ship over the highway (about 13 feet routine, up to 16 with a special permit), so make all joints shop welded, ship in one piece, and use a bolted splice only every 60 to 120 feet; deeper trusses force field-bolted connections [16:33], [17:05]. Connection cost dominates heavy trusses, where connection weight (gusset plates, bolts, stiffeners, welds) can be 30 to 35 percent of total truss weight, so simplifying connections and even spending more on members can be the cheaper path [12:23], [12:54]. Connection options scale with load: single gusset for double-angle or tee members, slotting and perimeter-welding HSS pipe or tube to a single gusset, single-gusset wide-flange with claw angles for medium loads, and double gusset plates (one each side, bolted flanges) as the standard for heavy W12/W14/W16 chords [16:33], [18:37], [20:39], [21:09]. Tees that deepen the stem let web members bolt or weld directly and save a gusset, with the trade-off of slender-element checks per Chapter F [18:06].
### Erection Notes
The engineer should carry a preconceived erection procedure and show it as a suggested sequence on the drawings, since for long-span roofs the heaviest loads can occur during erection [22:41], [23:11]. Long-span design should model an out-of-straightness (built-in P-Delta) because the truss will end up that way regardless [24:12], [24:42]. Slip-critical bolts with oversized holes are recommended to absorb fit-up and reduce field fixes and hole wallowing, though fabricators argue the cost both ways and the fabricator-erector team should settle it [24:42], [25:12]. The Miller Park "Big Blue" crane collapse is referenced as an erection-load lesson, attributed primarily to operating the crane outside acceptable wind conditions [26:45], [27:15].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The headline failure is a mislocated anchor rod, for example a 2-inch rod set 3 inches off, which is an expensive, schedule-killing fix because the column usually cannot move [00:01]. Contractors exploiting the looser ACI concrete tolerance to dodge the tighter AISC tolerance is named as a recurring contractual trap [02:03]. Model-side errors named: designing one steel grade on the drawings (grade 50) while the analysis used another (A36) or vice versa, wrong unbraced length assumed by the program (it often defaults to member length when the real value may be half or twice that), ignoring the roughly 5 percent friction at "frictionless" pot bearings or Teflon sliding supports which can crack supports under large axial force, and stopping at first-order analysis instead of running second-order [27:45], [30:51], [31:52], [28:45].
### Bid / Cost Implications
In heavy long-span trusses the money is in the connections, not the member sizes, so design simplicity at the joints drives bid price more than tonnage [12:23]. Engaging the fabricator and erector early to capture shop preferences yields the best bid price, and grouping repetitive truss member sizes can trade a little extra tonnage for detailing and fabrication savings [22:09], [22:41]. Grade 65 steel for long-span chords costs only 0 to 3 percent more than grade 50 and reduces member sizes, effectively free strength, with the caveat of overseas sourcing and a possible shipping delay that pre-ordering can absorb [25:42], [26:12].
### Your Company Application
This maps directly to Your Company anchor-rod doctrine: specify AISC Code of Standard Practice tolerances explicitly so a GC cannot fall back on looser ACI 117 numbers, require top and bottom templates and a qualified surveyor at the pre-construction meeting, and design rods with extra extension length so a pushed-down rod still takes a nut.
