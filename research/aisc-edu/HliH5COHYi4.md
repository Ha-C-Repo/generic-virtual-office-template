### Core Concept
This session teaches horizontal diaphragm behavior and design: how a floor or roof deck collects in-plane wind or seismic shear and distributes it to the vertical lateral-load-resisting (VLR) elements. The speaker classifies diaphragms as rigid, semi-rigid, or flexible and shows that this classification depends not only on the material (concrete slab vs metal deck) but also on the spacing of the VLR elements, that is the diaphragm aspect ratio [03:37][07:15]. The controlling idea is to treat a simple rectangular diaphragm as a deep beam spanning between VLR supports, with a shear and moment diagram, chord forces at the edges, and a verified load path from the field of the deck through collectors and connections into the bracing [11:55][32:28].

### Spec References
The speaker cites IBC 2003 and IBC 2006 for the code definition of a flexible diaphragm: flexible when the maximum diaphragm lateral deformation (Delta D) exceeds two times the average story drift (Delta VR) of the associated story [04:08][04:40]. He references the Steel Deck Institute (SDI) for an alternate classification based on shear stiffness G Prime [05:12][06:12], and ACI 318 Chapter 21 with ACI equation 21-10 for the nominal shear strength of a concrete-slab diaphragm [28:52]. He names the SDI diaphragm design manual and research by Larry Luttrell of West Virginia University [22:11][27:50][28:20], and Jim Fisher's book "Designing with Steel Joists, Joist Girders and Steel Deck" for joist-seat shear capacity [17:32]. ### Technical Details
Diaphragm shear stiffness is expressed as G Prime, equal to deck thickness T times the shear modulus G, and is tabulated in deck catalogs for both bare and concrete-filled decks [21:06][23:14][24:16]. Shear deflection generally controls over flexural deflection because diaphragms are very deep in their own plane; the shear deflection of a uniformly loaded shear beam goes as q L squared over 8 B T G [20:36][21:06]. Diaphragm shear strength S is governed by four phenomena: edge fastener strength, interior panel strength, corner fastener strength, and shear buckling of the deck, tabulated in pounds per linear foot of attachment [24:48][25:19]. Typical bare-deck shear capacity runs about 150 to 200 up to roughly 1200 lb/ft, with most decks in the 400 to 500 lb/ft range; California high-seismic work uses fillers to raise capacity [25:19][25:49]. Terminology covered includes drag struts, collector beams, chord (tension and compression) elements, the field of the diaphragm, support fasteners, side-lap fasteners, and the deck fastening pattern notation such as 36/3, 36/4, and 36/9 [08:47][30:24].

### Fabrication Notes
The session is design-oriented, but fabrication-relevant points are the deck-to-purlin attachment pattern and side-lap fastening, since the chosen pattern (welds or screws, 36/3 through 36/9) dictates the transferable shear per running foot and the published G Prime value [22:43][30:54]. The speaker notes safety factors of about 2.75 to 3.25 depending on whether the diaphragm is welded, screwed, or filled, reflecting test scatter [26:50][27:20]. He warns that catalog strengths must be read carefully for whether they are stated as ASD or LRFD values and whether the one-third ASD wind increase is already built in, to avoid taking the increase twice [25:49][27:20].

### Erection Notes
Standing-seam roofs have no diaphragm capacity and act only as linear vertical-load elements, so the erector must install a separate horizontal diaphragm, commonly rod bracing, angles, or T's woven between bar joists and over, under, or through purlins, which the speaker calls expensive and no fun to erect [00:33][01:35][02:05]. Where joist seats create a 2.5 in or 5 in gap between the concrete slab plane and the steel beam, filler elements (an angle, a tube section, or a channel) are field-added to supplement the limited joist-seat shear capacity and complete the load path down into the beam [16:29][18:02]. The erector must respect the load path through collector beams, beam-to-column connections, and across to the brace frame so high diaphragm shears actually reach the ground [15:29][16:29].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The repeated failure mode is an incomplete or invalid diaphragm load path: forgetting that a standing-seam roof carries no shear, or failing to supplement joist seats in a high-wind or high-seismic condition, which has produced documented diaphragm failures in earthquakes [01:04][18:32][19:04]. Offsets where a brace frame steps from one plane to another dump the full accumulated shear from above into the diaphragm at that level, an easily missed high-shear condition [11:23][11:55]. Large floor openings act like a cope and create local shear and bending concentrations that need added reinforcing steel and a shear check across the reduced width [19:35][20:06].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The speaker is explicit that supplemental diaphragm steel is a real cost. Adding a horizontal rod or angle diaphragm under a standing-seam roof, weaving steel between joists and purlins, is described as expensive and labor-intensive to erect [01:35][02:05]. Filler tubes, angles, or channels to bridge joist-seat gaps in high-shear roofs are added pieces and added fabrication and field labor [18:32]. The takeoff must reflect that the deck fastening pattern (36/3 vs 36/9) and side-lap count drive both connection labor and required deck strength, so a denser pattern in a high-seismic or high-wind zone carries more installation cost [30:54][31:25].
