## Fundamentals of Connection Design: Shear Connections, Part 2

### Core Concept
This is the final session of a four-part AISC series taught by Dr. Brad Davis, covering the design of four shear (simple) connection types: single angle, single plate (shear tab), unstiffened seated, and stiffened seated [01:38]. The unifying theme is that every simple shear connection must be flexible enough to behave as a pin, which requires demonstrated rotational ductility so the small end moment that develops can be relieved rather than fracturing the connection [02:11]. Each connection is placed at the lower-right of the AISC manual beam-to-column classification curve, treating the member and connection as pinned once ductility is ensured [01:38, 02:11].

### Technical Details
Single angle connections use two distinct eccentricities, e-sub-A for angle design and e-sub-B for bolt design, equal only for a single bolt column [04:48]; the outstanding leg adds angle flexural yielding (phi Fy times t L-squared over 6) and flexural rupture through the holes (phi Fu times Z-net, phi 0.75) [07:23, 07:55]. Conventional single plate (shear tab) limits are: one bolt column, 2 to 12 bolts, A not over 3.5 in, weld at least 5/8 of plate thickness both sides, and plate or web no thicker than Table 10-9 max [16:38, 17:38]. Ductility is achieved by "plowing," keeping the plate thin enough that bolts bear-deform it [18:08]. Extended single plate connections drop those limits and add plate buckling (Section F11 with Cb floor of 1.84) and shear-flexure interaction (a von-Mises-type squared inequality, phi 1.0 shear and phi 0.9 bending) [37:40, 39:44]. The worked extended example was a half-inch by 9 by 15 in A36 plate, six 7/8 in A325N bolts in two columns, W14x43 beam to a W14x90 column, V-u 30 kips, governed near 0.932 on interaction [42:21, 53:42]. Seated connections rely on web local yielding and web local crippling to set required bearing length L-sub-B, with setback taken as 3/4 in (1/2 in nominal plus 1/4 in beam-length tolerance) [58:00, 66:18].

### Fabrication Notes
The single angle is shop-welded or shop-bolted to the supporting member so the beam simply lands and bolts to the leg parallel to the web during erection [06:21]. Shear-tab plates are shop-welded to the support with double-sided fillet welds, sized at 5/8 of plate thickness so the weld develops the plate and no explicit weld strength calc is required [13:00, 28:26]. Holes on the support side of a single angle must be standard, not slotted, so the angle cannot rotate the beam end out of bearing; beam-side holes may be standard or short slots [05:51]. For stiffened seats the stiffener-to-seat weld must match the size of the stiffener-to-column weld so the horizontal weld works with the vertical [68:54]. A 5/16 in fillet was noted as completable in one pass [54:19].

### Erection Notes
Single angle connections eliminate the double-angle double-sided shared-bolt erection problem, since there is only one angle [03:17]. Seated connections (both unstiffened and stiffened) are favored for erection safety: the beam is set on the seat and bolted down, with no double-sided bolt access issue, then a stability top angle (minimum 1/4 in) is installed to keep the beam from rolling [55:22, 64:09, 73:00]. Seated connections are specifically convenient at a column web [56:26, 64:40]. Single angle connections are not recommended for laterally unbraced beams such as in industrial framing [03:47].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The speaker flagged outdated handout slides: a deleted Table 17-1 reference for the single-angle eccentric coefficient [10:28] and a weld-eccentricity dimension that earlier slides located to the wrong point [62:37]. A conceptual error addressed in Q&A is assuming no moment on the bolts because plowing puts the rotation at the bolt line; test data over 20 to 30 years show real bolt eccentricity, so eccentric bolt shear is still checked even though it changes the result very little [83:14, 84:16]. No NDE or weld-defect rejection criteria were discussed; the failure modes covered are design limit states (flexural rupture, block shear, plate buckling, shear-flexure interaction, web punching shear), not field inspection rejections.

### Bid / Cost Implications
Connection-type selection is a cost lever: shear tabs are the simple, few-part default, but only carry low to moderate reaction, so a handful of higher-reaction beams must escalate to double angles or tees, which costs more parts and labor [13:30, 14:36]. Single angle connections trade fewer parts against possibly larger angles, bolts, or welds because bolts act in single shear [03:17]. Seated connections add a part (the top angle) but speed erection [55:52]. The manual design aids (Tables 10-8, 10-9, 9-4, 15-3, and the eccentric C tables) exist to cut engineering time, which is real estimating value when these connections repeat across a job [09:26, 67:51].
