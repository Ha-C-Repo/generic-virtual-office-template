### Core Concept
This is session three of AISC Night School "Seismic Design in Steel," written and presented by Rafael Sabelli of Walter P Moore, covering the seismic design of braced frames [00:03]. The controlling idea is capacity design: the brace is the ductile fuse, and every other element (connections, columns, beams, gussets, base plates) is proportioned to resist the maximum force the brace can deliver so the fuse acts as a fuse [10:50][20:36][44:42]. Three ductile systems are taught. The special concentrically braced frame (SCBF) gets its inelastic drift from brace tension yielding (expected strength RyFyAg) and controlled compression buckling [07:17][13:21]. The buckling-restrained braced frame (BRBF) uses a steel core that yields in both tension and compression because a sleeve plus grout and a bond interrupter prevents the core from buckling, giving nearly symmetric behavior [36:32][39:06]. The ordinary concentrically braced frame (OCBF) instead limits inelastic demand through higher strength and a low R factor of 3.25, and is used mainly for low industrial and metal-type buildings [03:09][62:09].

### Technical Details
Braces are sized for their compression capacity because earthquake load reverses, which creates overstrength since tension capacity (RyFy) can be two to three times compression capacity; that overstrength is part of the R factor [09:18][10:19]. SCBF braces have high compactness limits (thicker HSS walls spread strain and resist local kinking) and slenderness limits [17:30][24:45]. Buckling plane is controlled through end fixity (pinned in one plane, fixed in the orthogonal) and brace shape (round HSS has equal radius of gyration both axes; a wide flange has a clear weak axis), so designers tune K and r per axis to force buckling in the chosen plane [25:15][27:18]. Two plastic mechanism analyses are required for SCBF: condition one uses maximum tension (RyFy) and maximum compression (1.14 times design, capped at RyFy) for columns, gussets, and base plates; condition two uses post-buckling compression at 0.3 times maximum (0.342 when multiplied out) to capture unbalanced beam forces and drag-through forces [50:20][51:21][105:00]. BRBF needs only one mechanism analysis since tension and compression are similar; maximum tension is omega times RyFyAg (strain hardening 30 to 50 percent) and maximum compression is beta times omega times RyFyAg (compression overstrength 10 to 25 percent), both from testing [46:47][96:00]. Prohibited and penalized configurations: K-bracing into a column mid-height is prohibited for SCBF, BRBF, and OCBF; single diagonals are allowed only with the overstrength factor of 2; tension-only bracing is allowed only for OCBF [64:42][70:00][65:12].

### Fabrication Notes
A1085 HSS is preferred for seismic projects because of tighter dimensional controls and a higher Ry of 1.25; the speaker advises verifying that the chosen HSS shape is actually available through AISC service center listings or the fabricator [74:56]. For brace-to-gusset slots, the slot for gusset width should be one eighth inch wider than the plate (not one sixteenth) so the erector can maneuver the brace while keeping fillet welds small enough to bridge the gap [73:23]. Slot length should be about 2 inches longer than the final position to give erection maneuverability, and the design should anticipate the brace ending anywhere within a 2 inch zone with sufficient lap and reinforcement length [73:53]. Gusset fold-line detailing: minimum width of the linear fold line is twice the gusset thickness, with a recommended extra inch for tolerance; the knife-plate method uses about three times the knife-plate thickness [31:26][65:00]. Special welds with toughness requirements are required at column splices and at the ordinary-moment-frame beam-to-column connections used to accommodate rotation [67:47].

### Erection Notes
Erection tolerance drives the slot detailing: the deliberate one eighth inch slot oversize and the 2 inch extra slot length both exist to let the erector get the brace into place [73:23][73:53]. Brace buckling produces large out-of-plane movement (rule of thumb: about 10 percent of brace length), so the field layout and architectural coordination must keep the brace clear of sensitive elements such as glass curtain walls; configuring in-plane buckling can avoid hitting a facade [69:19][70:19]. Critically, the gusset fold line that allows the brace to buckle must not be restrained by concrete composite deck or a ground-floor slab cast against it, which would defeat the hinge [70:51]. Protected zones cover essentially the entire brace and connection; do not connect metal studs, handrails, or other attachments in areas of anticipated inelastic strain since that creates a fracture initiation point [71:51][72:21].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named connection limit states to check: brace net section fracture, block shear fracture in the brace and in the gussets, weld fracture at interfaces, and gusset buckling [55:27]. A specific bad practice called out is using very thick gusset plates with very thin beam webs, which the University of Washington guidance warns against [55:57]. Designing a connection weaker than the brace is a primary failure: the connection fractures before the brace yields, causing sudden loss of frame strength and stiffness [11:20]. Restraining the gusset fold line with a slab, or attaching architecture to the protected zone, are detailing errors that prevent the intended ductile buckling and can create falling hazards [70:51][71:21]. Column fracture under the rotational demand of a gusseted fixed beam-to-column connection was observed in the UC Berkeley test program [59:02]. Designing the middle column of a brace bay for near-zero seismic axial (because elastic analysis shows it at the neutral axis) is a mistake; plastic mechanism analysis gives the real unbalanced design force [49:19].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The unbalanced vertical force in inverted-V and V (chevron) configurations forces a substantial beam, so chevron framing drives heavy beams into the SCBF system and adds tonnage [63:42][64:12]. BRBF braces are proprietary specialty items (the speaker names Nippon Steel, Star Seismic acquired by CoreBrace as generic vendor examples) and in practice must be bought from a manufacturer that has done the required testing, which means specifying a brace not from a specialty manufacturer is difficult and the brace design is largely the vendor's domain [40:42][86:22]. Multi-tiered braced frames are noted as generally not economical when done as SCBF, with BRBF the better choice for that geometry [66:46]. Capacity design inflates connection, column, base plate, and beam sizing well above the elastic demand, so these frames carry connection-heavy detailing and material premiums that must be priced.
