### Core Concept
This is AISC Night School session three, Behavior of Flexural Members, presented by Ron Ziemian of the Structural Stability Research Council [00:01]. The lecture covers the strength limit states of beams: full cross-section yielding to the plastic moment Mp, and member instability by lateral torsional buckling (LTB), both elastic and inelastic [02:33]. The throughline is that a long-enough unbraced compression flange behaves like a column and buckles laterally while twisting, so beam capacity drops as unbraced length grows. It is theory and behavior, not a fabrication or erection lecture; the design-application companion was deferred to Todd Helwig the following week [84:50].

### Technical Details
Yielding moves through the section depth from the flanges inward, and the plastic-to-elastic section modulus ratio (shape factor Z/S) is only about 1.15 to 1.2 for wide flange shapes [09:11]. LTB couples minor-axis bending with twist; the resisting torque is the sum of Saint-Venant torsion (GJ, small for open shapes) and warping torsion (E Cw, the dominant term for wide flanges), with Cw approximated as Iy h squared over 4 [37:43]. The worked comparison of a W8x13 open shape (J = 0.0871) against an equal-area closed tube (J = 22.95) shows closed sections carry roughly 264 times the torsion, which is why HSS rarely governs by LTB [29:36]. Residual stresses from differential cooling run on the order of 30% of Fy and pull first yield below My, driving inelastic LTB [62:48]. The Cb factor scales the uniform-moment solution; AISC is conservative on Cb relative to Eurocode but liberal on the inelastic curve, so the codes wash out to similar capacity once moment gradient is included [83:18].
### Fabrication Notes
No shop fabrication, fit-up, or QC content. The only fabrication-adjacent point is metallurgical: hot-rolled wide flange shapes lock in residual stresses near 30% of Fy because the flange tips cool and set before the web-flange junction, which the section then carries as locked-in compression at the tips and tension at the core [62:48].
### Erection Notes
The one direct field caution is about handling long, deep girders: if a crew releases the hoisting cable before torsionally securing the girder ends, the member can pick up torque under its own self-weight, twist, and tip over [58:44]. The lecture stresses that beam ends must be braced against twist at supports or the section will roll over once it buckles. Detailed bracing design was deferred to lectures 7 and 8 [88:26].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
This is a behavior lecture, so the named failures are design and analysis errors rather than weld or bolt rejections. The headline error is treating an inflection point as a brace point: a 40-ft W24x68 in reverse curvature is far stronger with a real torsional brace at midspan (shorter unbraced length) than if the inflection point is assumed to brace it, even though adding the brace lowers Cb from 2.27 to 1.67 [77:07]. A second error is forgetting that adding a brace creates two unbraced segments that each need a separate Lb and Cb check; the longer span does not automatically govern [75:37]. A third is releasing rigging before securing girder ends against twist [58:44].
### Bid / Cost Implications
No direct pricing, schedule, or estimating content. The implied takeaway for cost is that unbraced length controls beam capacity, so brace spacing decisions in detailing affect required member sizes and therefore tonnage. Using Cb = 1 is always conservative but leaves capacity on the table when moment gradient exists [75:06].
