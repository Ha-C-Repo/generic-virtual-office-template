## 01 22 16 History of the AISC Specification

### Core Concept
This is an informal history of the AISC Specification told by Dr. Theodore Galambos, who has sat on the AISC Committee on Specifications for more than 40 years [00:31]. The core lesson is why a single national steel specification exists at all: at AISC's 1921 founding the construction industry had numerous and different proprietary manuals, and the inaugural objective was to establish a single code authority recognized by building-code bodies and designers to eliminate that confusion [04:43]. The talk traces how the document grew from a 23-page pocket volume with a 9-page spec costing 25 cents [05:13] to roughly today's size, driven by new research, new steels, and new construction practices. The recurring theme for a fabricator is that every allowable, column curve, and connection rule in current AISC 360 carries decades of test-backed history, not arbitrary numbers.
### Spec References
The speaker names editions and methods by year rather than the clause numbering a modern detailer cites. Named items: the first AISC specification (1921, 23-page pocket volume, 9-page spec) [05:13]; the first AISC manual (about 1928, 476 pages, $2) [08:18]; the 1936, 1949, 1961, 1963, 1969, 1978, and 1989 ASD editions [12:24] onward; the 1986, 1993, and 1999 LRFD specifications [52:27]; the combined LRFD-plus-ASD unified specification that followed [52:59]; the 1989 ninth-edition ASD green book [53:29]; and the 2016 specification where the Q-factors are retired and effective length is moved into an appendix (he recalls it as "appendix 7") [39:21]. He also cites ASCE 7 load factors superseding the old one-third wind/seismic stress increase [31:56], and AASHTO composite-bridge standards from 1957 that fed AISC's 1963 composite rules [44:35]. No AISC 360 section number, AISC 341 seismic provision, AWS D1.1 clause, RCSC clause, or numbered Design Guide is cited verbatim; this is editions and methods, general principles, no section cited.
### Technical Details
Column strength history runs from the Rankine first-yield curve used roughly 50 years [10:21], to the 1936-1949 two-branch parabola-plus-Rankine curve splitting primary and secondary members at L/r [18:42], to the 1961-63 curve built on the tangent-modulus average of strong- and weak-axis buckling of a W8x31 with a variable factor of safety from 1.67 to 1.92 (5/3 to 23/12) [19:47], to the present curve covering both initial imperfections and residual stresses, derived from reanalyzed Lehigh tests and simplified by Ray Tide [21:55]. Lateral-torsional buckling moved from a Rankine-type allowable to the 1949 Ld/bt formula from Bethlehem Steel research, then to the Timoshenko elastic-buckling formulation split into St. Venant and warping-restraint terms, taking the maximum of two formulas [22:59]. Combined axial-plus-bending stress evolved from simple addition to the interaction ratio formula, then the 1961-63 Cm moment-gradient factor and the 1/(1 - fa/Fe') amplification with the effective-length term [27:43]. Slender-element Q-factors arrived in 1969 borrowed from the cold-formed specification [30:19]. Plastic design entered in 1963 with moment redistribution [32:26], and post-buckling (tension-field) plate-girder strength, block shear, and compactness limits all came from Lehigh research [37:46].
### Fabrication Notes
This is a history talk, so it gives no shop tolerances, fit-up rules, or QC procedures. The only fabrication-adjacent thread is the long shift from riveted to welded construction: welded plate girders replaced riveted ones after Lehigh testing [37:14], and the talk notes the political "noise abatement of riveting" complaints that helped push welding forward [41:25]. Welding and high-strength bolts entered the specification in 1949 along with friction connections [35:36]. General principles, no fabrication tolerance or section cited.

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The talk frames "errors" historically rather than as modern inspection rejections. The original problem was inconsistent column formulas where the basic unit stress was not a consistent index of the so-called factor of safety, with allowable strength varying by about 10 ksi depending on which manual you used [09:20]. Block shear and certain compactness limits were written specifically as responses to observed failures [37:46]. A recalled full-scale steel-joist floor test at a John Deere plant in Davenport, Iowa failed when the first compression member buckled, and that embarrassment drove Steel Joist Institute specification changes [56:38]. Ponding failures prompted the ponding provisions [33:01].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Economy is a stated through-line: the speaker says the specification was always concerned about economy, and the growth in provisions reflects wider use across more industries, not an attempt to "make things fat" [54:31]. Incomplete (partial) composite action was adopted in 1969 specifically because it gave designers economy [34:31], and plastic design was promoted because it gives a better understanding of behavior under load and yields economy [47:42]. The takeaway for estimating is that method choice (ASD versus LRFD, full versus partial composite, plastic versus elastic) historically changed member sizes and therefore tonnage and cost.
