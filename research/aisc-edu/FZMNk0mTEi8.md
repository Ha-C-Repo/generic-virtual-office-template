## Field Fixes - Part 1
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FZMNk0mTEi8 | Duration: 29:21 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This is a two-speaker AISC seminar on diagnosing and fixing structural steel problems found in the field, with the structural engineer and the fabricator presenting as a team because the designer creates the problem and the fabricator has to fix it [02:01]. Part 1 covers a decision process for any field problem followed by a deep section on footings and anchor rods, which the speaker says cause some sort of trouble on almost every project [02:30]. The governing theme is that a field fix is a money and schedule event before it is an engineering event, so the engineer must act fast, verify the proposed fix, and document and price the work.
### Spec References
ASTM F1554 named as the current proper anchor-rod specification, explicitly replacing the older ASTM A307 [16:21] (slide 8). F1554 three grades cited: 36 ksi, 55 ksi, and 105 ksi (slide 9), with Supplement S1 to obtain weldable 55 ksi material [17:53]. Product-marking color code stated verbatim from the slide: 36 ksi blue, 55 ksi yellow, 105 ksi red. OSHA erection rules cited generally: minimum four anchor rods per column except minor posts, and anchor rods designed for a 300 lb person at 18 in eccentricity from the column face [19:03]; OSHA also requires SER approval before any anchor rod is repaired or replaced [19:51]. AISC recommended anchor-rod hole sizes referenced as being in the then-new 13th edition manual and the prior LRFD manual [27:37]. IBC cited generally for special inspection of adhesive anchors (slide 15). No AISC 360 section, no AWS D1.1 clause, and no RCSC reference were cited.
### Technical Details
F1554 grades carry distinct yield strengths and dimensional limits: 36 ksi and 55 ksi rods are available up to 4 in diameter, 105 ksi up to 3 in diameter (slide 9). The speaker's standing recommendation is to use fewer, larger, lower-strength rods rather than many small high-strength rods, because four rods are far easier to place accurately than eight, ten, or twelve [18:44]. A worked anchor-rod strength comparison from slide 15: a 3/4 in F1554 Grade 36 rod has a design tensile strength of 14,310 lb, and a 3/4 in adhesive anchor at 5.25 in embedment in 4000 psi concrete reaches 12,630 lb, nearly matching it, which is why cutting bad cast-in rods and re-drilling adhesive anchors is a viable fix. The term anchor rod, not anchor bolt, is deliberate AISC usage because treating them as bolts led to rods too short and base-plate holes sized as bolt holes with too little clearance [14:21].
### Fabrication Notes
The classic fabrication-side error shown is a template made wrong: the engineer dimensioned center-of-template to anchor-rod as 8 in, the template maker read it as 8 in center-to-center of rods, and the pattern was built to the wrong spacing [22:52]. Fixes that land in the shop include cutting the column off its base plate and fabricating a new base plate sized to the as-built rod pattern, then rewelding it to the column, or relocating the column on the base plate [24:05] (slide 14). The speaker calls survey-before-column-fabrication "baloney" in practice because columns are already being fabricated by the time rods go in [26:37], so the realistic shop posture is to expect some rework. He also flags that base plates can be re-fabricated with long thread length on the rods to absorb position tolerance [29:12].
### Erection Notes
The headline erection-safety lesson is a Boise collapse: an erector tied two roughly 45 ft columns with a beam, went to lunch, a 30 mph wind came up, one beam was not tied off, it slipped, and it pulled the building down [13:14]. Anchor rods commonly break in an erection collapse and crews blame "green concrete," but here the rods held and the footing tipped over [15:24]. The core point is that structures are designed for the completed state, not for erection forces or a falling beam, so erection stability is the erector's responsibility. The four-rod-plus-300 lb OSHA rule was meant to let a worker stand 18 in off the column face safely but gave erectors a false sense of column stability [19:34]. Field layout should use a qualified field engineer or qualified surveyor, symmetric rod patterns such as 8x8 or 12x12 rather than 6x8, and wood or steel templates firmly fastened to the footing or pier so they do not move during the pour [26:51] (slide 16).
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Anchor rods cast in the wrong position is called the recurring problem the industry will never fully eliminate [23:43] (slide 11). Named failures include the wrong template-dimension reading that put 1,300 anchor rods out of place and forced a re-drill to adhesive anchors with a back charge [22:57], anchorage poured roughly 12 ft off location, and a 30 by 40 by 8 ft monster mill footing poured in the wrong spot that had to be blown out [22:25]. Field torching and slotting of base-plate holes to force rods to fit is shown as a common field expedient, sometimes accepted after evaluation using plate washers over the enlarged holes [20:11] (slide 11). A footing-tipping case led to the engineer being sued for an undersized footing even after an erector substituted larger 1-1/2 in rods for the specified four 3/4 in rods on his own [15:00].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The dominant cost in any field fix is field labor, not material; material is described as almost free by comparison, and the erector charges large dollars for any field work [08:20]. Delay charges drive the urgency: a crane sitting idle can cost thousands of dollars a day, so acting immediately is the lever that controls total cost [06:22]. The speaker repeatedly tells the engineer to discuss fee up front, get a PO, and not absorb the 4,000 to 5,000 dollars of calculation effort silently when costs are divvied up [10:16]. A fix proposed by the erector or fabricator usually prices lower than an engineer-originated alternative, because the party whose idea it is prices it as they see fit and someone else's idea almost always costs more [12:30]. Sometimes the cheapest answer is no fix at all: if no money is spent on a fix there is no argument over who pays [07:30].
