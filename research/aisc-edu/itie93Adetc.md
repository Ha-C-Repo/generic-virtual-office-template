## Is It Likely My Design Will Fail?
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=itie93Adetc | Duration: 57:51 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Ron Hamburger (Simpson Gumpertz and Heger, past Higgins lecturer) teaches a method to estimate the annual probability of failure of a member whose demand-capacity ratio (DCR) exceeds 1.0, instead of just writing "OK" at the bottom of a calc sheet [03:07][05:13]. LRFD separates a log-normal load (demand) distribution from a log-normal resistance distribution; the overlap is the notional failure probability, and a code-conforming DCR of 1.0 already builds in large margins [06:13][18:38]. The procedure: pull the target probability of failure at DCR=1.0 from ASCE 7, estimate the combined dispersion of load and resistance, then use Excel LOGNORM.DIST to back out the failure probability at the actual DCR [21:16][35:39]. This is a design/evaluation engineering lecture, not a fabrication or erection lecture.
### Spec References
The speaker cited specific documents: ASCE 7 (editions 7-05, 7-10, 7-16) Tables 1.3.1 and 1.3.2 for target reliability / probability of failure by risk category and failure mode [20:46]; ASCE 7 Chapter 1 reliability tables and the Chapter 2 commentary on load and resistance factor derivation [10:20][14:03]; NBS Special Publication 577 (Ellingwood, National Bureau of Standards, 1977) for load and resistance dispersions [27:54][28:25]; and a Galambos paper giving bias and coefficient-of-variation values for steel members [28:55][30:58]. No AISC 360 section numbers, AISC 341 seismic provisions, AWS D1.1 clauses, or RCSC bolting sections were cited; this talk stays in reliability theory and ASCE 7 load combinations.
### Technical Details
A code DCR of 1.0 for a simply supported steel beam in flexure (dead plus live) carries an annual failure probability near 3 x 10^-5 [10:50][32:31]. The worked example: a beam at DCR 1.07, 10 percent dead / 90 percent live demand, combines dead dispersion 10 percent, live dispersion 25 percent, and modeling dispersion 9 percent (root-sum-square) to a load variability of 25 percent; resistance variability for an elastic beam is 12 percent; total dispersion 0.28 [33:02][34:04][34:37]. Solving in Excel gives a median DCR near 3.1 and a failure probability of 7 x 10^-5 at DCR 1.07, roughly twice the code target but still low [35:39][36:11]. For seismic, ASCE 7 sets a 10 percent failure probability at maximum considered earthquake (MCE) for risk category II, dispersion 0.60; design-level shaking (two-thirds MCE) then yields about 2.5 percent [40:22][41:53][42:26]. A 50 percent material yield deficiency in an old structure was shown to give a 21 percent failure probability under design shaking [42:57].
### Fabrication Notes
Fabrication is not the subject of this lecture. The one relevant aside is material-strength bias from the mill: A572 Grade 50 steel has a 50 ksi specified minimum yield but actual heats often run 58 to 60 ksi, and there is no upper cap on yield, so the resistance distribution is skewed upward and gives the design 20 to 30 percent of unstated reserve [13:32][25:50]. Mills remelt heats that test below the minimum, which is why the lower tail is controlled and the upper tail is not.

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The recurring error named is the reflexive "OK" written next to a DCR of 1.05 or 1.07 without quantifying the actual risk [03:07][05:43]. A second is misreading the Excel LOGNORM.DIST inputs: its "mean" argument wants the log of the median value and its "standard deviation" argument wants the coefficient of variation, not the literal mean and standard deviation [27:24][27:54]. The speaker warns that accepting DCRs of 1.2 to 1.3 routinely is a real failure path: across 100,000 members over a 50 to 100 year life, one or more will fail even at 0.1 percent annual probability each [37:48]. He also flags that building officials will not approve a sub-code DCR even under alternative-means provisions, because it is demonstrably less safe than code [48:13].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The driver is the cost of strengthening a member discovered to be overstressed after fabrication or after finishes are installed [04:43][46:07]. A late beam fix can require demolition, delay building opening, and cost in the tens of thousands; the example cites a roughly 20,000 dollar contractor fix [46:37][47:08]. The reliability method gives the engineer and client a basis to decide whether to spend that money or accept a quantified, low residual risk, and the lecture stresses that the client, not the engineer alone, should own that risk-versus-cost decision [44:30][52:09].
