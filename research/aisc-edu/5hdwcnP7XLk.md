## AISC Design Guide 31    Castellated and Cellular Beam Design
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5hdwcnP7XLk | Duration: 1:07:27 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This webinar walks through AISC Design Guide 31, which documents US state-of-practice design for castellated beams (hexagonal web openings) and cellular beams (round web openings) and conforms to the 2017 AISC spec [01:34][04:39]. Both are fabricated by splitting a rolled wide-flange along its web, staggering the two halves, and welding them back together to create a deeper section from the same material, increasing depth by about 50 percent so the section gains bending stiffness because depth is cubed in moment of inertia [06:12][07:13]. The presenters (Dave Deinhart, Sam Ferris, John Colson) stress that the single biggest benefit is stiffness, so these members make sense on long spans (about 40 ft and up) and heavier loads, not on short stiff-controlled spans [13:50][15:23].
### Spec References
The speakers cited AISC 360-16 throughout: vertical shear strength per Spec Section G2 [32:28], T-section compressive strength per Chapter E3 [41:12], T-section flexural strength per Section F9.1 (yielding) and Section F9.2 (lateral-torsional buckling) [43:19], and the combined axial-plus-flexure interaction per Equations H1-1a and H1-1b [43:49]. Welded fabrication is per AWS D1.1 [31:57]. Design Guide 31 itself is the governing document. Web-post buckling equations for castellated beams trace to Dr. Redwood's McGill University testing, and the cellular-beam web-post equations to the Steel Construction Institute of England [35:31][37:37].
### Technical Details
Castellated geometry uses a cutting angle phi held between 58 and 62 degrees, with geometric limits E greater than or equal to 3 in, opening height over E less than 8, and E over web thickness less than 30 [23:00][24:00]. Cellular geometric limits are spacing over hole diameter between 1.08 and 1.5, and gross depth over diameter between 1.25 and 1.75, with E at least 3 in [25:34]. Unlike a wide-flange where you check max moment and max shear once, these beams require checking moment-shear interaction at every opening and every web post [27:16]. The full limit-state list is compactness and local buckling, vertical shear at gross and net section, horizontal shear yielding in the web post, web-post buckling, Vierendeel bending of the tees, axial tension and compression in the tees, overall flexural strength, and lateral-torsional buckling if the top flange is unbraced [30:21][31:27]. Composite asymmetric sections converge effective depth over three or four iterations, and full versus partial composite action is checked opening by opening, not once for the whole beam [44:50][48:27]. A castellated section is named like CB30x44 (gross depth x weight) and cellular like LB30x44, with asymmetric forms such as CB30x44/50 [07:44][11:48].
### Fabrication Notes
A castellated beam is made with a single zigzag cut that yields two halves and a little end waste; a cellular beam takes twice the cutting (two passes) and drops a waste sliver from the middle [06:42][10:15]. When split, the tees tend to banana from internal stress and are straightened back flat on the table before staggering and welding [11:18]. Asymmetric composite sections combine a lighter top beam and a heavier bottom beam (rule of thumb roughly 20 percent heavier bottom), so two parent shapes are needed and an odd beam count leaves a spare top or bottom [08:44][14:22]. Cellular depth varies with spacing, so the beam supplier needs a spacing range rather than a single S to fit a profile to the beam length, and the depth call (LB24) is nominal and can run plus or minus [54:39][55:41]. The lone named US fabricator was SteelFab of Charlotte NC, with Arcelor (Belgium) and Westok (UK) named in Europe [56:43].
### Erection Notes
The webinar does not cover field erection sequence, crane, rigging, or bracing in any detail. Its erection-economy point is that long-span castellated framing reduces erection piece count, sometimes by nearly half, which the presenters argue is where the real cost savings sit because piece handling, not weight per square foot, drives erection cost [16:23][16:54].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Failure modes named are web-post yielding, web-post buckling (which carries a twist, distinguishing it from local web crippling), web yielding at the fillet, web crippling, and horizontal weld rupture along the weld line [29:51][30:21]. A design error flagged was specifying a single hole spacing instead of a range, which makes it hard for the supplier to fit a profile [55:41]. The closing research story is a cautionary material failure: a 3-in thick end plate fractured prematurely under cyclic seismic loading because Charpy through-thickness toughness was below 10 ft-lb where 70-plus was expected, traced to thick plate not manufactured properly [65:28][66:29].
### Bid / Cost Implications
There is an up-front fabrication cost premium for cutting and re-welding, so the steel savings have to be large enough to clear it; the rule of thumb is the parent beam should be roughly 40 lb/ft over a roughly 40 ft span before castellating pays, because saving 30 percent on a 14 lb beam is not enough steel to beat the manufacturing cost [15:23][15:53]. The presenters argue against judging these by tonnage per square foot alone and toward total installed cost including reduced piece count [16:23]. A 40 ft beam can require on the order of 350 individual calculations (160 bending, 72 shear, 26 web-post buckling, plus LTB and 100 deflection checks), so design is software-driven via tools such as Bentley RAM SBeam and RAM Steel [53:36][54:07]. Parking structures at 60 ft clear spans are called the single best application, sometimes cheaper than precast [19:59][20:29].
