## Stability Design of Low- and Medium-Rise Steel Buildings
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hrcunOPP7DA | Duration: 1:34:45 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This is AISC Night School Session 6, presented by Dr. Donald W. White of Georgia Tech, teaching stability design of low- and medium-rise steel buildings using modern analysis methods [00:02]. The controlling idea is estimating the sidesway amplifier B2 from serviceability and code drift limits rather than guessing it, then using that B2 in member design [02:06, 37:21]. White works one example building (a 4-story office near Atlantic Station in Atlanta) end to end: braced frames in the north-south direction designed by the effective length method, and moment frames in the east-west direction designed by the direct analysis method [05:48, 22:09, 23:10]. The recurring lesson is that for practically all multi-story buildings under wind and earthquake combinations, the structure comes out stability non-critical with B2 at or below 1.7, so notional loads are not required with the lateral combination [76:28, 78:30].

### Technical Details
The braced frames are vertically cantilevered trusses designed for axial force only, neglecting secondary moments [60:42]. Sidesway amplifier B2 is built from a required minimum story lateral stiffness, taken as the larger demand from three limits: a first-order service wind drift (assumed L/500 at a 10-year MRI wind, which ASCE 7 puts at 0.44 of the strength-level nominal wind) [38:24, 39:27, 79:35]; a seismic drift limit converted to elastic drift through Cd = 3, giving an elastic ratio of 1/120 [40:59, 41:30]; and the ASCE 7 seismic P-Delta limit, which for braced frames with beta = 1 and Cd = 3 yields theta_max = 0.167 and PL_story_min = 6 x Px [42:30, 43:35]. Px uses 1.0 dead + 0.5 live + 0 snow with no individual load factor above 1.0 per 12.8.7 [44:06, 49:51]. For the moment frames he back-calculated a first-order wind drift limit (L/593) needed to hold a target second-order drift of L/400 using Bill LeMessurier's 1977 EJ method [62:48, 68:59, 70:35], used Rm = 0.94 for P-small-delta and the 0.8 stiffness reduction of the direct analysis method [66:27, 73:16]. A live-load-reduction technique applies equivalent upward joint loads so the global second-order analysis is not run "filled to the gills" with unreduced live load, trimming B2 from 1.24 to 1.21 on the braced frame [50:54, 58:07, 59:39].
### Fabrication Notes
This is an analysis and design webinar, so explicit shop fabrication, fit-up, and QC processes were not covered. The only fabrication-relevant outputs are the selected member sizes: a W12x53 braced-frame bottom-story column (W10x54 or W14x61 as alternates) [61:46], a 9x9x1/4 HSS diagonal brace [62:18], a W14x68 moment-frame column [81:45], and W24x62 then W24x68 moment-frame spandrels after a drift-driven upsize [85:23, 90:00]. Column splices occur at the third-story level on both the braced and moment frames [06:49].
### Erection Notes
Field erection procedures, sequencing, crane and rigging, and bracing were not addressed in this design-focused session. The structural idealization assumes rigid diaphragms distributing lateral load to the perimeter braced and moment frames, with all interior gravity ("leaning," "dummy," or "gravity") columns providing no lateral resistance and leaning on the lateral system through the diaphragm [04:45, 13:36, 47:12]. That diaphragm-transfer assumption is the load path an erector and detailer must preserve.
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
No bolting, welding, or NDE inspection failures were named; this session does not touch shop or field QC. The design-side cautions White raised are the analysis equivalents: analyzing the frame with full unreduced live load overstates second-order effects [58:37, 59:39]; the gravity-load B1/B2 shortcut he used "gets you into trouble" if the frame has significant sidesway under gravity (his example was symmetric, so it was safe) [60:42]; and if B2 exceeds 1.7 the frame is stability-critical and you must add a notional lateral load to the seismic combination [74:21]. He also flagged a copy-paste typo on his own stiffness slide as a reminder to check the numbers [87:58, 89:28].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The cost lever in this talk is member weight driven by drift, not strength. White states that braced-frame members are usually governed by strength while a meaningful fraction of moment-frame members are governed by lateral stiffness [23:40], so moment-frame tonnage can grow late in design when a story fails its drift check (his spandrel jumped from W24x62 to W24x68 purely to add stiffness) [89:28, 90:00]. The live-load-reduction-by-equivalent-upward-load method buys a smaller B2, which matters most on flexible moment frames with large amplification and can reduce required member sizes [59:08, 60:09]. His rule of thumb for which member to grow (columns when G is much less than 1, girders when G is much greater than 1, both near G = 1) is a fast way to converge a design without overspending on steel [89:28].
