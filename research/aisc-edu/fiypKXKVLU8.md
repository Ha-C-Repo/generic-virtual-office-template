## Analysis and Design of Chevron Brace Connections with Flat Bar Gussets: Part 2
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fiypKXKVLU8 | Duration: 1:34:46 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
Patrick J. Fortney (Cives Engineering, vice chair of the AISC seismic design manual committee) extends Part 1 to seismic chevron brace connections, the V and inverted-V configurations in a Special Concentrically Braced Frame (SCBF) [04:10][05:12]. The governing idea is capacity design: the connection and the frame are not designed for elastic analysis forces but for the expected strength the brace can deliver, so the brace itself becomes the energy-dissipating fuse that buckles and yields in a code-level event [07:13][89:54]. The work flows through expected tension yield (RyFyAg), expected buckling strength, expected post-buckling strength (30 percent of buckling), a hinge/fold line that accommodates out-of-plane brace buckling, protected zones, and a mechanistic two-load-case analysis of the beam [12:19][16:56][20:06].

### Technical Details
For the worked example the braces are HSS4x4x3/8 and HSS5x5x3/8 in A500 Gr. B (Ry 1.4, Rt 1.3), gussets and plate A572 Gr. 50 (Ry 1.1, Rt 1.2) [10:47][27:21]. Expected tension is RyFyAg = 398 kips; expected buckling uses Ry inside Fe, giving 1.1 times buckling = 176 kips for connection design, and post-buckling = 0.3 times 176 = 52.8 kips [28:24][32:09][33:11]. Buckling length is end-of-brace to end-of-brace, not work point (here 197 in after subtracting an assumed 36 in pull-off), with KL/r = 105 against the 4.71 limit of 118 [13:22][29:59]. The hinge/fold line is drawn perpendicular to the brace line of action, offset 2t (two times gusset thickness), crossing the free edges; he treats 2t as a minimum clearance and provides extra for fabrication and erection tolerance [14:22][56:12]. Connection limit-state checks shown: weld brace-to-gusset (401 vs 398), shear rupture of brace walls using design wall thickness 0.349 in with Rt (853 kips), gusset buckling via Whitmore width with K=0.65 and Fcr from Table 4-22 without Ry, gusset tension yield (402), net-section rupture with computed X-bar 1.64 and U=0.909, and the gusset-to-beam weld using 2M/L converted to an equivalent normal force [34:46][72:00][51:27].

### Fabrication Notes
The brace is slotted over the gusset; the slot width is gusset thickness plus 1/8 in (e.g. 3/4 in gusset gives a 7/8 in slot) so an over-rolled or undercut slot still slides on, centering the brace with about a 1/16 in gap each side [42:31][87:00]. The reinforcing plate must restore the slotted net section: its effective net area must be at least the brace gross area and its Fy at least the brace Fy (Gr. 50 plate over Fy 46 brace satisfies this) [15:23][41:27]. Plate-to-brace welds are sized on the plate expected strength (Ry 1.1, here 55.6 vs 46.4 kips) and run on each side of the reduced section with enough length overlap [15:53][41:58]. The X dimension (slot beyond the gusset edge) drives the reinforcing-plate length on shop drawings, and the detailer must verify the HSS workable flat so the plate sits flush and there is room to weld both sides [16:24][42:31].

### Erection Notes
The X slot dimension must be large enough that the erector can drop the brace into place [16:24]. The 2t hinge clearance carries a deliberate tolerance margin so field fit-up does not pinch below 2t and obstruct the intended brace buckling [56:12][57:50]. Protected zones (gusset, center quarter of the brace, and a zone of length B equal to the out-of-plane brace depth adjacent to each connection) must be shown not only on structural drawings but explicitly on shop drawings and erection drawings, with no welded attachments, studs, or direction aids placed in them [16:56][59:23][60:26].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Fortney names several traps. Pulling Fcr from Table 4-22 for the brace buckling check is wrong because the table values omit the Ry factor needed for expected strength [32:09]. Double-counting the phi factor by reapplying it to Table 4-22 values that already embed it [36:49]. Forgetting that connection-design compression carries a 1.1 amplifier while the frame mechanistic analysis does not, so the beam analysis divides the connection compression by 1.1 [20:37][61:28]. Using Section J4.2 instead of G2 for the connection-region (panel) shear can miss the G2-1 h/tw slenderness limit and overstate strength [77:13]. He also flags omitting beam axial-flexure interaction when the brace horizontal components are carried through the beam to the column rather than dumped into the diaphragm (Chapter H1 needed) [78:50][81:23]. Welded attachments inside a protected zone create heat-affected zones and stress hotspots that degrade ductility [73:26].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The mechanistic analysis can force a large beam upsize: the example W24x84 was inadequate in bending (needed 1700 kip-ft, only 840 available) and jumped to a W24x176 (Zx 511 vs 462 required), which Ivan must capture as real tonnage when a chevron SCBF appears in a takeoff [66:11][68:48]. Fortney notes a deeper replacement beam shifts connection geometry and pull-off, so a re-iteration may be needed, adding engineering time [69:51]. On gusset economy he states from running dozens of cases and a paper with Bill Thornton that elliptical yield lines are not more cost-effective than a compact linear fold line with a 10-degree Whitmore spread or a hinge-plate gusset; a flat-bar gusset can work seismically but usually needs shaping to get economical plate thickness and weld length [50:22][24:13][88:15]. He also notes that combining the two gussets into one can be more or less demanding on the beam case by case, with no general rule [82:27][85:03].
