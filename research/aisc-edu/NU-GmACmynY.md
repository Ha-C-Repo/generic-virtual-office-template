## Seismic Design of Ductile Special Concentrically Braced Frames
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NU-GmACmynY | Duration: 1:38:02 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
This University of Toronto webinar (Constantine Christopoulos and Jeff Packer) teaches capacity-based seismic design of Special Concentrically Braced Frames (SCBF). The governing idea is the ductile fuse: the brace yields in tension and buckles inelastically in compression as the energy-dissipating element, and every other element (beams, columns, gusset connections, foundations) is sized to remain elastic against the brace's true developed force [13:19][41:02]. The system uses an R factor of 6, an overstrength factor Omega of 2, and a deflection amplification CD of 5, so the structure is designed for forces roughly six times smaller than the elastic demand and must supply ductility on the order of six in return [20:31][44:36]. Two parallel worked designs anchor the talk, an SCBF in Los Angeles (high seismicity, two braced bays) and one in Boston (lower seismicity, single bay, wind-governed) [18:28].

### Spec References
Speakers cited the following by name: ASCE 7 for the seismic loading, response spectrum, base shear distribution, the accidental 5 percent eccentricity, and the P-Delta stability ratio Theta with a 0.10 threshold [29:15][31:47]; AISC 341 (referred to as the AISC seismic spec, 2005 edition) for the SCBF system, the Ry/Rt overstrength tables, the KL/r upper limit of 200, and the D/t and b/t section-slenderness limits [14:51][52:51]; AISC 360 / LRFD ("AISC 05") for member design [33:19][35:23]; AWS D1.1 for the allowance to overlook a 1/16 inch root gap when sizing a fillet weld [70:01]. Material specs named: ASTM A500 Grade B and C (Ry 1.4, same for both grades), ASTM A501 hot-formed, the proposed new ASTM A1085-type tube spec ("A1000-something") with Fy 50 to 65 ksi and CVN 20 ft-lb at 0 F, ASTM A36 gusset plate, E70 electrodes, and ASTM A958 Class 8050 cast steel (composition near A514) for the cast connector [52:51][84:30][94:08]. Brace force for capacity design is the AgRyFy expected-yield force [51:18].

### Technical Details
Brace ductility is controlled by two parameters: global slenderness KL/r (kept below 200, with a useful range cited as roughly 70 to 200, and ongoing research possibly adding a lower bound) and section slenderness via b/t and D/t limits to prevent local buckling and early fracture [14:20][15:22]. Round HSS (A500 Grade B) is preferred over square or rectangular because square cold-formed corners have heavy cold work, raising Fy near Fu and cutting cyclic life [75:17][76:19]. The Chevron / inverted-V configuration requires the beam to be designed for the unbalanced force between the tension brace at AgRyFy and the compression brace at its post-buckling (lower-bound) capacity [21:03][43:36]. The expected-strength factors Ry and Rt account for delivered steel exceeding minimum specified Fy and Fu; designing a connection to the nominal force when the brace arrives over-strong is what fractures the connection [42:36].

### Fabrication Notes
The standard slotted detail puts a slot in the round tube end, inserts the gusset plate, and fillet welds it. Over-slotting (slot longer than the plate insertion) leaves an open gap at the most critical net section, where shear lag concentrates and fracture starts [54:24][55:24]. Because that reduced net section, after the 0.8 shear-lag U-factor, falls below the gross area, the connection must be reinforced: quarter-sections cut from the same HSS are stretched and patch-welded 90 degrees off the slot to add net area, raising effective area to 4.55 sq in against a 4.03 sq in requirement [65:27][72:08]. Fabricators like to burn the slot rough; under seismic (cyclic fatigue) loading the slot ends must be cut smooth because cracking initiates at the weld termination [56:28]. A500 round HSS must use the 0.93 design thickness, not nominal, when sizing welds [72:38]. The cast-steel connector (marketed as Cast ConneX) welds to the gross tube with a single CJP weld and removes most of this detailing skill [79:24][80:25].

### Erection Notes
The connection is detailed as a field-welded slotted HSS-to-gusset joint: plumb the frame with gusset plates pre-attached to the building frame, bring the bracing member in, set it, then fillet weld it as the final field operation [57:28]. The 2tp gap (about a two-plate-thickness setback) is left so the plastic hinge forms inside the gusset plate rather than in the brace, which gives a stable, repeatable hinge and shortens the brace working-point length for design [37:25][74:14]. The talk centers on connection and member behavior; broader crane, rigging, and sequencing topics are not covered.

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named failure and error modes: circumferential fracture of the tube driven by shear lag at the open slot end (the dominant brittle mode the design must outrank) [59:00]; shear/tear-out rupture along the weld lines when plate insertion length is too short [59:31]; connection fracture from designing to nominal Fy when delivered Fy is higher [42:36]; rough-burned slot ends seeding cracks under cyclic load [56:28]; square-HSS corner low-cycle fatigue fracture after few cycles from cold-work embrittlement [76:19]; beam yielding under the Chevron unbalanced load if not capacity-designed [21:03]; and forgetting non-seismic load cases (wind governed every Boston brace, and a heavy point load can govern a brace) [34:53].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Using the ductile R=6 SCBF instead of an R=3 ordinary frame roughly halves design seismic forces, so every non-yielding element (diaphragms, foundations, columns, beams) is sized for far less load, often the cheaper path overall despite the detailing cost [91:29][93:03]. Large brace tension forces drive foundation cost, which can dominate seismic economics, so brace sizing has a downstream foundation-price effect [18:59]. Reinforced slotted HSS connections add significant shop labor (cutting and patch-welding quarter-tubes, especially on round sections), which is exactly the skilled work the cast connector replaces with a known, fixed connector cost and one CJP weld [80:25][81:25]. Square HSS is chosen in practice mainly because the flat-face patch plate is cheaper to fabricate, a fabrication-economics tradeoff against worse seismic ductility [67:27].
