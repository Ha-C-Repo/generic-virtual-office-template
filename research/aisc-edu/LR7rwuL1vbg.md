### Core Concept
This final session of Rafael Sabelli's eight-part AISC seismic series covers the design of buckling restrained braced frames (BRBF) end to end, applying capacity design. The braces are the fuse: size them first against 100% of the story shear, then run a plastic mechanism analysis (PMA) to find the maximum tension and compression forces those braces can impose on beams, columns, and connections, then design those protected elements for the amplified force [05:15][70:28]. The maximum brace forces include a strain hardening factor omega and a compression overstrength factor beta drawn from manufacturer testing, so the system is a design partnership between the engineer of record and the BRB manufacturer [16:27][70:58].

### Technical Details
BRB area is set so phi*Fy*A equals the brace force, using the lower bound of the allowed Fy range 38 to 46 ksi and an expected RyFy of about 42 ksi, with round-up increments of 1/4 in to 5 sq in, 1/2 in to 10 sq in, then 1 in [11:21][15:26]. Selected areas ran 2.0 sq in at the roof to 5.5 sq in at the base [14:25]. PMA design stresses were about 68 ksi in compression and 59 ksi in tension using beta 1.15, omega 1.4, RyFy 42 ksi, producing a governing overstrength ratio near 2.2 at the second floor versus the code default of 2.5 [18:30][20:00]. In a chevron, the beam carries a net vertical load proportional to (beta minus one) of the vertical brace component because compression capacity exceeds tension [22:33]. Final design used a W10x77 compression column (753 kips per Table 4-1), a W18x55 beam, and a 32 in by 18 in tall "mega gusset" with stabilizing top and bottom flange plates [28:42][46:28].

### Fabrication Notes
The engineer specifies the BRB to the manufacturer either by area (for example 5 sq in) or by design strength (for example phi*Psc of 200 kips), and must state clearly whether phi is included [11:21][12:24]. Gusset thickness is often dictated by the BRB core plate thickness, so the fabricator cannot freely pick a plate gauge [40:55]. A rule of thumb from fabricators is a chevron gusset length of beam-length over six (48 in here against a 40 in to 42 in minimum); a far longer required gusset signals going to a heavier beam [31:16][55:12]. Where the beam web is thinner than the gusset, a web doubler is added to match thickness and raise block-shear/tear-out strength [51:39]. Drag-element shapes were quarter sections of HSS rather than double angles specifically so they would not interfere with the gusset-to-base-plate fillet weld [66:24].

### Erection Notes
Bolts are kept 4 in off the bottom of the gusset plate for erection access [42:56]. For the beam-to-gusset double-shear connection the engineer asks for tack welds for erection so the gusset plate does not fall off and the bolts can all be driven straight through once the beam is set, instead of staggering bolts [50:36][51:08]. Anchor rods at the base plate are shown deep-bolted, and the base plate column connection is CJP welded to allow inelastic rotation to come out of the column [58:18][65:53].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The session frames pitfalls more than rejections. Making braces stronger than needed forces stronger and costlier connections, beams, and columns, so over-rounding is penalized downstream [15:56]. Relying on gravity-column shear to carry part of the frame force is not code-compliant, which is why gravity columns are commonly pinned [81:14]. A Q&A point noted the Whitmore section tension check (30 degree fan from the bolt group) must still be run on the gusset [81:45]. Designing anchor rods for combined shear and bending would push rods from 1-3/4 in to 3 in, so a separate shear load path is preferred [63:21].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Ductile detailing yields more economical buildings because the required strength is reduced and large inelastic drifts are accommodated through ductility rather than brute strength [71:59]. Brace areas were "pretty tightly designed" with at most about 10% round-up, which keeps connection and member sizes down [15:26]. BRBs are proprietary manufactured items, so brace pricing, the stiffness factor (about 1.4 here), and the beta/omega factors come from the manufacturer brochure or direct consultation, and that information must be in the bid model and the connection design [35:17][41:25]. Gusset design is increasingly shared with the brace manufacturer, which shifts connection-engineering scope and must be coordinated on load path and design responsibility [63:51][70:58].

### Bolting / Welding Summary
Two manufacturer-specified bolted brace cases appeared: a 338 kip / 294 kip brace using four 1-in A490X bolts in double shear with a 3/4-in grade 50 gusset (bolt shear 396 kips per Table 7-1, bearing 339 kips, block shear 349 kips) [42:56][43:27]; and a beam-to-gusset case using five A325X bolts in double shear with tack welds for erection [50:36]. Per AISC 341 these bearing-type joints are built as pretensioned with a Class A faying surface, and there is no advantage to designing them slip-critical [82:46]. Welds: gusset-to-beam/column fillets sized off Table 8-4 (two 5/16 in and two 3/8 in fillets in the two gussets), base-plate flange CJP welds specified as demand critical with demand-critical web welds, and S1 supplement specified for anchor-rod weldability on the grade 55 rods [48:01][61:19][65:53].
