## Design of Column Reinforcement
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xltavaJQaGg | Duration: 1:04:52 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Bo Dowswell (ARC International, AISC Committee on Specifications) teaches how to design reinforcement for axially loaded wide-flange columns using welded reinforcing plates, where the failure mode is flexural buckling [02:25][03:25]. The talk covers four linked problems: partial-length reinforcement that must be set back from framing members, the effect of preload (the load present on the column when the plates are welded on), a notional-load analysis that replaces the effective-length method, and how to size the reinforcement welds [09:01][09:32]. The central practical idea is that if the reinforcement is "stabilizing" you can ignore preload and just add preload plus postload as a total load into the spec [18:57].

### Spec References
The spoken references are: AISC 360 Chapter E flexural buckling provisions and specifically Section E3 for wide-flange columns [10:02][10:32]; Section C2.3 for the reduced stiffness EI star equal to 0.8 times tau-sub-b times E times I [41:41]; the tau-sub-b equation from the spec [42:11]; Section H1.1 combined biaxial bending and compression interaction equations [46:46]; and the AISC Code of Standard Practice column out-of-straightness tolerance of L over 1000 [40:08]. He cites the AISC Engineering Journal column-stepped-column effective-length tables published in 1969 [15:06], and a prior 2013 AISC webinar "Design of Reinforcement for Steel Members" (parts 1 and 2) for the allowable-stress preload method and local buckling [25:11][25:41]. He references Blodgett's welding book for weld-shrinkage distortion control [38:38].

### Technical Details
Reinforcement types in rough preference order are: two flange plates (his favorite, easy to design and install, leave roughly 1 inch land at the flange tip for field fit) [03:55][04:25]; flange plates plus a web plate when more weak-axis stiffness is needed [04:25]; a single-sided plate when only one flange is accessible [05:25]; a box (closed) section for torsion and large weak-axis gain [05:56]; and continuous four-sided reinforcement only when load is too high to set back [06:59]. The stabilizing-reinforcement test is that the reinforced radius of gyration R-sub-R must be at least 0.85 times the original R-sub-O [19:28][60:40]. The concept rests on tangent-modulus behavior: a preloaded section yields and loses stiffness, so the elastic reinforcing plates carried far from the neutral axis (flange tips) resist buckling [22:06][23:09]. Partial-length reinforcement is modeled as a stepped column using the moment-of-inertia ratio I-sub-R over I-sub-O and the length ratio L-sub-R over L, with K factors K-sub-S1 (set back one end) and K-sub-S2 (set back both ends); a worked example reads K-sub-S2 of 1.139 at ratio 2.5 and L-sub-R/L of 0.5 [15:39][17:19][17:49]. The notional-load method applies a uniform lateral load W-sub-N equal to P-sub-R divided by (125 times L) when out-of-straightness is within tolerance, or 8 times P-sub-R times delta-sub-D over L squared when it exceeds tolerance, then uses K equals 1, the EI star reduced stiffness, the B1 moment multiplier, and the H1.1 interaction with P-sub-C replaced by the squash load P-sub-NS [40:39][42:41][47:17].

### Fabrication Notes
The dominant shop concern is weld-shrinkage distortion that can push the column out of straight beyond the Code of Standard Practice L over 1000 limit [38:08][39:08]. Two-sided reinforcement lets welders stagger and alternate passes side to side to balance shrinkage, an art the shop knows better than the engineer; single-sided plates give far less control over distortion [38:38][39:08]. Detailers should hold a land of roughly 1 inch (the AISC manual notes about a half inch for certain weld sizes) between flange tip and plate so the field can shift the plate during fit-up [03:55][04:25]. Web-plate and box reinforcement are slowed by beams, struts, and vertical-bracing gusset plates framing into the web, which often force the plate to be cut short [04:55][05:56].

### Erection Notes
Reinforcement is typically a retrofit on an erected, loaded column, so plates are welded in place rather than dismantling the frame [12:03][12:33]. Reinforcement must be set back from work points where beams, struts, or gusset plates frame in, commonly about a foot and sometimes pulled back three or four feet for economy when column strength allows [12:33][13:03]. The plate must extend past the theoretical cutoff point by the anchor length D-sub-RT to develop the load in the plate [51:20][53:21]. He avoids welding across the bottom end to stay away from overhead welds [55:54].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The named failure risks are column out-of-straightness exceeding L over 1000 from weld-shrinkage distortion, which voids the plain spec equations and requires the extra notional-load moment [38:08][39:38]; welded flange-tip tension residual stresses (welding overrides the original compression residual stress at the tip and can actually raise capacity by about 6 percent in the tests) [30:50][31:24]; and, for bolted reinforcement, using non-slip-critical bolts, which lets oversized holes (a sixteenth each) slip and create a large initial rotation before the bolts engage [62:46][63:16]. A common design oversight is missing that the controlling cross section can be at the end of the reinforcement, not only at column mid-height [36:36].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Setting reinforcement back further than strictly required saves plate, weld, and engineering labor while reducing distortion, so the setback decision is a direct cost lever [12:33][13:03]. Treating stabilizing reinforcement as a simple total-load case avoids running two finite-element models (one before and one after reinforcement) and skips the per-section allowable-stress hand calculations, which he states saves substantial engineering time and yields higher usable loads [33:28][33:58]. Choosing the simple two-plate flange detail over web or box reinforcement reduces welder time lost to web obstructions [04:55].
