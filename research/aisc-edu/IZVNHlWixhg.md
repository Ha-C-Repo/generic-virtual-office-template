## SpeedCore:  Rainier Square -- A Project Case Study
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IZVNHlWixhg | Duration: 1:00:18 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
The webinar is a case study of the SpeedCore composite plate shear wall, concrete-filled (CPSW-CF) system used on the Rainier Square tower in Seattle, an 850-foot, ~58-story office and residential building, presented by Brian Morgan of Magnusson Klemencic Associates [00:33][06:50]. SpeedCore replaces a traditional reinforced concrete core with prefabricated steel sandwich panels, two outer steel face plates tied together with cross ties, that are erected in sequence with the steel floor framing and then filled with self-consolidating concrete as a trailing operation several levels below the active erection deck [02:37][14:29][15:01]. The face plates serve as the formwork, eliminating rebar and forming, and the whole tower erects under one trade tolerance instead of separate concrete (ACI) and steel (AISC) standards of practice [04:13][04:44]. The driving benefit was schedule: the core dropped off the critical path, since a reinforced concrete core takes roughly twice as long per floor as the trailing steel framing [10:55][11:27].
### Spec References
The speaker cited AISC 341-2016 as the standard housing the CPSW-CF system [02:37], ASCE 7-16 for the seismic R factor classification as a steel-and-composite plate shear wall [03:10], and ASCE 7-22 Chapter 14 as the target for incorporating new provisions [46:19][46:49]. He referenced the AISC 341 requirement that cross-tie connections develop the yield strength of the bar [18:08], AISC 341 recommendations on effective stiffness of a composite section [48:51], AISC 360 effective stiffness equations for filled composite members for elastic flexural stiffness [45:47], FEMA P695 confirming an R of 8 for the coupled system [46:19], and ASTM A572 grade 50/55 plate (stated as A592 in the auto-caption, almost certainly A572) specified at 55 ksi yield with a 5 ksi tolerance each way [17:07]. Proposed updates to AISC 341 Section H7 plus a new section for coupled CPSW were noted as in the balloting pipeline [46:19].
### Technical Details
Wall face plates were nominally half-inch steel on each face, comprising 75 to 80 percent of the core, increasing to three-quarter inch at high-demand zones and up to two inch where BRB outrigger gussets enter [16:36]. Wall thicknesses ran 21 to 45 inches (two to four feet), thickest at the base and tapering up [16:36]. Cross ties were 1-inch-diameter, grade 55 rod, spaced 12 inches on center both ways on all faces; at Rainier Square this gave about 240,000 cross ties and 480,000 welds [17:07][18:38][19:09]. Concrete infill was a 10 ksi self-consolidating mix requiring no vibration [17:07][30:55]. Coupling beams (the H or dog-bone panels at door openings) were built-up concrete-filled box tubes with cross ties both ways [21:11]. The lateral system was a CPSW-CF core plus buckling-restrained-brace outrigger and belt trusses at the two-thirds height; no dual moment-frame backup, no SMF or IMF, designed by performance-based seismic design [08:21][49:53][50:23]. Wind controlled overturning by a factor of 3 to 4 over seismic, so walls could not be thinned [49:21].
### Fabrication Notes
Panels were fully prefabricated in the shop with cross ties, deck ledger angles, shear tabs for incoming beams, and MEP penetrations all pre-installed, so a panel could be lifted and have steel hung off it immediately [14:29][33:30]. The fabricator was free to choose the cross-tie connection so long as it developed the bar yield; Rainier Square used unthreaded grade 55 rods passed about three-quarters of an inch through the plate and fillet welded each side [17:38][18:38]. Plate width drove sourcing: domestic mills roll to about 10 feet, so below-grade plate (10-foot floor heights) was domestic, but the 13-foot-9 typical floors required 14-foot-wide plate ordered from Korean mills to avoid a shop splice [34:31][35:02]. Plate was cut on a burn table to specification and moved down an assembly line; the fabricator voluntarily added small angle-iron lattice bracing so panels would not rack during handling [32:27]. Mock-ups were built first: a plywood replica of the worst congestion to prove the concrete fill technique and mix, and a steel corner-section mock-up to shake out run-off tabs, backer bars, and fit-up issues before approving shop drawings [29:22][31:26].
### Erection Notes
The first core lift was set on stanchions that delivered full core capacity into the mat foundation; rebar and concrete for the mat were then built around it [36:34]. Corner box columns were erected first, plumbed and welded, then face panels flew in between and were field spliced [19:39][37:36]. Concrete was a fully trailing operation roughly four to six (and up to four to eight) levels below the active erection deck, pumped at 100 to 120 cubic yards per hour through blind access ports using telltale and vent holes for visual confirmation [15:01][26:20][30:25][38:38]. As many as four stories could be unconcreted at once, with the young, unconcreted core carrying the weight of erected floors; the cross ties provided stability during erection, shipping, and the unconcreted condition, then acted as form ties against hydrostatic pressure and finally as composite shear connectors [25:48][26:50]. Concrete placed floor by floor meant only about a 14-foot hydrostatic head [56:29]. Steel went vertical October 15, 2018 and topped out exactly 10 months later on August 15 [37:05][39:39].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The field weld was a full-depth PJP with a reinforcing fillet, chosen over a CJP specifically because there is no way to reach the inside of the wall to repair a CJP, so inspection and repair access drove the joint selection [20:40]. The weld was prequalified by testing to confirm it was a capacity joint that yields the plate outside the weld rather than failing the weld [20:10]. Automated field welding was tried in mock-up but rejected because it could not adjust to the variable root gap; the quarter-inch designed root gap could vary from zero to a quarter inch across a 35-to-40-foot panel due to plumb tolerance, so all 36 miles of field weld were hand welded [54:28][55:29]. Concrete voids were the main quality-control fear, addressed with interior-plate flow holes ("Swiss cheese"), coupling-beam vent holes, and telltale holes to confirm fill [50:53][51:23]. No observed panel distortion or out-of-plumb occurred at the 12-inch cross-tie spacing; distortion risk rises if spacing widens toward 18 inches [56:59][57:32].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Schedule was the entire business case. The paper estimate was 21 months for a concrete core versus 12 for SpeedCore; the realized result was a core topping out in 10 months and concrete finished at 11, a roughly 10-month schedule savings against the contractor's estimated 91 weeks versus 50 weeks for the two approaches [22:45][24:17][24:47]. Faster delivery cut construction cost, reduced owner financing cost, and brought rental income sooner [04:44][05:14]. The 480,000 welds make weld-cycle time the dominant labor variable: every minute saved per weld multiplies across the count, which is why cross-tie spacing and weld automation were targets even though automation failed here [19:09]. Sourcing 14-foot Korean plate to avoid shop splices, and the conservative full cross-tie population, were deliberate cost-versus-risk choices made because this was a world-first in high seismic at great height under difficult peer review [27:51][35:02][58:21].
