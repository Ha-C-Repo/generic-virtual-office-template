## Specifying Camber: Rules of Thumb for Designers
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zJk5FWaNHH4 | Duration: 55:21 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Camber is a curvature fabricated into a beam or truss to compensate for deflection induced by loads, taken about the strong axis (bending about the flanges), while bending about the weak axis (about the web) is called sweep [03:38]. Presenter Erica Winterstetter (AISC Great Plains Regional Engineer) frames camber as "more of an art than a science," not a checkbox in analysis software, and a topic learned by trial and error in the field rather than in school [02:36]. The drivers are reduced framing weight and reduced floor-to-floor depth, where going from a W21 to a W18 saves 3 inches per floor that compounds across a multistory facade and MEP stack [04:08]. The controlling rule of thumb: camber is only worth it if it saves at least 5 pounds per foot of beam weight [08:14].
### Spec References
The speaker cites ASTM A6 for mill rolling tolerances of hot-rolled shapes, shown graphically in the 13th edition Steel Construction Manual Tables 1-22 through 1-29 [09:47]. AISC Specification Chapter A references A6, and the Code of Standard Practice Sections 5 and 6 reference ASTM A6 [09:47]. Induced-camber tolerances are in the Manual page 2-28 and Code of Standard Practice Section 6.4.4 [13:00]. The mill-camber tolerance for camber and sweep is 1/8 inch per 10 feet [10:19]. The 80%-dead-load camber guidance traces to two papers at the 1996 NASCC conference [22:56]. The R=3 seismic provision is named to illustrate that nominal pin connections carry minimal fixity [25:29].
### Technical Details
Mill (natural) camber comes from uneven cooling at the mill where flanges cool slower than the web and the beam arcs into a crown; at the A6 limit of 1/8 inch per 10 feet a 30-foot beam gets at most 3/8 inch, usually less, so mill camber is generally not a design factor [09:16]. Induced camber carries a negative-zero, positive-1/2-inch tolerance: a called 1.5 inches cannot finish below 1.5 inches and may run to 2.0 inches [13:30]. Transportation losses can run up to 25% of induced camber, partly offset by that positive 1/2-inch [14:00]. Camber is chosen for one of roughly 150 load combinations, so the floor is flat for only that single case [21:55]. Conservative practice cambers out 80% of permanent dead load (real connections give about 20% fixity versus a theoretical pin-pin); an aggressive stance adds superimposed dead and up to 10% live load, but that SDL-plus-live portion should not exceed 1/2 inch of the total [22:56].
### Fabrication Notes
Camber is induced in the fabrication shop, not the mill, by cold cambering or hot cambering [12:27]. Cold cambering uses a jig with two pressing rams at the third points, deflecting the beam 2 to 3 times the target so it passes the yield point into permanent plastic deflection while the elastic portion springs back as the rams retract; long beams (40 to 60 feet) are pressed at multiple points serpentine-style through the machine [15:02]. The pure tension/compression in the flanges means no residual stress is induced in the flanges, though the web is less clear [17:38]. Hot cambering applies heat at 1100 to 1200 degrees in isosceles wedge patterns (about a 30-degree apex) along web and flange, worked upside down so gravity assists, and costs about 10 times cold cambering (roughly 3 man-hours cold versus 30 hot) [19:46]. Camber is performed after the beam is cut to length and punched or drilled, so square-end members may need re-trimming [25:59]. Camber is verified in the shop in the horizontal position, never in the field [14:00].
### Erection Notes
Per the Code of Standard Practice, beams detailed without specified camber are fabricated so any incidental rolling or shop crown points upward after erection, so a beam never ships already sagging [10:53]. If a beam arrives with 75% of its specified camber from the mill, no further cambering is required, realistically only on a long beam with a low specified camber (a 50-foot beam at 3/4 inch where 9/16 inch falls inside the 5/8-inch mill tolerance) [11:24]. During erection, double-angle connections have enough play to warp into place on a cambered beam; shear-plate, tee, and single-angle connections may need short-slotted holes for fit-up, which do not bump the joint to pretensioned or slip-critical as long as load is perpendicular to the slot [26:33]. Camber differences between adjacent beams, and between cambered beams and joists or non-cambered supports, complicate deck placement and should be transitioned by trimming an adjacent camber [33:54].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Common mistakes include over-specifying camber (the speaker says always round down, specify in 1/4-inch increments only, never eighths or sixteenths, and never call camber below 3/4 inch because transportation losses make it ineffective and shops cannot reliably induce that little) [31:48]. Correcting an over-cambered beam by running it backward through the machine is the wrong fix per AISC because it induces strain weakening and fatigue-type back-and-forth action; the correct correction is mild heat in the 200-to-500-degree range for a few minutes [18:46]. Cambering moment connections (cover-plated, directly welded, or end-plate) is a frequent error because the differential pinches one weld root gap closed and opens the other or forces tapered shim plates [27:38]. Ponding from a flat-set slab elevation can leave shear studs poking through the slab or pull beams into deflection [30:46].
### Bid / Cost Implications
A fabricator bid is roughly 30% raw material and 70% labor and value added, so the economy is in simple, repetitive members and connections rather than minimizing weight [04:38]. At the example pricing (steel near $1,100 per ton, about 56 cents per pound), a $75 cold-camber charge equals about 134 pounds, hence the 5-pound-per-foot break-even; cold-camber quotes ranged $35 to $200 per infill beam depending on width, depth, length, geography, fabricator, and camber amount [07:43]. Hot cambering is about 10 times cold and is reserved for members exceeding cold-camber machine capacity, often plate girders in bridge work [21:20]. A worked example showed a cambered W21x44 jumping to W21x83 and a cambered W18x35 jumping to W18x65 when camber is removed, illustrating the weight-versus-labor trade [38:44].
