## Efficient Lateral Load Resisting Systems for Low Rise Buildings
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1UQIe4K5aDA | Duration: 1:08:23 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
Jason Perry (Stanley D. Lindsey) and Patrick McManus (Martin/Martin) walk through choosing and economizing lateral systems for low-rise steel buildings, focused on low-seismic R=3 systems where the engineer has freedom to detail braces and frames around architecture [01:07][01:37]. The controlling theme is that least weight is not least cost: structure is only about 18 to 25% of total construction cost, structural steel about 10 to 12%, and that steel cost splits roughly a third material, a third shop labor, a third erection labor, so chasing tonnage rarely moves the project number [38:03][38:35][39:07]. The better levers are constructability, fewer trades, less sequencing, and less time on site [40:38][41:10]. A second thesis is that high-seismic systems (SCBF, EBF, BRBF) can pay off even in low-seismic areas when they cut foundation size on poor soil or spread footings [56:29][68:12].

### Technical Details
Braced frames are almost always cheaper than moment frames, with the tradeoff of bigger uplift-resisting foundations; keep brace angle 30 to 60 degrees, ideally near 45, sometimes held to 35 to 55 for connection economy [02:08][03:11]. For an X-brace in a non-high-ductility R=3 system, the tension brace can be taken to brace the compression brace so an effective length of 0.5L is justified, because tension stiffening offsets P-delta and yields the same stiffness as an eigenbuckling analysis [07:14][08:15]. Single-diagonal frames erect one piece but have no redundancy and larger braces; chevron and V configs drive a heavier collector beam [05:13][08:45]. Moment frames are drift-governed (wind drift on a 10-year wind, a serviceability state so stiffness is not reduced for direct analysis), and the cheapest stiffness gain is a deeper column (W18, W21, W24) rather than more weight [11:52][12:25][17:32][18:02]. Diaphragm starting points: 1.5-inch B deck at 6.5 ft max span, 22 gauge minimum, 5/8 spot welds in a 36/4 pattern give about 335 plf service, while 18 gauge with 36/7 reaches about 943 plf; aspect ratios run 2:1 up to 5:1 by flexibility [22:10][22:40][23:10][24:43]. Continuous-chord joists used as moment frames avoid chord splices and barely hit joist cost; the difficulty is decoupling large gravity chord forces (carried around the column on sandwich plates) from the small lateral component carried into the column [29:24][32:59][35:32].

### Fabrication Notes
The recurring fabrication economy argument is to avoid full-penetration welds: a shop-welded column cap plate with four bolts beats a CJP beam-flange-to-column detail on a roof, even when the bolts grow to 1.25-inch A490 to carry the moment [15:27][15:58]. Single-story cap-plate detail is a cap plate with stated thickness, a stated bolt count and size, one stiffener for an HSS column and two stiffeners for a W10 or W12 column [16:31]. Continuous moment lines extend the connection toward the gravity inflection point so the moment connection need only be a fraction (about 50%) of beam capacity, allowing a PJP top flange (chosen for deck interruptions) and a bolted flange plate or PJP at the bottom [17:01][17:32]. Joist seats should not carry large axial loads because that cranks moment into the joist chord the manufacturer will not design for, so attach to the outstanding legs concentric to the chord [34:31][35:01]. Composite shotcrete walls keep every connection steel-to-steel with vertical deck as the form, so there are no fit-up or form-saver issues [48:49][49:19].

### Erection Notes
Single-diagonal braces save erection because there is one brace to set per bay per floor instead of two [05:13]. Beam lengths are held to about 50 to 60 ft to fit on a truck without a special permit [19:05]. Cast-in-place concrete cores are critical path, must come out of the ground first, and over three stories create sequencing conflicts where you can only build so much wall before steel must go up to brace the next lift, putting trades over each other [43:45][44:15]. Concrete tolerance is far looser than steel, forcing expensive adjustable field-welded connections, and every shear-wall building the speaker has done has had a missed embed [44:45][45:15]. The shotcrete composite wall is built to let steel erection finish and the crew leave site before the shotcreters return to spray [48:49]. Whether shotcrete or deck pours first changes whether the spandrel beam can be light or must carry wet concrete weight [52:55][53:25].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named pitfalls: missed embeds on cast-in-place shear-wall jobs (the speaker says he has never had one without it) [45:15]; concrete tolerance misfits that drive field welding and delays with disputed fault [44:45][45:46]; loading a joist seat with large axial force that overstresses the chord against the manufacturer's design [34:00]; assuming an X-brace compression effective length of full L when 0.5L is defensible, leaving tonnage on the table [07:44]; treating diaphragms as rigid in software when reality is flexible or semi-rigid, so analysis assumptions do not match behavior [26:45][27:17]; and under-detailing the brace-to-foundation anchorage on capacity-designed SCBF braces, where a brace sized for 100 kips compression forces a connection and anchorage to develop 400 to 500 kips in tension and surprises the GC [60:32][61:02][66:07].

### Bid / Cost Implications
A 5% tonnage saving on a project equals about 0.2% of total project cost, so weight-based value engineering rarely matters; GCs and pre-con estimators price on unit cost ($/ton, $/SF), which reinforces the least-weight habit but is not reality [39:07][39:37][40:07]. Real savings come from schedule and trade reduction since GC time on site always costs money however the bid was priced [41:10]. Concrete cores are partly a GC profit play: one GC told the speaker self-performing cores lifts profit from 2 to 3% up to 8% [43:13]. Two foundation case studies quantify the high-seismic-in-low-seismic argument: an SDC B job dropped base shear from 490 kips at R=3 to far less at R=8, cutting one footing from 46 to 21 cubic yards (54%) for about $12,500 saved against a BRB at roughly $3,000 per brace [62:02][63:33][64:04]; an SDC C job on poor soil used SCBF R=6 to roughly halve foundation loads, where the R=3 footing alone would have run about $51,000 versus $23,000, leaving room to add about 120 plf to every brace and still come out ahead at an in-place steel cost of about $4,000 per ton [64:35][66:07][67:09].

### Your Company Application
For Ivan takeoff, this validates the verify-don't-generate posture: do not let a least-weight assumption drive a bid number, because the $/SF gate and the third-third-third (material, shop, erection) split mean schedule and connection complexity move price more than tonnage, and AISC member weights still come only from aisc_validator.py. For Mario's shop, the cap-plate-with-four-bolts versus CJP point is a direct fabrication-cost flag (1.25-inch A490 bolts can beat full-pen welds on a roof), and the PJP top flange at deck interruptions plus joist seat eccentricity rules are AWS D1.1 and SJI items for WPS planning. For anchor rods, the SCBF brace-anchorage warning is the most load-bearing item: capacity-designed braces force very large tension into the anchor-rod group and foundation, so our anchor-rod design, setting, and tolerance workstream must size for the developed brace strength, not the service load. For joists, the continuous-chord moment-frame detailing and "attach to outstanding legs, not the seat" guidance feed Tekla detailing and any in-house PE stamp on joist-to-column moment connections.
