## Design of Curved Members with the new AISC Design Guide

### Core Concept
The webinar walks through AISC Design Guide 33, Curved Member Design, presented by Bo Dowswell of ARC International ahead of the guide's release [02:08]. The central method is that curved members are not equal to straight members, but with three modifications you can design them as equivalent straight members using the standard specification [13:33], [53:22]. The guide covers vertically curved members (arches and beam-like curves), horizontally curved members (flexure plus torsion), and connections, with two design chapters plus an examples chapter [23:31], [30:20]. Vertically curved members get the deep treatment with full equations; horizontally curved get an overview because their behavior is governed by serviceability and deformation rather than closed-form strength [39:17], [80:05].

### Technical Details
For axial in-plane strength the developed (arc) length Ld replaces the straight column length, an effective length factor Ki from a Design Guide table replaces K (for example parabolic pinned arches use Ki = 0.5 for all rise-to-span ratios, treating the member as braced at midspan), and the maximum load anywhere along Ld is run through Section E3 [53:52], [55:56], [56:57]. Snap-through buckling must be screened out first: if H/Ls is greater than 0.2 it is not a concern, otherwise a table of Ls/r ratios versus rise-to-span and end fixity must be satisfied, where the slenderness must EXCEED the tabulated value (for H/Ls = 0.15 pinned, Ls/r greater than 36) because snap-through is governed by axial stiffness while the preferred buckling mode is governed by slenderness [49:15], [51:19], [52:19]. Out-of-plane axial design uses developed length between braces Ldb, a Ko effective length factor derived from setting the flexural-torsional buckling equal to flexural buckling, with a Co coefficient carrying the St. Venant torsion, warping constant, and out-of-plane moment of inertia [60:02], [61:40]. Flexural strength uses Cb,i in place of Cb and distinguishes opening moments (compression in the outer flange, use the negative root, lower capacity than a straight beam) from closing moments (compression in the inner flange, use the positive root, may conservatively be taken as a straight beam) [64:24], [66:34]. A flange-bending check applies only at small radius and high flange width-to-thickness (example: 15 ft radius on a thin W21 flange) and a web-buckling check applies only at very tight radius (example: 5 ft radius, 20 in deep, 3/8 in web) [19:48], [20:18].

### Fabrication Notes
Chapter 2 of the guide covers three primary bending processes: pyramid roll bending (cold, the most common, three rolls in a pyramid with a supplementary roll to limit tension-flange distortion, multiple passes with roll adjustment between each), incremental step bending (cold, cantilever bending applied as the member is fed through), and induction bending (the only hot method, a heated band cooled immediately while the member pivots around a radius arm) [31:33], [33:07], [35:44]. Fabricators typically do not bend to these radii in-house; their own equipment is generally limited to camber bending, so they sub the work to specialized bender-roller companies whose equipment, personnel, and shop layout differ by shop [27:42], [38:14]. Detailers must add geometry the bender needs, including the rotation or twist dimension on an off-axis curve and a section view showing the rotation angle, before the member can be bent [10:59], [28:13]. Wall thickness can be increased on an HSS to limit distortion when an architecturally exposed (AESS) finish is wanted, and the guide gives arc-length (developed-length) equations for parabolic and elliptical curves so quantities can be computed [26:09], [25:38].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The speaker frames failure modes rather than shop rejections. Snap-through (oil-canning) collapse is the catastrophic case, sensitive to second-order effects and support spreading, and is screened by the H/Ls and slenderness rules [44:33], [49:15]. Excessive deformation and torsional rotation govern horizontally curved members, which often cannot reach their full plastic strength because of deformation, and there are no rotation limits in the building codes, so the engineer must set one (the British guide's 2 degrees is widely borrowed but must not be applied where it does not fit) [74:53], [80:35], [82:09]. Bending defects such as fracture at very tight radius, flange or web distortion, and section instability during rolling are noted as real but generally controllable by the bender roller [27:42], [34:10]. Material-property and brittle-fracture concerns from cold work only arise for tight radius combined with galvanizing and low service temperature; for normal building radii the cold-work effect actually reduces residual stresses and ductility loss is less than from typical punching or torch cutting of a straight member [29:47], [88:02], [89:33].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Curved work is a sub-out cost item: the fabricator routes bending to a specialized bender-roller company, so a curved-member scope should carry that vendor cost and lead time rather than assuming in-house bending [27:42], [38:14]. Calling the bender roller early in design, before sizing is locked, avoids redesign loops and lets them recommend a thicker HSS wall to control distortion for an exposed finish, which is a member-cost and quantity input [26:09]. Developed (arc) length, not span, is the controlling length for both quantity takeoff and design, so weights and lengths must be computed on the curve [25:06], [54:25]. Horizontally curved members are usually serviceability-controlled, meaning member size is set by deflection and rotation limits the engineer chooses, not by strength, which can push sizes up beyond what a strength check alone would suggest [80:05], [83:41].
