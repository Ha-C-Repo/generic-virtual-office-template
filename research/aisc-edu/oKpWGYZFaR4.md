## SSPC Surface Preparation - Shop Painting of Structural Steel
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oKpWGYZFaR4 | Duration: 1:14:00 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This is a three-part AISC conference session on protecting fabricated structural steel: SSPC surface preparation standards (Bill Shope, SSPC), corrosion and fire-protection coatings (Andrew Smith, International Paints), and architecturally exposed structural steel or AESS (Todd Elwood, AISC). The unifying message is that coating performance is bought at the surface-preparation stage, not the paint can. As the coatings speaker put it, "you only get one chance to paint steel properly and that's when it's new" at fabrication [47:12], because mill scale, salts, and contaminants must be removed and a sharp angular profile created before any coating is applied [47:43].
### Spec References
SSPC surface-prep standards named verbatim: SP 1 solvent cleaning, SP 2 hand-tool cleaning, SP 3 power-tool cleaning, SP 5 white-metal blast, SP 6 commercial blast, SP 7 brush-off (sweep) blast, SP 10 near-white blast, SP 11 power tool to bare metal, SP 15 commercial-grade power tool [03:07][12:20][50:14]. Visual guides VIS 1 (dry abrasive), VIS 2 (degrees of rust), VIS 3 (power tool), VIS 4 (water blasting), VIS 5 (slurry) [08:46]. Abrasive standards AB 1, AB 2, AB 3 [12:51]. Certification programs SSPC QP 3 (shop painting) and AISC SPE (Sophisticated Paint Endorsement), being harmonized under ANSI public-comment procedures [15:56]. Coatings standards ISO 12944 for system selection, ISO 4628 breakdown grade RI3, and ASTM B117 salt-spray test [31:18][32:50]. AESS: AISC Code of Standard Practice sections 2.1 (what is structural steel), 2.2 (exclusions: cold-formed, cables, castings), 10.2 (AESS tolerances), 10.3 (fabricator special care), 10.4 (erector special care) [54:51][57:55][58:27].
### Technical Details
Cleanliness levels are defined by foreign matter remaining per nine-square-inch area: SP 5 white metal removes all foreign matter (cleanest), SP 10 near-white allows light staining and is the level typically required to strip all mill scale from new steel, SP 6 commercial allows 33 percent staining, SP 7 brush-off just roughens intact coatings [06:42][07:14][07:45]. The written consensus standard in the SSPC red book always holds legal precedence over the reference photographs [13:21]. Profile matters: angular grit produces a sharp peak profile and far greater surface area for adhesion than round shot, and profile is measured with a profile gauge [47:43][48:13]. Corrosion needs water, oxygen, and an electrolyte; loss can reach roughly 200 microns per year in aggressive atmospheres, faster with pitting [27:15][28:17]. Zinc-rich primers (zinc silicates and epoxy zincs) are the workhorse because zinc sacrifices itself, demonstrated on a scribed B117 panel where the scribe filled with zinc corrosion products and showed no rust [29:48][31:18]. Steel loses about half its strength near 550 C (400 C used offshore as a safety threshold); intumescent coatings swell and char to delay reaching the critical core temperature, rated in minutes (a 60-minute example shown) [37:30][42:35].
### Fabrication Notes
SP 1 solvent cleaning is mandatory before any abrasive or brush blasting so contaminants are not driven into the steel [03:39]. Shop blasting commonly uses centrifugal wheel (automated) machines roughly three times faster than hand-nozzle blasting, up to ten times on repetitive identical pieces, and these suit straight regular members but not unusual shapes [15:24][48:13]. Airless spray is the fastest, most economical coating-application method; brushing and rolling are reserved for small repairs [35:59][37:30]. Recycled shot and grit must be cleaned in a recycler and topped with new abrasive so adhesion is not lost to oil or lead pickup [14:53]. For AESS the Code of Standard Practice 10.2 roughly halves general tolerances; welds get no automatic special treatment, so welds ground smooth, contouring and blending of welds (notably on HSS), continuous versus stitched fillet welds, minimized weld show-through, tighter coping, and removal or grinding of piece marks and mill marks must each be specified separately [57:55][61:01][62:31].
### Erection Notes
Field connections are bolted or welded on site and always require some on-site touch-up painting, with SP 2 and SP 3 the methods typically specified for field work [49:00]. Field surface prep by scraper, wire brush, or needle gun is slow, unpleasant, often poorly done, and risks polishing the surface, which harms coating adhesion [49:44][50:14]. The Bristle Blaster (a wire brush with kinked bristles that impact rather than brush) was cited as new technology able to produce field steel clean enough for zinc silicates [50:44]. For AESS, Code section 10.3 requires the fabricator to take special care in handling and shipping, and 10.4 requires the erector to minimize visible erection damage; erection field aids should be removed if the architect or engineer requires it [58:27][63:03]. A closing audience exchange treated shimming for proper member fit-up as an erection tolerance issue rather than a specific AISC requirement [70:53][71:55].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The recurring field dispute is contractors specified to SP 6 commercial blast but effectively held to SP 10 near-white, since SP 10 demands more labor and abrasive [05:40][11:19]. Agreed comparator panels rust over time and give a false target unless sealed with a clear lacquer barrier [10:19]. Applying coatings over existing corrosion or polishing the surface during field prep both compromise adhesion [28:47][50:14]. The named AESS failure mode is the disconnect where engineers specify only to the Code of Standard Practice yet expect seamless ground-smooth welds and tight fit-up that the bare code does not guarantee [59:00]. A concrete example: a castellated beam (cut and rewelded) supplied for a 60 to 70 ft high gymnasium roof drew a dispute over ungrounded welds that no occupant could ever see, money that should not have been spent [66:38].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Coatings deliver their protection at only about 0.1 to 0.3 of total project cost, an easily justified spend against an unprotected frame [25:43]. SP 10 costs materially more than SP 6 in labor and abrasive, so the prep class written into a spec directly drives the blast line item [05:40]. ISO 12944 ties system thickness to environment and required life, with example systems ranging from 160 microns (C2) up to 335 microns (C5I, fifteen-year durability), each thickness a different material cost [35:26][36:29]. Heavier steel has a smaller surface-to-volume ratio and needs less intumescent thickness, so section factor drives fire-protection cost [42:35][44:37]. The AISC AESS brochure includes a cost matrix showing AESS can add roughly 100 to 200 percent for the highest category-one steel (within 20 ft and touchable), with a user-defined column so only the wanted items (for example welds ground smooth) are priced rather than the whole category [64:04][66:07][68:11].
