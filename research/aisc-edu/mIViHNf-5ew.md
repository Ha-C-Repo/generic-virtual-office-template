## Field Fixes - Part 9

### Core Concept
This webinar segment is a field-diagnostics walkthrough of recurring failures in industrial crane buildings and joist framing, drawn from the presenter's career as a forensic structural engineer. The controlling theme is that small, unconsidered details (a stiff tieback, an off-center rail, a diaphragm plate, an out-of-position anchor rod) turn into expensive field failures under repeated crane loading because the original detail did not account for movement, fatigue, or thermal expansion. The opening also covers joist serviceability around folding partitions and joist seat-depth coordination [00:01-02:35].

### Spec References
The presenter cites AISC Design Guide 7 for crane building design [03:38-04:08]. He references AISC member-supplied crane lateral and longitudinal load provisions that "used to be in the spec" and are now in ASCE 7 [07:16], noting the 20 percent lateral-load fraction historically used and 100 percent for mill/scrapyard cranes per the AISE mill-crane document. He cites AISC fabrication and rail-centering tolerances generally rather than as a numbered table [14:38, 16:12-16:43], and contrasts them with ASTM A6 mill tolerances. He states A325 bolts for rail joints [05:11] and references AWS welding only in passing for a field repair [09:54]. No AISC 360 section, 341 section, RCSC clause, or specific AWS D1.1 clause number is stated aloud.

### Technical Details
Long DLH and DH joists carry built-in camber (he cites 8 inches of camber on a 120-foot DLH joist) and details must accommodate it; a roof-deck diaphragm tie-in failed because the cambered joists rose above the wall [01:32-02:03]. Joist seat depths differ by series (a K-series 2.5-inch seat versus an LH 5-inch seat) and must be matched end to end or the floor steps [02:03-02:35]. Unequal joist or joist-girder reactions twist a supporting beam, requiring bottom-chord extensions or torsion design [02:35-03:06]. Crane fatigue is keyed to duty class: classes A, B, C are treated as no-fatigue-concern, while D, E, and F (mill) cranes require fatigue design [06:13-06:46]. AISC tolerance cited: rail eccentricity shall not exceed three-quarters of the girder web thickness, and a quoted runway alignment value of about a quarter inch in 50 feet maximum [15:41-16:43].

### Fabrication Notes
For crane runway plate girders, the recommended built-up detail is a complete-joint-penetration weld of the web to the top flange with a fillet-weld reinforcement (the mill-people detail); a fillet-only web-to-top-flange weld cracks under repeated wheel loads, especially when the section is out of square [13:04-14:07]. Rail joints should be specified as milled joints for a tight fit and bolted with A325 bolts, or welded for high-cycle service [04:40-05:11]. ASTM A6 sweep tolerance is an eighth inch per 10 feet, which over a 40-foot runway beam allows up to a half-inch offset, and the web can be up to 3/16 inch off the flange centerline, so a drawing note should require the fabricator to straighten the beam before shipping if it would leave the rail too far off the web [15:10-16:12].

### Erection Notes
The cautionary case is a 280-foot box-truss runway girder that erected with an S-kink because the column anchor rods at one end were set 6 inches out of straight; the girder was forced down onto them and permanently deformed, and the fix was to realign the crane aisles to the kink rather than rebuild [16:43-22:00]. Rail clips must provide positive lateral restraint while allowing longitudinal thermal movement, and a crane stop should sit about 2 inches off the rail end so thermal growth does not buckle the runway girder laterally [22:30-23:30]. Diaphragm plates added between runway girders make a simple span act continuous, generating unintended negative moment that shears bolts and elongates plates; the field fix is to remove them and reroute the loads [05:41-09:22].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named failures include cracked rivets in an old mill building that broke clapboard-sash windows [08:18-09:22], cracked and field-welded tiebacks that were too rigid and pulled until something broke [09:22-12:02], rattled-loose bolts in tieback connections that led to slotted holes and a switch to welding [11:30-12:02], fillet-welded crane-girder web-to-flange cracks from out-of-square fabrication [13:04-13:36], rail clip failures and rail gouging from misalignment or excessive building movement [22:30-24:32], loosened bolted rail butt joints that chip the rail and wear crane bearings [04:08-04:40], and joist deflection exceeding the 3/4-inch dust-skirt clearance of folding partitions so the partition cannot open [00:01-00:31].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The presenter flags that gantry-type patented tiebacks with spherical bearings are expensive but worth it on heavy high-cycle cranes because the failure becomes the manufacturer's problem, while welded maintenance-free rail joints are costly and not justified on light, infrequent-use buildings [05:11-05:41, 12:02-12:33]. He warns against accepting crane-supplier demands for eighth-inch tolerances that are not achievable, since agreeing to an impossible tolerance shifts every later crane problem onto the engineer [14:38-15:10]. The segment closes by previewing the partner's talk on fabricators complaining that design drawings lack information yet they are expected to do the work for "$1.98" [25:03-25:33].
