## Seismic Load Paths for Steel Buildings
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vFvN6JwYADc | Duration: 1:28:54 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Raphael Sabelli of Walter P Moore teaches the complete seismic load path for steel buildings, the path that connects every mass in the structure down to the foundation. The central idea is the fuse concept, also called capacity design [09:43] [11:15]: yielding elements (beams in a moment frame, braces in a braced frame) act as fuses that are deliberately weaker and more ductile than the protected elements (columns, collectors, connections), so inelastic deformation happens where it is wanted. Because design forces are reduced far below the maximum considered earthquake (MCE), the speaker explains the ASCE 7 reduction chain: take the MCE spectrum, multiply by 2/3 to reach the design-basis level, then divide by the R factor [04:35] [05:05], so an R=8 structure designs for roughly one twelfth of MCE forces [06:37]. The talk then walks the load path through foundations, diaphragms, chords, collectors, and ends on deformation compatibility for non-load-path elements like stairs.
### Spec References
The speaker repeatedly cites ASCE 7 for the load-reduction process, the omega-sub-zero (overstrength) factor, ASD load combinations for foundation stability, LRFD load combinations, and the C-sub-d deflection-amplification factor [04:18] [25:10] [74:06]. He names the AISC Seismic Design Manual (five-volume, which he chaired) as the source for the collector axial-strength tables [62:50] and as a general reference [77:13]. Seismic systems are named generically: special moment frame (SMF), special concentrically braced frame (SCBF), and eccentrically braced frame (EBF) [02:01]. No AWS D1.1 clause, RCSC pretension clause, or specific table number was stated aloud, so those are not cited here.
### Technical Details
Three diaphragm types are covered: bare steel deck welded or screwed or shot-pinned to framing, concrete-filled or composite deck, and horizontal steel truss diaphragms for industrial roofs [29:49] [31:53]. Diaphragm rigidity drives the analysis method: a flexible diaphragm (about an order of magnitude more flexible than the frames) is a determinate system solved like a beam, while rigid and semi-rigid diaphragms are indeterminate and need a 3D building model [42:16] [44:18]. The speaker stresses that a flexible diaphragm is not a simple span; multi-span and cantilever conditions get envelope chord forces, and seismic loads reverse so diagrams must be enveloped both ways [46:21]. Collectors are beam-columns with combined gravity flexure and seismic axial load; their compressive strength may be governed by constrained-axis flexural-torsional buckling when a composite deck restrains the top flange, which most software ignores in favor of conservative minor-axis buckling [60:46] [62:20]. The talk also covers the backstay effect in tall and podium buildings, where a shear reversal drives a force into the backstay diaphragm larger than the building base shear [38:43] [39:44], and P-delta thrust accumulating through gravity columns into the diaphragm [34:58].
### Fabrication Notes
This is a design webinar, so shop fabrication detail is light. The fabrication-relevant content is connection type selection for collectors and gravity beams: single-plate (shear tab) and double-angle connections from the manual carry large rotation capacity, single-plate connections tested at UC Berkeley exceeded 10 percent rotation [67:27], and typical connections are detailed for 3 percent rotation capacity within the design range before seismic drift is added [67:57]. For higher collector forces the speaker describes a welded top-flange detail (introduces eccentricity) or welding both flanges to make a moment connection, plus the extended single-plate method with a double column of bolts for added strength [65:26]. Collector reinforcement embedded in a composite deck must be sized for a width that keeps unconfined concrete stress below 0.5 f-prime-c [58:13]. No WPS, electrode, preheat, or weld-procedure tolerance was stated.
### Erection Notes
Erection-specific procedures, crane, rigging, and sequencing are not addressed in this design-focused webinar. The closest content is the stair-isolation detail meant to keep stairs off the seismic load path: tie one end hard (often a fully welded connection at the top) and tie the other end flexibly with a slotted-hole angle connection at the bottom so the stair moves with the upper diaphragm [69:34] [70:04], with the bolt deliberately made weak enough to shear before tearing up gravity support if drift exceeds the slot [71:06]. The speaker also stresses maintaining adequate building separation to prevent pounding, especially where mismatched story heights let one building strike another mid-column rather than at a deck [71:36] [72:06].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The speaker names several recurring design mistakes rather than shop inspection rejections. Assuming a flexible diaphragm is always a simple span is called a mistake many people make [46:21]. Placing a member with zero axial capacity where a multi-span moment diagram is assumed to cross zero is warned against, because the true zero location is uncertain [46:52]. Failing to tie a bare-joist-and-deck diaphragm together so it can pull apart is flagged [48:55]. Counting on foundation rocking or uplift to limit forces is discouraged because deep foundations carry large unaccounted overstrength [29:18], and the ASD-versus-LRFD mismatch can make a footing appear stable under one combination and not the other [25:10]. Letting stairs act as unintended brittle braces, and underestimating seismic drift past the detailed slot length, are both called out [69:02] [71:06].
### Bid / Cost Implications
In the closing Q&A the speaker addresses system selection cost directly [86:34] [87:34]. A fully elastic structure would be very strong and very expensive, which is why the R-factor reduction is used [05:35]. Choosing a special (high-R, ductile) system versus an ordinary (low-R) system is a real cost trade: special systems carry added inspection cost and possibly added erection time, while ordinary systems carry the cost of heavier foundations and the rest of the structure designed for higher accelerations [87:34] [88:05]. The speaker advises weighing not just steel tonnage but inspection, erection, and foundation costs together. Backstay overturning moments are described as very expensive to design for, so reducing them benefits structural economy [38:10].
