### Core Concept
This segment teaches how high-strength bolts develop and retain pretension through installation, and how inspection should match the load case rather than chase a number that cannot be measured after the fact. The central idea is that turn-of-nut and similar methods deliberately drive the bolt into the inelastic flat region of its load-elongation curve, so that very different snug elongations still converge on nearly the same pretension [02:36][03:38]. The speaker also covers when pretension is and is not actually required, the three main proprietary fastener systems, washer requirements, and a combined bolt-plus-weld joint example.

### Technical Details
Turn-of-nut runs the nut down to snug (full effort on a spud wrench or the first impact of an impact wrench, which sounds different), then applies an additional one-half, one-third, or two-thirds turn by bolt length, with one-half turn the usual case [00:01]. Snug elongations vary widely (roughly 0.03 to 0.05 in the example) yet pretensions land within a fraction of each other because the bolt is taken past yield into the flat portion [02:36][03:38]. Rotational tolerance past the half-turn mark is large: A325 can go nearly two more turns, A490 about one and a quarter, before failure [04:09]. Field measurements show actual pretension above specified: turn-of-nut A325 about 35% over, A490 about 26% over, and calibrated wrench about 13% over, and these margins are already built into the rules rather than available as extra capacity [15:07][15:37].

### Fabrication Notes
Rotation (rotational-capacity) tests are required for galvanized A325 bolts and are good insurance for any short-grip bolt (around an inch and a half where a half-turn becomes a third-turn), because short bolts may fracture during installation; A490 cannot be galvanized so the issue does not arise there [12:28][12:58]. Calibration of any installation method must be replicated on the job site, not done for convenience in the shop, to match field conditions [09:50]. For TC bolts and load-indicating washers the friction-controlling lubricant is proprietary and manufacturer-supplied, so the fabricator and installer may not clean or re-lubricate them and must use them in the as-delivered condition [18:12][18:42].

### Erection Notes
Pre-installation verification with a bolt calibrator (load cell) is required once per day and again whenever conditions change, for every method; you cannot return three weeks later and inspect seriously [08:46][09:19]. Work proceeds from the stiffer regions toward the edges to establish snug across the joint [00:01]. A bolt taken through installation rotation without fracture is highly likely satisfactory, and a later quarter-turn rarely causes failure except in exposed structures vulnerable to vandalism, such as towers [04:40][05:12]. TC bolts install from one side with a single operator using a quiet electric wrench, reducing crew and equipment on site [20:44].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The named failure mode for TC bolts is trusting the twist-off tip alone: very high friction (bolt dropped in sand) twists the spline off before adequate elongation, giving no pretension, while a super lubricant prevents twist-off yet over-elongates and does develop pretension. Tip twist-off does not by itself prove pretension; only calibration does [19:12][19:44][20:14]. Pretensions greater than specified are explicitly not cause for rejection [11:56][12:28]. Determining pretension after installation is essentially impractical; an ultrasonic bolt-load indicator costs about 25,000 to 30,000 dollars and is a research tool, not a production tool [05:12][05:42]. Snug-tight joints should not show baked-on gaps that invite corrosion in exposed structures [08:15].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Matching inspection to the load case controls cost: if pretension is not required (snug-tight bearing connections, A325 only, no fatigue or vibration), do not inspect for it; verify the right bolts, nuts, washers, and hole condition [06:44][07:14][07:44]. TC bolts are generally more expensive than standard assemblies but cut labor to a single-side, single-operator install [20:44]. Every method carries the same daily calibration overhead, so that cost is not a differentiator [20:44]. RCSC and the bolted-joint guide are free downloads, and AISC FAQ resources are free, which lowers the engineering-research cost on interpretation questions [22:18][24:20].

### Bolting / Welding Methods
Bolting methods named: turn-of-nut, calibrated wrench, tension-control (TC) bolts to ASTM 1852, and load-indicating washers (direct tension indicators) to F959. TC bolts are torque-dependent and shear off the spline at about 1.05 times the specified minimum pretension when correctly calibrated; load-indicating washers are elongation-dependent and read with a go/no-go feeler gauge on the flattened protrusions [16:08][17:41][21:14][21:46]. Washer rules: hardened washers are required for torque-based methods (calibrated wrench, TC bolts) and for DTIs, but not for turn-of-nut or snug-tight; special washers are needed for slip surfaces, for A490 on steels with yield below 40 ksi to prevent galling, and bar washers for some slotted or oversized holes [25:22][25:52][26:23]. The closing combined-joint example pairs bolts in shear with longitudinal and transverse fillet welds on a renovation, solved by aligning the load-deformation curves (transverse weld near ultimate, longitudinal at about 80%, bolt at about 40% of that deformation) [26:23][27:26][27:56].
