## Drawing and Specification Requirements for Seismic Design
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gr3G7fNniVI | Duration: 1:31:45 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
This 2016 AISC webinar by Larry Muir (AISC director of technical assistance) and Tom Sable (principal at Englekirk) covers what information the engineer of record must show on contract documents for a seismic project, defined as one that must meet AISC 341 the seismic provisions, versus a non-seismic project that only meets AISC 360 [01:02-01:32]. The driving message is that a fabricator bids what it sees in the contract documents, so missing or unclear seismic requirements lead to bids that come in at a fraction of the real cost and to change orders later [03:33-04:33]. Muir frames AISC 341 as a supplement to AISC 360, which applies to all projects seismic or not [11:37-12:07].

### Spec References
AISC 341 seismic provisions, including Section A4 (information to be shown on drawings), Chapter J (seismic inspection requirements), Chapter K (qualification testing of moment connections, stated as "I believe it's chapter K") [38:07], and the commentary chapter on demand-critical welds (stated as "Chapter D, I believe") [85:50]. AISC 360, with Chapter N for quality assurance and inspection [19:47, 47:19]. AISC 358 prequalified connections for special and intermediate moment frames. AWS D1.1 structural welding code and AWS D1.8 its seismic supplement (item 13 on assembly order/welding sequence [18:14, 55:08]; demand-critical weld definition; weld-access-hole welder qualification test [73:36]; tapered transition detail [76:38]). RCSC specification for structural joints using high-strength bolts, with the speaker noting there is no seismic supplement to the bolting spec [13:09]. IBC Section 1603 (seismic design parameters on drawings), 1704 / 1704.3 (special inspections), 1704.5 (structural observation) [43:12-48:20].

### Technical Details
Demand-critical welds are required only where AISC 341 lists them: groove welds at column splices and bases in higher-ductility systems (IMF, SMF, special truss moment frames, special cantilever columns, SCBF, EBF, BRBF, steel plate shear walls) and groove welds at beam-to-column connections in those systems, with the exception that beam-to-column groove welds are demand critical even in an ordinary moment frame [25:26-27:27]. The Code of Standard Practice gives three connection-design options: engineer completes all connection design, an experienced detailer selects from the AISC manual tables, or design is delegated to a licensed PE working for the fabricator [13:39-15:11]. Protected zones, lowest anticipated service temperature (the 50 degree F limit), and RBS dimensions A, B, C, and R are required drawing items [54:07, 72:34-73:05]. Backing-bar treatment depends on location: to the inside of a section (top flange of a moment beam, or a column base plate where backing is inside) the backing may stay with a contouring or reinforcing fillet; to the outside (bottom flange) the backing must be removed, back-gouged, and a smooth transition provided; butt-splice CJP welds at column splices generally need no backing removal [28:57-32:31, 89:27-90:58].

### Fabrication Notes
Muir recommends the engineer leave welding process, sequence, and technique to the fabricator's shop floor unless there is a very specific reason, since the shop owns the risk of weld cracking [18:45-19:47]. AISC 358 prequalification limits constrain fabrication: there are no prequalified connections to the weak axis (web) of a column, only built-up cruciform, box, or plated wide-flange columns provide four flanges; HSS options are limited to one concrete-filled HSS column option and one HSS beam option, with no prequalified HSS-column-to-HSS-beam joint [37:06-38:38]. RBS connections require rolled wide-flange or built-up I-shapes with limits on depth, weight, flange thickness, span-to-depth and width-to-thickness ratios, and lateral bracing [39:39-40:39]. A992 is not mandatory; A36 plate and grade 50 are explicitly allowed in AISC 358, and requiring all steel to be A992 can make a project unbuildable [23:53-24:56].

### Erection Notes
Anchor rods drive erection accuracy: grade 36 or 55 usually work for gravity, but seismic often needs grade 105, which cannot be welded, so a misplaced grade 105 anchor rod is very difficult to correct in the field [60:17-60:48]. SCBF gusset plates that buckle out of plane need a free-to-bend fold/bend line, and where the gusset is embedded in concrete or a metal-deck diaphragm a compressible material (about 1.5 inches, the detail says Styrofoam) must surround it so the bend line can form [80:15-82:18]. Structural observation focuses on first-time conditions, the first SMF connection for example, to confirm the contractor and inspector have read the design intent correctly [49:21-51:28].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Muir groups recurring problems as imagined requirements: belief that all steel must be A992 or grade 50, belief that all welds in the seismic force resisting system must be demand critical, and belief that all backing must be removed, none of which AISC 341 requires [23:23-25:26]. Sable adds that engineers sometimes throw up their hands and mark every weld demand critical, which wastes the owner's money [53:35-54:07]. The most frequent and costly error ("public enemy number one") is designing an IMF or SMF without first confirming a prequalified AISC 358 connection exists for the chosen members, which can force an expensive owner-funded testing program under AISC 341 [34:34-37:06]. Inspectors and detailers also confuse weld backing with weld run-off tabs; removing a tab does not require removing the backing [90:58]. Connection configurations are sometimes desired but not defined, violating the Code of Standard Practice when design is delegated.

### Bid / Cost Implications
The webinar's central cost point is that an informal study put a special moment frame (R near 8) at roughly 2.5 times the cost of an R-equal-3 system, because ductile detailing adds cost even though base shear drops [08:36-10:06]. Incomplete bid documents leave the fabricator a choice: bid what it sees and fight change orders later, or bid what it thinks the engineer wants and price itself out, which penalizes the most knowledgeable bidder [10:06-11:37]. Per the Code of Standard Practice, anything required but not in the contract documents is a scope change that justifies a price and schedule adjustment [07:35-08:36]. A discovered need for a physical connection-testing program is a cost a fabricator would not have carried in a bid [39:09].
