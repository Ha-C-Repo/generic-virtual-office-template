## Designing Structural Stainless Steel - Part 1

### Core Concept
This is part one of a two-part AISC webinar on designing structural stainless steel, presented by Katherine Husa of TMR Stainless [00:02]. It covers applications, history, performance, sustainability, the specific stainless families used in structural work, physical and mechanical properties, and a long treatment of corrosion-driven specification [01:33]. The governing reference throughout is AISC Design Guide 27, Structural Stainless Steel, issued September 2013, which covers hot-rolled and welded structural sections 0.125 in thick or greater, including I-beams, channels, angles, and hollow sections, plus fasteners and tension bars [05:07][05:37]. The central engineering message is that stainless is specified first by required corrosion resistance for the actual environment, then by mechanical and physical properties, because the name is stainless not stain-free and the wrong alloy will still pit, stain, or crack [22:42][23:12].

### Spec References

### Technical Details
Three families are covered: austenitic (304, 304L, 316, 316L, non-magnetic, strengthened by cold work, easiest to weld) [07:11]; duplex (UNS 32101/LDX 2101, 2304, 2205, magnetic, higher strength) [08:13][08:45]; and precipitation-hardened (17-4 PH, highest strength but least corrosion resistant, tension bars and fasteners only) [09:18]. The L suffix means low carbon, required for welding and weld corrosion integrity [07:41]. Stainless has no defined yield point, so yield is the 2% offset, and the elastic limit runs roughly 40 to 70% of that offset yield [10:21][88:29]. A pitting resistance equivalent number ranks relative corrosion resistance, with 17-4 PH lowest near 15, 304 a step up, 2205 around 35, and 2507 higher [25:15][25:45]. Austenitic grades hold excellent impact toughness to cryogenic temperatures; duplex thermal expansion is close to carbon steel while austenitic expansion is near copper [69:30][70:31]. Chloride stress corrosion cracking is the named failure mode for 304/316 in load-bearing high-chloride service, with six-moly austenitics or super-duplex 2507 recommended instead [46:33].

### Fabrication Notes
Welding is the common method and AWS D1.6 governs, covering all welding methods, all stainless grades, dissimilar stainless joints, and stainless-to-carbon joints, plus stud welding, pre-qualification, welder qualification, inspection, and rejection criteria [73:23][75:57][79:35]. Do not preheat austenitic or duplex stainless or you destroy corrosion resistance; only PH grades sometimes need careful preheat [73:54]. Use a matching, slightly higher-alloy filler so corrosion resistance survives alloy loss in the weld; 304L, 316L, and lean duplex can be welded without filler, while 2205 should almost always use filler [76:28][76:59]. Stainless joints use wider included angles, a smaller root face, an allowed root gap, and more tack welding than carbon steel [78:01]. Heat tint must be removed by pickling paste or abrasion (remove the tint plus a little base metal), with ASTM A380 for restoring corrosion resistance and A967 for passivation; A967 does not restore weld corrosion resistance [80:35][81:05][81:36]. Use experienced stainless fabricators, dedicated stainless work areas, thoroughly cleaned tooling, and never let carbon-steel-contaminated equipment touch stainless [82:43][83:14]. Do not use oxyacetylene welding on stainless [75:57].

### Erection Notes
The webinar states that fabrication and erection costs should be roughly equivalent to carbon steel [17:32], but it gives little field-sequencing, crane, or rigging content. The practical erection-adjacent cautions are galvanic: dissimilar metals in regular contact with moisture must be separated by insulating washers, paint, or a non-conducting barrier, or one metal corrodes rapidly [48:04][48:36]. A field example showed galvanized fasteners in a stainless assembly corroding fast and becoming the structural failure point because of an unfavorable small-anodic to large-cathodic surface-area ratio [49:38][50:46]. Thermal expansion must be accommodated during installation: 316 wall panels installed flat in winter with no expansion room ended permanently oil-canned [69:30].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named failures include chloride stress corrosion cracking that split a 304 load-bearing deck a third of the way through in a high-snow region [46:01]; a broken 316 ceiling bolt in a load-bearing application [46:01]; carbon-steel framing under a Tokyo cathedral roof corroding away from condensation contact [51:47]; galvanized fasteners corroding catastrophically when paired with stainless or carbon surfaces because of surface-area ratio [49:38][50:46]; crevice corrosion concentrated under washer heads and inside cable strands even when exposed members stayed clean [54:26][54:58]; and accelerated corrosion from rough finishes above Ra 0.5 microns (20 microinches) [56:01][56:34]. Specifying AWS D1.1 instead of D1.6, calling out obsolete ASTM A167, or specifying pressure-rated pipe where structural tube would do are all named specification errors [64:48][66:22][73:23]. Welder certifications for the specific alloy and method should be in the bid package so no one is learning on the job [78:31].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Raw-material premium over carbon steel, before fabrication: rolled angles and channels in 316L run about four to five times; duplex welded structural sections about six times; 316L welded structural sections about eight times [16:32][17:02]. Those figures exclude offsetting savings: no galvanizing or painting, possibly no fireproofing, no coating-reapplication maintenance, and potential section-size reduction with higher-strength duplex [17:02][17:32]. Stainless rebar installed cost runs only 2 to 12% higher than carbon when repair and replacement are counted [18:33]. The recommended bid-time analysis is a life-cycle cost analysis rather than first-cost comparison [16:02]. Stock sizes are more limited (typically only 304/304L/316/316L stocked), so custom fabrication and short runs through specialty sources add lead-time and cost considerations [61:11][61:43].
