## Constructability of Structural Steel Buildings: Part 1
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0BIruFjIYNk | Duration: 1:10:46 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This is the primer session in a four-part AISC series on constructability presented by Dave Ruby and Jay Ruby of Ruby and Associates. The thesis is that constructability is a design philosophy, not a one-time event or a value-engineering check at 30/60/90 percent [35:33] [50:42]. The recurring theme is breaking the "silos of influence" where designer, fabricator, and erector each optimize on first-dollar (lowest first cost) in isolation, which does not yield the lowest-cost or most-buildable structure [05:16] [29:27]. The fix is to infuse construction knowledge and a construction professional onto the design team from the feasibility stage forward, not at 30 or 60 or 90 percent [32:01] [32:31].
### Spec References
No AISC 360, AISC 341, AWS D1.1, or RCSC section numbers are cited in this session; it is a philosophy and delivery primer. The speaker references the AISC constructability Design Guide being sold on the AISC website as the companion document but does not give a Design Guide number [01:09]. He cites the Construction Industry Institute (CII), affiliated with the University of Texas at Austin, and its 1989 definition of constructability as "the optimum use of construction knowledge and experience in planning, design, procurement, and field operations to achieve overall project objectives" [20:40] [32:01], plus a 2010 expansion adding building operations and maintenance [36:38]. General principles otherwise, no spec section cited.
### Technical Details
The most concrete technical content is a bay-size framing study comparing 30, 40, and 60 foot bays for the same building envelope [41:22] [43:29]. At 30 foot bays: 11 trusses, 786 joists, 144 bottom-chord braces with 228 connections, 145 struts with 290 connections, 11 columns, 40 diagonals, 111 joints in the bracing. At 40 foot bays: 8 trusses, 72 braces, 74 struts with 148 connections, 8 columns, 30 diagonals. At 60 foot bays: 6 trusses, 450 joists, 102 braces, 36 struts. The point is that tonnage is nearly identical across all three ("throw a blanket over the difference in tonnage") but piece count and connection count, which drive fabricator man-hours, change dramatically [42:58] [43:59]. The cladding hides the difference so the owner cannot tell the bays apart [42:58].
### Fabrication Notes
The session frames fabrication cost as a man-hour problem, not a dollars-per-ton problem [43:59]. Fabricators buy raw steel by the ton, then add man-hours for painting, shipping, handling, cleaning, drilling, welding, and bolting; piece count and detail complexity are the real cost drivers before a fabricator gives a number [37:09] [44:00]. The drivers for a fabricator are stated as numbers of pieces and complexity of details [37:41]. No tolerances, fit-up procedures, or shop QC steps are specified in this primer.
### Erection Notes
Erection is treated through site-coordination and access rather than field procedure. The speaker stresses evaluating site review, truck lay-down, lifting space, and crane positioning, none of which sit on the designer's decision matrix but all of which affect buildability [55:14] [55:47]. He gives the example of congested sites where erection can only run off the street from 10 a.m. to 2 p.m. [55:47]. No bracing, sequencing, or rigging specifics are given.
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
No inspection failures, weld rejections, or bolting rejections are named; this primer does not cover NDE or QA/QC. The "errors" discussed are process errors: making the structural system decision weeks before the mechanical engineer is involved [28:55] [57:20], treating BIM as a substitute for full design drawings rather than a tool [24:51] [25:21], and waiting for a 30/60/90 constructability review, which only critiques what was done and never what was not done [35:33] [36:04].
### Bid / Cost Implications
This is the strongest section for estimating. A single connection-type decision on a 52-kip single-shear reaction with six bolts ranges from about $21 (snug-tight, threads excluded from shear plane) to $28 (threads in shear plane), to $31.50 and $38 for pretensioned bearing bolts, to $68 for slip-critical, and that cost is bolts and installation only [39:14] [40:17] [40:52]. A connection redesign on one project cut bolt count from 44,000 to 21,000, a direct man-hour and schedule reduction [41:22]. Reported value-engineering returns: a $20,000 investment yielding $100,000 to $400,000, and a $200,000 investment yielding $1 to $4 million, from a study of 100 to 150 projects [38:44]. Case studies cite roughly $500,000 saved and 17 weeks cut on a 150,000 SF hospital (about $3.75/SF) [46:08] [47:10], $800,000 and 16 weeks on a 105,000 SF Georgia project [47:40], and $4.5 million and 3 months on a 1.2M SF Detroit hospital by switching a concrete core to steel [52:43] [53:43]. The closing argument is that the controlling metric is dollars per square foot, not dollars per ton: one four-option study ran $1,000 to $2,100 per ton but $88.80 to $113.75 per SF [65:48] [66:20].
