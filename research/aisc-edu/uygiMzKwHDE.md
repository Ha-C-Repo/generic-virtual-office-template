### Core Concept
This is session one of AISC Night School 4, Fundamentals of Earthquake Engineering for Building Structures, presented by Raphael Sabelli, SE, on February 24, 2014 [00:01]. It is a foundations lecture on seismology and earthquake effects, not a connection or fabrication session. Sabelli covers plate tectonics, how stored strain energy releases as ground-shaking waves, how earthquakes are measured, and the hazard, risk, and probability framework that drives code design accelerations [09:15]. The central engineering point is that we do not design from a Richter magnitude directly to a force; we design to a target acceleration tied to a probability of exceedence or return period, and the strategies to resist horizontal shaking and overturning are strength, stiffness, displacement capacity, and energy absorption [31:57][46:25].

### Technical Details
Earthquakes originate where tectonic-plate strain energy ruptures along a fault, radiating primary (pressure), secondary (shear), and surface (Rayleigh and Love) waves; surface waves carry most of the damaging energy to structures [15:22][84:36]. Ground-motion intensity attenuates with distance from the source, but soil controls amplification: soft bay mud acts like a soft spring and amplifies motion far more than bedrock [20:31]. Richter magnitude is logarithmic at roughly 32x energy per unit; Modified Mercalli is a subjective 12-step intensity scale keyed to observed effects on conventional construction [23:08][24:40]. Current ASCE 7 uses a risk-targeted MCE with a 1% chance of collapse in 50 years, replacing the old uniform-hazard 2,475-year (2% in 50-year) return period [65:25][66:28]. Brittle systems are designed for higher forces than ductile systems to keep collapse reliability high [76:52].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
No connection rejections, weld defects, or inspection failures are discussed. The failure modes shown are geotechnical and structural-system level: liquefaction of saturated sandy soils causing loss of bearing and rigid buildings tipping over intact [43:54][44:24], lateral spreading and sand boils [44:54], fault-rupture offset under structures, and code prohibitions on building over known active faults or unimproved liquefiable sites [42:20][44:24]. A common conceptual error the speaker corrects is treating "we are due for an earthquake" as fact; risk does rise as strain accumulates, but a magnitude 6 does not meaningfully relieve a magnitude 7 fault [82:02].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Cost here is societal earthquake loss, not bid estimating: Northridge at roughly 20 billion dollars and the Tohoku Japan event as the costliest on record are used to motivate design [38:11]. The design-economics point relevant to estimating is that the seismic design category and ground-motion level vary by site and govern lateral-system demand, and the move to uniform risk gives modest relief (0 to about 15 percent) in low-seismic regions like New York versus high-seismic Los Angeles [71:04]. For a Houston, TX fabricator this signals low seismic demand locally, but any out-of-region or higher-SDC job changes the lateral system, member sizes, and connection demand that drive tonnage and labor.
