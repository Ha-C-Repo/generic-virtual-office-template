## Lateral-Torsional Buckling and its Influence on the Strength of Beams
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QtyGWZDPtCI | Duration: 1:29:46 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This NASCC webinar by Craig Quadrado and Perry Green covers lateral-torsional buckling (LTB) as the limit state that governs the flexural strength of beams, defined at [03:35] as a geometric instability combining lateral translation of the compression flange and twist of the cross section. The presenters frame the AISC beam curve as three regions tied to unbraced length: elastic LTB at long unbraced lengths, inelastic LTB in the middle, and full plastic moment capacity Mp at short unbraced lengths [05:38][06:08]. The controlling design variable for a given cross section is the unbraced length Lb [04:35], where Lb is the distance between points that restrain lateral displacement of the compression flange or restrain twist of the section [08:38]. Green's research half derives rotation capacity and shows that achieving Mp also requires a stocky (compact) flange and effective bracing, not just short Lb [11:45][13:15].

### Spec References
The talk lives in AISC 360-16 Chapter F, primarily Section F2 with reference down into Section F3 for non-compact and slender flange sections [02:35]. Specific citations: Section F3 paragraphs (b) and (c) for reduced strength under flange local buckling [15:19]; the statement that full plastic moment is the optimum use of steel on manual page 16.1-323 [06:38]; the list of shapes susceptible to flange local buckling on manual page 16.1-47 [15:50]; AISC Commentary Appendix 6.3 for bracing design [10:42]; the rotation-capacity and compact-section definitions in the AISC 360-16 Glossary [20:58][31:45]; AISC 341 as the home for seismic and cyclic loading beyond the static basis of 360 [19:57]; Commentary to F1 for alternate Cb formulations including reverse-curvature and uplift cases [71:35][72:36]; and the SSRC Guide to Stability Design Criteria for Metal Structures and the fire/elevated-temperature appendix tables [78:13][35:26]. The compactness limit is given verbatim as 0.38 times the square root of E over Fy [13:15], replacing the legacy 65 over the square root of Fy from the 9th edition ASD [33:53]. The Cb equation is stated as 12.5 Mmax over (2.5 Mmax + 3MA + 4MB + 3MC) using absolute values [68:57].

### Technical Details
Mp equals Z times Fy [65:54]. Lr is set by making the elastic moment equal 0.7 Fy times Sx, where 0.7 accounts for residual stress, then solving for unbraced length [64:53]; Quadrado notes as SSRC Task Group 2 chair that this residual-stress reduction for modern hot-rolled shapes may be revisited [64:53]. Lp is anchored in equations near the plastic range (with GJ set to zero and Me set to Mp) but its final form is experimentally derived to deliver the rotation capacity [65:54][66:24]. Compact sections must develop a rotation capacity of approximately three before onset of local buckling [31:45], a requirement that matters for cyclic/seismic loading, not monotonic [32:48]. Green's 13-specimen program found monotonic tests reach rotation capacities of eight or nine while cyclic moment-gradient loading drops them toward three [38:30][41:34], and higher-yield steels (50, 60, 65, 80, 100 ksi grades discussed) carry less rotation capacity than A36 [38:30]. The open wide-flange torsional constant J equals the sum of (b t cubed over 3) for the three plates, roughly 4 in^4 class versus about 22.95 for a comparable closed tube [53:00][74:40]. Both E and Fy drive slenderness, and elevated temperature (200 to 300 degrees on industrial supports) reduces it as well [34:55].

### Fabrication Notes
The practical fabrication theme is reusing one critical-beam section across many members so the shop runs fewer distinct pieces even when most beams never see full load [42:11]. Producers have nearly eliminated flange local buckling from rolled shapes except the specific list on manual page 16.1-47, and the manual flexural-strength tables already bake in the reduced values for those shapes [15:50]. For built-up plate girders that cannot be looked up, J is computed as the sum of (b t cubed over 3) and flange local buckling is managed by using thicker flange plates rather than changing material [15:19][53:00]. The Q&A discusses variable-cross-section built-up members made from coil/plate where thickness or width is profiled along the length, with the caution that a single Cb cannot be applied across a varying section [85:43][86:52]. The presenters do not state AWS D1.1 weld procedure detail; welding appears only in the lab context of vertical flange-to-flange stiffeners welded to specimen ends to act as end restraint [45:47].

### Erection Notes
The strongest erection message is that bracing controls Lb only if it is effective, meaning it restrains twist, is not connected at the shear center, and is not taken at a point of inflection [10:11][10:42]. Reverse-curvature points of inflection are explicitly not valid brace points [10:11]. During steel erection before the deck is placed, the girder unbraced length is the spacing of cross frames or the distance between the two beams providing twist resistance, not a continuously braced length [09:09][09:40]. A repeated field warning: when re-roofing, removing deck or purlins removes top-flange (compression-flange) lateral support, so contractors must confirm stability before stripping too far [59:44][60:14]. The Edmonton bridge girder failure is attributed not to absence of bracing but to bracing that was too slender to be effective, the braces themselves buckled [11:13][11:45]. Warping-restraint tubes between flanges at girder ends gave a 30 percent class increase (up to about 130 percent more lateral capacity in a W30x90 test) and are most useful during erection while the steel stays elastic, before deck and final bracing are in place [73:39][75:41][81:17].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named errors: treating points of inflection as brace points [10:11]; designing bracing that is not effective (insufficient brace force, a failed specimen brace rated for only 10 to 15 kips of flange force buckled and lost rotation capacity, forcing a rebuilt test) [28:43][29:13]; computing a nominal moment Mn greater than Mp from a Cb multiplier and using it instead of capping at Mp [70:29][71:35]; and forgetting that Cb uses absolute values of the moments so reverse curvature must not be entered as a negative [69:27]. A material-specific error is using table values valid only for A992 grade-50 steel after the job switches to a different grade or higher yield, which requires recomputing slenderness from the spec rather than the manual tables [17:21][39:00]. Field LTB failures shown include permanently bent bridge girders from overload and elastically buckled girders that recovered when load was removed [05:38][06:08].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The cost logic is to design one governing beam and replicate it so fabrication is simpler and most members pass by inspection, accepting that non-critical beams are under-utilized in exchange for fewer section types [42:11][42:42]. Living with a small strength reduction from a slightly slender flange can be more economical than switching cross sections mid-structure when the lower-strength region is also lightly loaded [15:19]. Higher-strength steels look attractive for weight but the talk warns rotation capacity and slenderness limits degrade with yield strength, so a higher-grade substitution can force added analysis and may not bank the expected savings [38:30][39:30]. Warping-restraint tube devices add elastic capacity cheaply during erection but were not widely adopted in buildings because building members are usually not kept in the elastic range, unlike bridge erection under AASHTO [73:39][74:10].
