## ATFP Retrofits for Unconventional Buildings
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=p__OuSqnNFk | Duration: 1:00:51 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This is a blast and progressive-collapse retrofit webinar by two physical-security engineers from Thornton Tomasetti, covering anti-terrorism force-protection (ATFP) upgrades to two existing steel and concrete buildings that were never designed for those loads [02:12]. The teaching focus is which federal security standard governs (DoD owned versus DoD leased versus other federal agencies), how blast facade hardening and progressive-collapse mitigation interact, and how to develop reactions into an existing structure that has nothing substantial to anchor to. It is a structural-retrofit and detailing webinar, not an AISC member-design course, so steel-spec content is thin and the dominant standards are the security UFCs and ISC documents.

### Spec References
The standards named are mostly federal security criteria, not AISC: UFC 4-010-01 minimum anti-terrorism standards for DoD owned facilities, spoken as "UFC 4-1001" [04:14]; the companion UFC blast and progressive-collapse documents spoken as "4-2001" and "4-1002" [10:35]; the Interagency Security Committee (ISC) standards that govern DoD leased and most non-DoD federal space per a December 2012 DoD memo [07:25]; "PDC TR 06" / "PDC TR 1006", the Protective Design Center technical report for ductility and rotation response limits in single-degree-of-freedom analysis [13:13][13:46]; and the GSA interpretation document for ISC facilities [13:46]. AISC-adjacent references stated once at [17:21] are ASCE 59-11 (spoken "ASC 59 11"), AISC Steel Design Guide 26 (spoken "ASC steel design guide 26"), and the AISC blast and progressive-collapse resource; the speaker notes these are not formally referenced by the UFC or ISC but are useful. No AISC 360 section, AISC 341 seismic provision, AWS D1.1 clause, or RCSC bolting reference was cited.

### Technical Details
Case one is a 130,000 SF three-story steel building, originally a tennis dome with two wings of column-free space framed by three 120-foot trusses carrying bar joists, later infilled with two structurally independent steel-framed office floors on open-web joists and a thin 1.5-inch slab on 9/16-inch rolled deck [17:51][18:55][24:28]. Because that thin slab and the open-web joists gave nothing to anchor to, the facade fix was a three-story cable-catch system: shatter-resistant film on the glass plus half-inch cables (about 20,000 lb break strength each) run from a new ground-floor grade beam up to new tubes on the roof underside, with sacrificial fuse plates (knife plates, slotted holes, Teflon bearing pads that slide) so a half-inch cable could survive 100 psi-msec impulse loads that otherwise demanded inch-plus cables at 50,000 to 60,000 lb [24:58][27:30][28:32]. Case two is a 2013 post-tensioned concrete office with banded and uniform tendons; the facade fix was security film plus an HSS steel-tube backup behind every vertical mullion, connected by discontinuous aluminum extrusions at three points, with bent edge plates that bear on the slab inbound and FRP plus epoxy developing the rebound (outbound) force without drilling anchors into the PT slab [40:21][42:22][46:28][49:38]. Progressive collapse on case two used the GSA-allowed specific-local-resistance method: harden columns for the defined threat rather than do alternate-path column removal, ultimately a four-sided steel jacket on the columns [41:52][57:26].

### Fabrication Notes
The engineer of record detailed all the connections in-house rather than delegating reactions to a steel fabricator, because the multiple loading cases, large eccentricities, and very high blast forces made delegated connection design impractical [32:39]. A steel fabricator was brought on early and worked directly with the engineer on connection layout to fit the fabricator's tolerances and the field tolerances of an existing building [32:39]. On case two, steel tubes were chosen over aluminum backups partly because aluminum extrusions had a long lead time while steel tube was readily available, and tube-plus-knife-plate assemblies were fabricated first so the aluminum extrusions could drop in later [43:54][44:57]. Plates were coped as much as possible to cut weight and material cost, because every piece had to ride the building elevators, and the coping and sequencing of which plate went where was described as quite complicated [52:15].

### Erection Notes
Both retrofits were erected from inside the occupied or finished shell because schedule barred removing the roof, the windows, or the facade [22:58][35:43]. Ring beams and column-pickup assemblies had to be buildable inside and lifted into place; on case one, collars and rods were dropped through the slab to grab columns from above [37:50][38:20]. On case two, all steel moved through the elevators, so weight and plate size were hard erection constraints, and overhead FRP installation made the contractor nervous but went fine [52:15][51:44]. Selective demolition before erection mattered: the drawings showed a three-inch slab-to-curtainwall gap but as-built ranged from zero to three inches due to construction tolerance, so details had to be adjusted in the field [50:09][50:41].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
No formal inspection-failure or rejection categories were named; the warnings are field lessons. Trusting the construction documents was the recurring error: the case-two shop drawings were treated as the as-built and proved conflicting, and the actual slab edge condition forced detail revisions [45:58][50:09]. A demonstrated bad practice from a separate building was cables fished through drilled holes in steel angle and wrapped loosely around members, not pulled tight, which the speaker said will fray off when they scrape the steel and fail because cable systems depend on pretension [30:35][31:07]. Cutting through and failing to restore fire-stopping at the slab edge was flagged as an easy miss, resolved by working with the firestop manufacturer [51:11].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Schedule was the dominant cost driver, not material: case one ran a three-month design and six-month construction window, and the team explicitly said most of these retrofits would not be chosen for ninety percent of buildings if schedule were not forcing the decision [21:26][39:20]. Procurement lead time drove material choice, with long-lead aluminum extrusions losing to readily available steel tube [43:54]. Hidden site costs were large: removing and reinstalling multi-ton precast panels at an urban location required pulling sidewalk and pavers and a special permit, a major cost line [54:22]. Constant on-site presence and tight architect-contractor-subcontractor coordination were repeatedly cited as the price of these short-duration jobs [59:31].
