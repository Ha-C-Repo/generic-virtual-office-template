## Around the Bend - What You Need to Know about Steel Bending
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UjaHYlX3b-M | Duration: 48:48 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This is an AISC Bender-Roller Committee webinar (TD Alwood, AISC regional engineer; Barry Feldman, president of Cotler Metal Products) on cold and hot bending of structural shapes [00:01][03:09]. The committee is 13 member firms (11 US, 2 Canada) formed to fill an information gap because curved steel had been treated as a "black box" with no published tolerance or specification guidance [00:34][03:40]. The central message is that bending is a craft and proprietary process that varies by shop, so the controlling deliverable is complete, unambiguous shape, radius, orientation, and end-use information given to the bender before material is purchased [01:37][08:50][09:20].
### Spec References
Almost no formal code sections are cited. The speakers name architecturally exposed structural steel (AES, called "AESS" generically) as the highest-quality finish baseline [04:11][10:54], and reference ASTM A500 by name as the HSS tube standard whose dimensional tolerances are "exhaustive" and can leave tube shape and radius off [32:42]. They point to AISC resources: the Bender-Roller web page (cited as aisc.org/benders), the Steel Solution Center / "think tank," the curved-steel brochure, the nomenclature chart, and a cold-bending paper. No AISC 360 chapter, AISC 341 section, AWS D1.1 clause, RCSC provision, or Design Guide number was stated, and no bending tolerance standard exists per the speaker [36:15].
### Technical Details
Six bending methods are described: rotary draw (compression) bending for very tight radii and high repetition, limited by machine and tooling size [05:43][06:13]; rolling (pyramid/three-roll) bending, the most common in structural work, done in XY coordinates up to 360 degrees [06:43]; point or gag (ram) bending applying point loads at measured increments [07:16]; synchronized incremental cold bending with internal support for tight-radius HSS [07:47]; hot bending around pins and acorn plates at full chrome points [07:47]; and induction bending with an electric coil heating a short length for large-diameter pipe and heavy wall, common in oilfield work [08:19]. Required inputs are shape, size, radius, chord or depth dimension, angle, and orientation (easy way vs hard way, leg/flange in vs out, odd-leg angle leg position) [09:20][09:52][10:23]. Examples shown include W27x146 rolled easy way, W18x40 hard way with no web or flange distortion, W24 Hummer-dealership beams bent hard way, and spiral stairs needing arc degree, rise, run, and overall rise [06:43][14:58][16:29].
### Fabrication Notes
Hold and tail stock is required between the rolls, so the bender needs extra material beyond the finished arc length: roughly 6 ft (3 ft per end) for W8-W10 easy way and W6-W24 easy way, about 11 ft (5.5 per end) for W6-W24 hard way, and 10 ft easy / 14 ft hard for W27-W40 [13:27][13:57][14:27]. Trimming and whether straight ends are needed must be stated, and a customer who supplies exactly the arc length leaves the piece short, forcing welds or splices [12:57][13:27]. QC is a visual inspection with XY coordinates marked on the floor and the piece checked against a square every 12 inches before shipment; tank work can hold an eighth-inch on chord over a 40 ft piece, sometimes by tack-welding an angle across the chord to hold dimensions while the piece relaxes [27:29][37:48]. A500 tube variation argues for ordering all pieces in an assembly from the same mill and heat number for consistent initial shape [32:42]. Galvanizing or painting after bending must be disclosed because some benders heat-correct parts, which affects coating [33:13].
### Erection Notes
The session is shop-focused, but transport is treated as an erection-handoff risk. Curved pieces must ship flat, never with the legs in the air, because road bounce opens the curve, and over-tight strapping twists hard-way beams or opens the bend, so blocking and tie-down must respect the built-up stress in the member [34:45][35:15]. Even crane handling of a heavy section (example: 7-inch tee at 55 lb/ft) can change a tight-tolerance bend, which is why tack bars are added to hold geometry through handling [37:48]. Field fit-up to a defined point A to point B should be flagged to the bender so they engineer a support to keep the chord-and-depth geometry in place on delivery [38:19].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named failures include web and flange distortion, oil canning, and interior ripples on tube (acceptable if wall-encased, rejectable on AES) [11:25][11:55][27:00]. A spiral stair was bent to a 38 ft radius when the design meant 38 ft diameter, delaying a restaurant opening (nomenclature error) [16:59]. An audience member reported large G-shaped pieces that should have matched arriving with 6 to 8 inch field fluctuation with no change-order relief [28:00]. The presenter cited his own shop failing a 20x12x0.5 wall tube at a $3,800 replacement cost, and noted bends too tight to roll were instead fabricated from bent plate and welded up [29:04][45:31]. Standard tolerances do not exist, and AES not stated up front cannot be recovered after the fact [36:15][33:44].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Bending is an added process cost on top of base steel, and the increment scales with method, radius tightness, wall thickness, and shape complexity [18:34][26:28]. Tighter and multi-radius or parabolic curves cost more than a single constant radius [43:00]. AES specified after quote raises cost because it may require a different machine, a heavier wall, or different internal support [33:44]. Estimators must include hold-and-tail material in the buy, since being a couple feet short on 100 beams is a costly rework [30:06]. The recurring guidance is to consult the bender and price the curve before the buyer sees it, so steel is not priced out of a job by a surprise [43:00][08:50].
