## Direct Analysis Method Applications and Examples
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dcKQ0SD-rp8 | Duration: 1:28:53 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
This webinar by David Landis of Walter P Moore (recorded December 8, 2016) teaches the Direct Analysis Method (DAM) for steel stability design, a second-order approach that captures both P-big-delta system effects and P-little-delta member effects directly and lets the engineer design with K equal to 1 [01:33], [02:03]. DAM works by accurately modeling frame behavior, factoring all loads even for ASD, including initial frame imperfections through notional lateral loads, reducing member stiffness to account for residual stresses and distributed plasticity, running a second-order analysis, and using K equal to 1 in all member equations [19:03], [36:30]. The recurring lesson is that the effective length method underpredicts the moments needed to stabilize a frame at the strength limit state, which matters for base plates, anchor rods, beams, and connections [16:29], [16:59].

### Spec References
Cited verbatim by the speaker: AISC Specification Appendix 7 (where DAM first appeared in 2005, and where the effective length and first-order methods now live as alternates) [01:33], [02:33]; AISC Specification Chapter C (the primary stability method since 2010) [02:33]; Appendix 8 amplified first-order B1 and B2 factors [08:15]; AISC Design Guide 28 "Stability Design of Steel Buildings" by Larry Griffis and Don White [02:33]; the AISC Code of Standard Practice column out-of-plumbness tolerance of H over 500, consistent with the 0.002 notional factor [24:05]; Chapter H interaction equations, specifically H1-1A [42:04], [43:37]; Chapter F flexural equations F1-1 for Cb and F2-2 for moment capacity [48:09]; Chapter E compression equations including the simplified equation referenced as 3-2 [47:39], [70:36]; Manual Table 3-2 for moment capacity [70:05]; the sidesway-uninhibited alignment chart and the story-buckling method in the Specification commentary for adjusting K for leaning columns [67:33], [68:33]. ASCE 7-05 load combinations and seismic provisions (R equal to 3 for systems not detailed for seismic, Cd equal to 3) are cited for the examples [44:37], [57:18], [62:26].

### Technical Details
Notional lateral loads are 0.002 times alpha times Yi, where alpha is 1.0 for LRFD and 1.6 for ASD, applied at each level per that level's gravity load and in the most destabilizing direction [24:36], [52:35]. Stiffness reduction is 0.8 EA axial and 0.8 tau-b EI flexural, with tau-b equal to 1 when required axial strength is below half the yield load (PR over PY less than 0.5), which is common for moment frames [28:42], [46:38]. A simplification lets you raise notional loads to 0.003 times the gravity loads and then take tau-b equal to 1 without calculating it [29:12]. When the second-order to first-order ratio exceeds 1.7 on reduced stiffness or 1.5 on nominal stiffness, notional loads must be added to all lateral combinations [25:39], [33:54]. Compression members should be meshed, the speaker uses four segments, to capture P-little-delta [31:48]. Serviceability checks, drift, building period, mode shapes, and vibration use the full unreduced stiffness, not the reduced stiffness [35:29], [58:22]. Examples covered a grain-bin frame, an unsymmetrical moment frame with a leaning column carrying 1875 kips, an ASD moment frame, a 10-story dual system, and long-span roof trusses at the KFC Yum Center and the Marlins Park retractable roof.

### Fabrication Notes
The webinar is an analysis and design webinar and does not cover shop fabrication, fit-up, or shop QC. The one fabrication-adjacent point is that out-of-straightness from fabrication tolerance is already built into the column strength curves, so only story out-of-plumbness needs explicit modeling [22:35].

### Erection Notes
Erection process is not covered, but the 0.002 notional factor is tied directly to the erection-stage tolerance: the AISC Code of Standard Practice allows column out-of-plumbness of H over 500, and the notional load reproduces the destabilizing force from that built-in lean [24:05]. If a job specifies and verifies a tighter plumbness tolerance, the factor may be reduced; if tolerances are relaxed or an out-of-tolerance condition must be checked, a larger factor is required [24:05]. This is the link between erection survey results and the design assumption.

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The dominant error named is forgetting leaning columns: gravity columns that are not part of the lateral system still lean on the frame and add P-big-delta, and engineers analyzing isolated 2D models often omit them, which understates the destabilizing demand [20:03], [21:03]. The second named error is K-factor misapplication under the effective length method, especially failing to adjust K for leaning columns; in Example 4 the alignment-chart K of 1.4 had to be raised to 2.52 by the story-buckling method, and software that returns only the 1.4 leaves an unconservative design unless corrected by hand [67:33], [68:33]. A third pitfall is illegitimately superimposing separately run linear load cases, since the second-order response is nonlinear and must be run as a single combined nonlinear case [07:43], [33:24].

### Bid / Cost Implications
DAM produces higher design moments than the effective length method because the frame stiffness is softened, so beams, columns, connections, base plates, and anchor rods carry larger stabilizing moments, which can drive heavier members or beefier connections on flexible frames [18:31], [16:59]. For stiff frames the difference is small and there is little cost impact [82:24]. The iterative cycle (analyze, design-check, rerun for stiffness, recheck, and repeat on any size change) is real engineering labor that an estimate of design or detailing hours should account for on moment-frame and long-span work [30:47], [33:24].

### Your Company Application
The load-bearing takeaway for Your Company is the base-plate and anchor-rod design question at [87:32]: the moments coming out of a DAM analysis are the valid moments for sizing base plates, anchor rods, and foundations, while the notional shears are fictitious and may be subtracted out (moments must be kept, shears are optional). That ties straight to our in-house PE stamp work and anchor-rod design, setting, and tolerance scope: the H over 500 plumbness tolerance from the Code of Standard Practice at [24:05] is the same tolerance our erection crew under Mario and the field survey must hold for the design assumption to be valid, so a survey that comes back worse than H over 500 is not just a field issue, it can invalidate the stability basis and should trigger a check. For Ivan's takeoff and estimating, flag that flexible moment frames and long-span systems analyzed by DAM tend to need heavier members and larger connection moments than a quick K-based check would suggest, so engineering and connection allowances should not be trimmed on those jobs.
