## Part 1: Seismic Design for Non-West Coast Engineers

### Core Concept
This is the first of a two-hour AISC webinar by Micah Engelhardt (University of Texas at Austin) aimed at engineers with no earthquake-engineering background, and it stays at the fundamentals level on purpose [00:30]. The central teaching is that earthquake load is an inertia force, mass times acceleration, so F equals MA, and lighter buildings draw less force [30:56][31:28]. The second core idea is the design philosophy embodied in the building codes: we do not design ordinary buildings to stay elastic in a strong earthquake because that is uneconomic, so the goal is to prevent collapse and save lives while accepting substantial damage [51:11][52:11]. The mechanism that makes this possible is ductility, inelastic deformation capacity, and the closing point is that in a strong earthquake the maximum load a building sees is set by the building's own lateral strength, not by the strength of the quake [54:15][58:22]. All steel-specific content (AISC 341, welded connections, ductile detailing) is explicitly deferred to Part 2 [02:33][03:03].

### Technical Details
The lecture builds the response-spectrum concept from a single-degree-of-freedom elastic model with mass, a damping ratio (typical two to five percent of critical), and elastic stiffness K, combined into a natural period T [37:44][38:44]. Using the 1994 Northridge Newhall firehouse record (peak ground acceleration 59 percent g), a 0.25-second building amplified to about 2.2 g, a 0.5-second building to about 1.25 g, and a 1.0-second building to about 0.7 g, illustrating resonance [40:49][41:52]. Period correlates strongly with height: roughly 0.25 s for a 2.5-story building, 0.5 s for five stories, 1.0 s for ten stories, with low-rise buildings hit hardest because they resonate with typical ground motion [41:20][47:01]. Soft soil shifts the spectral peak to longer periods and can bring tall buildings into resonance, as in 1985 Mexico City [48:33][34:36]. A worked example takes spectral acceleration of 1.5 g times building weight to show an elastic structure would see an inertia force of 150 percent of its weight, an order of magnitude above the 10 to 20 percent code design force, the gap being covered by ductility [49:38][50:40].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The economic argument running through the talk is that designing to stay elastic in a strong earthquake is not affordable, so codes trade strength for ductility because ductility is generally less costly than strength [57:51]. The cost is accepted damage: choosing ductility is described as making a deal that the building will be damaged in a strong event [57:51]. Lighter structures attract less seismic force, which ties mass reduction to demand reduction [31:59]. None of this is steel-pricing or estimating detail; there are no rates, tonnage rules, or per-SF figures in this hour.
