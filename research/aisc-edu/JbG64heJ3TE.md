### Core Concept
This segment closes lateral-torsional buckling of flexural members and opens composite beam design, the speaker calling composite floor construction the bread and butter of steel building design [12:25]. Two ideas anchor the LTB wrap-up: local buckling is rarely an issue for rolled A992 beams but matters for built-up and a few wide-flange sections, and understanding the Cb moment-gradient factor often yields real savings in non-composite beam design [00:01]. The composite section is framed as practical problem-solving (floor flatness, levelness, camber, weak versus strong studs, ponding) rather than hand calculation, since most engineers run computer programs now [12:25].

### Spec References
Cited by the speaker: AISC 2005 specification (then-current) and its new stud capacities [10:55][13:58]; Chapter B compactness table for B/t ratios tied to rotation/ductility [05:39]; the Cb upper limit of 3.0 introduced in the 2005 spec [10:55]; AISC Design Guide 19 (fire), authored by John Ruddy [25:19]; AWS requirement that the stud ferrule be removed after welding and the bend (hammer) test for stud verification [15:33][16:04]; an AWS rule that unless the stud is over the web, stud diameter shall be no more than 2.5 times the flange thickness [18:34]; Steel Deck Institute minimum deck-attachment requirements [07:15][10:25]; UL fire listings D916, D902, and D925 for composite construction [26:20]. ### Technical Details
A992 rolled beams at Fy 50 ksi are standard; composite decks run 1.5, 2, or 3 inches deep with embossments, made composite by 3/4 inch headed studs, one to three per flute [15:02][15:33]. Normal-weight concrete is 3 ksi minimum to 10 ksi maximum, lightweight 3 to 6 ksi, with strengths above the cap usable for stiffness only, not strength [20:43]. Back-slab cracks over supports are expected because the slab is designed as simply supported but behaves continuously; tests show the cracks do not materially reduce flexural capacity, so the fix is extra mesh or bars cheered up over the support for crack control [21:13][22:15]. Minimum composite action: the speaker keeps it above the common 25% floor because at 25% the studs are strained hard and can break if poorly welded [35:04].

### Fabrication Notes
Stud welding quality is the recurring shop and field concern. The AWS ferrule must be hammered off after welding, and a bend test per AWS confirms a sound weld [16:04]. Stud guns left uncalibrated, especially in early-morning moisture on the deck, produce studs that are not properly welded, a dangerous condition [16:34]. To honor the AWS 2.5-times-flange-thickness rule when studs are not reliably placed over the web, the speaker's practice excludes composite beam webs thinner than about 0.3 inch, ruling out light sections such as W10x12, W10x15, W8x13, and W12x16 [19:06][19:39]. Deck-to-beam attachment uses 5/8 inch puddle welds in higher uplift zones (I60/I90) and 3/4 inch in high-seismic zones [07:45].

### Erection Notes
Deck welding pattern provides compression-flange bracing, with a roughly 12 inch on-center support spacing and wider field spacing, per the project spec and SDI minimums [10:25][29:29]. Decks should be laid as two- or three-span conditions, not simple spans; the detailer can almost always arrange this, and simple-span deck (for example across an elevator lobby) is a safety hazard because it can slip off its support, so high-rise records advise turning the deck to run parallel to the elevator front for a multi-span layout [31:31][32:01]. Decks are generally sized heavy enough to span the beams unshored [31:00].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
A documented girder collapse during construction killed a worker: the engineer assumed the girder was fully braced for the wet-concrete load, but where deck ran parallel the contractor placed only a couple of tack welds on a pan, leaving the long span effectively unbraced between third-point beams, producing a lateral-torsional buckling failure and a lawsuit [08:48][09:21]. Other named failures: shoddy stud welds with the ferrule left on [15:33], uncalibrated stud guns yielding unwelded studs [16:34], concrete strength below D916's 3500 psi normal-weight minimum (some use 2500 or 3000) [27:55], and lightweight concrete density above the UL listing limit [34:01].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Concrete ponding is the headline cost item: a 2 or 3 inch deck spanning 9 to 12 feet deflects 5/8 to 3/4 inch at midspan, and that extra concrete over a million square feet of floor is real money [32:31]. The fix is a specification clause making the contractor responsible for the added concrete from ponding and deck deflection, otherwise it returns as an RFI plus change order [33:01]. Vendor catalogs setting load tables at 110 pcf lightweight concrete understate real 115-plus pcf density, requiring load adjustment [34:01]. Stiffening a section to solve a bracing question is unpopular with fabricators but is the quick path when analysis time is short [04:35]. West Coast seismic jobs trim slab thickness (2 plus 2.5 instead of 2 plus 3.5) to cut mass over many floors and accept spraying the deck [26:52].
