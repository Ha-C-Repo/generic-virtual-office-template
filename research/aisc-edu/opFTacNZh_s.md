## Design Tips for Constructible Steel-Framed Buildings in High-Seismic Regions
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=opFTacNZh_s | Duration: 1:32:31 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
John Hooper (MKA, Seattle) and Scott Eden cover how to design constructible steel lateral systems in high-seismic regions, defined as Seismic Design Category D and higher, the trigger for AISC 341 [02:03]. The central teaching is the fuse-and-capacity-protect model: pick the seismic system, size the fuse exactly to demand and never oversize it, then capacity-protect everything around it (columns, foundations, connections) [20:52][21:23]. Low seismic lets you use R=3 and AISC 360 with almost any configuration; high seismic forces defined, ductile systems with height limits and detailing rules [01:31][09:40]. Eden's half is a parametric study showing that deeper moment-frame columns (versus the traditional W14) cut frame weight 12 to 34 percent because drift, not strength, governs special moment frames [74:54][75:24].

### Spec References
AISC 360 (R=3 low-seismic baseline) [01:31]; AISC 341 seismic provisions, triggered at SDC D, cited for the ASFY full-tensile-capacity SCBF brace-connection rule and for orthogonal bracing penalties at moment-frame connections [02:03][86:44][90:35]; AISC 358 prequalified moment connections, including Chapter 9 (Kaiser bolted bracket, authored by Eden) and the WUF-W (welded unreinforced flange, welded web) used in the study [27:53][68:16][68:46]; ASCE 7 Chapter 12 design base shear, the systems table with 84 line items (20 usable for steel in high seismic), R=8 for SMF, the 160-ft height rule, and the period amplification allowance [09:40][10:41][22:53][60:42]; ASCE 7-10 for rho and omega-zero application to FX and FPX diaphragm forces [42:18]; AISC Seismic Design Manual including Table 8-1 for collector unbraced lengths and the new steel moment-frame base examples Eden contributed [46:23][47:54][58:38]; NIST seismic technical briefs (Sabelli on steel-deck/composite diaphragms, Eden on steel moment frames), free downloads [38:43][58:38].

### Technical Details
Systems covered: special and ordinary concentrically braced frames (OCBF limited to 35 ft, pushing designers to SCBF), buckling-restrained braced frames, eccentrically braced frames, special/intermediate/ordinary and special-truss moment frames, and steel plate shear walls [02:33][10:11][06:07]. Drift governs SMF, with a 2 percent limit for Risk Category II and 1 percent for Risk Category IV (health care), which makes tall moment frames costly [23:55][62:43]. The deep-column study held the strong-column-weak-beam ratio just above 1.0 (baseline pairing 1.06) and the normalized drift near 1.0 for an apples-to-apples comparison, throwing out moment ratios above 1.5 and drift ratios outside 0.9 to 3.0 [70:51][72:24]. Column flexural drift contribution dropped 43 percent (16.6 to 8.9) moving from W14 to deep sections [73:54]. The caveat: deep RBS columns can show local column instability / lateral-torsional buckling at the unbraced bottom flange; specimens were W14x283 and W18x211 columns with W30x132 beams, and continuity plates corrected the instability [80:31][82:35][83:37].

### Fabrication Notes
Gusset plates are the strongest constructability tip (Sabelli's work): avoid one-size-fits-all scheduling that takes the worst alpha and beta across cases and builds a plate that was never actually designed, avoid the forced 30-degree Whitmore fan that drives oversized plates needing edge stiffeners, and instead specify the gusset width directly on the drawings, allowing a 2-to-6-inch work-point offset for efficiency [32:05][34:37][35:39]. A web doubler is worth about 50 lb/ft of column weight per a Herrick fabricator, so upsizing the column to delete a doubler is often cheaper [28:30]. Fabricators want a clean column (no doubler plates, no continuity plates); the deep-column study notes you almost always still need a doubler when the beam-column ratio is held at 1.0 [76:26][76:56]. Match AISC 358 prequalified details exactly, do not redraw code details, and state explicitly any deviation so it can be priced [52:28][52:58].

### Erection Notes
Crane pick capacity runs roughly 25,000 to 30,000 lb, so member size and splice frequency must be planned for large-volume spaces with 30-to-50-ft unbraced columns and 60-to-70-ft braces [12:43]. Splice columns no more than every two levels, three if possible, never floor by floor because that slows erection [29:00]. The two-story X is the preferred braced-frame configuration because it has only three connections per bay versus five for a single-story X [30:33][31:35]. Hooper stresses prefab and pre-erection meetings, getting the engineer in the field to catch problems early, and confirming the WPS up front so everyone is aligned [54:28]. Brace angle should stay 40 to 50 degrees, and brace-frame aspect (height to length) should stay at or below 5, with 10 the practical ceiling [29:32][63:13].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Over-designating demand-critical welds: engineers mark everything demand-critical to avoid thinking, which fabricators called lazy because it spends money and slows schedule where the spec does not require it [54:59][55:29]. The SCBF chevron economy problem raised from the floor: engineers of record size braces for buckling strength but design the gusset connection for the full tensile capacity (ASFY required by AISC 341), forcing an oversized beam and an uneconomical connection; both presenters said they switch to BRBF to make that go away [85:43][86:44][87:14]. Oversized fuses break the capacity-protection economy [20:52]. Redundant general notes and specs that disagree, and referencing only the latest code edition instead of a specific dated standard, cause field confusion [51:27][51:57]. Omissions and tolerance issues hurt more in high seismic because fixes are harder (a bad BRB may need full replacement) [53:28].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Concrete dollar figures stated: special moment frame about $30/SF versus brace frame about $18/SF, dropping to $16/SF with disciplined repetitive grid spacing [13:44][15:16]. Fabricators need accurate tonnage early and must know what is included: connections, and specifically anchor-rod connections to the foundation [49:55][50:26].
