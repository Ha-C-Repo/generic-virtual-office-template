## Composite Steel Joists - Standards and Code of Standard Practice
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tbf93V4k9fY | Duration: 59:06 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This Steel Joist Institute (SJI) webinar introduces the then-new consensus standard for composite steel joists, the CJ series, presented by Robert Hackworth, David Samuelson, and Perry Green [00:00]. Before this spec each joist manufacturer used its own proprietary composite-joist design, so SJI spent roughly five years writing a single ANSI-approved load and resistance factor design (LRFD) standard, SJI CJ 1.0, ANSI approved in 2006 and published in the 2007 catalog [05:45][06:16]. The system is an open-web steel joist with a concrete slab attached to the top chord by headed shear studs welded through the metal deck, the top chord acting as the compression element and developing composite action by horizontal shear transfer [09:21][09:51]. The joists are treated as simply supported members and are not part of the lateral load resisting system unless the engineer coordinates directly with the joist manufacturer [09:21].
### Spec References
The standard cited verbatim is SJI CJ 1.0, the ANSI-approved Composite Steel Joist Standard, published in the SJI 42nd edition catalog with weight tables and bridging tables for both normal-weight and lightweight concrete [05:45][08:21]. An ANSI-approved Code of Standard Practice for composite steel joists is part of the same package, with sections covering general definitions and responsibilities, composite joists and accessories, materials, inspection, estimating (section 5), plans and specifications (section 6), handling and erection, shear-stud installation and inspection, concrete placing, and business relations [36:42][37:14][39:20]. The speakers reference AWS D1.1 for shear-stud installation and inspection, stating the SJI guidelines are very similar if not identical [38:16]. They cite the 13th edition of the AISC steel code (steel manual / composite spec) for comparison of stud strength rules, noting the joist stud-diameter-to-top-chord-thickness ratio limit of 2.7 versus 2.5 in the AISC composite-beam provisions [25:52][28:58]. The SJI Technical Digest TD9 on handling and erection of steel joists and joist girders is recommended [37:14].
### Technical Details
A CJ joist is designated by nominal depth, series, then three factored loads in PLF, for example 30CJ2188/1168/420 where 30 is depth, 2188 is total factored composite design load, 1168 is total factored composite live load, and 420 is total factored composite dead load, because the manufacturer must check three load stages [10:52]. The top chord is designed for compression from non-composite dead plus construction live load, then a total composite check; the bottom chord is designed for tension from composite dead and live loads, with the target failure mode being ductile tension yielding of the bottom chord [19:08][19:40]. Design limit states include bottom-chord tensile yielding and rupture, concrete crushing, and shear-connector strength, with the slab analyzed by the transformed-area method and deflection by the composite moment of inertia [22:43][23:14]. Top-chord area runs about 50 to 70 percent of bottom-chord area, top-chord end panels are conservatively assumed non-composite, and joists assume a minimum yield of 50 ksi for chord and web members [23:48][35:40]. Chord angle sizes range roughly 2.5 to 6 inches, far more selection than the AISC manual, and studs run 3/8 to 3/4 inch over four deck heights from 1 to 3 inches [09:51][29:59].
### Fabrication Notes
The joist manufacturer carries full design responsibility once a CJ designation is specified, the same as for K, LH, or joist-girder series [19:40][20:10]. Composite joists are standardly not painted; when a customer requests paint it must be kept off the top surface of the top chord so it does not impede shear-stud welding [36:12]. Top-chord leg thickness is set as a design minimum so the stud weld does not burn through the angle, with minimum leg thicknesses from a nominal 1/8 inch to 1/4 inch and minimum horizontal flat leg width from 1.5 to 2.5 inches matched to stud size [24:49][25:20]. Joists are cambered by the manufacturer so the floor comes out level under wet concrete weight, typically 1 to 1.5 inches of camber for 30 to 60 foot joists, and because of pin-pin bearing the full camber comes out under load [32:35][33:07][34:38].
### Erection Notes
Composite joist erection and handling is described as no different from non-composite steel joist work, governed by SJI TD9 [37:14]. In the field the deck hides the joists, so crews tap the deck with a hammer at the joist span end, locate the chord, and snap a chalk line over each top-chord angle [39:51][40:57]. The deck must be drawn down tight against the top chord, ideally within 1/16 inch, to make a sound stud weld [40:22]. Studs are laid out ahead of the welder; one observed crew on a large project placed about 250 studs per hour, roughly one every 15 seconds [40:57][41:29]. Two bridging load cases must be checked during erection: the erector standing on the joist before cables are released, and deck bundles landed on the joist, since composite action is not yet available at those stages [58:32].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The named failure observed in testing is the shear stud placed in the weak position tearing out through the metal deck because little concrete sits in front of it; the standard conservatively assumes all studs are in the weak position rather than mandating placement [25:52][26:23][26:54]. A burn-through of a too-thin top-chord angle during stud welding is the risk the minimum-thickness rule prevents [24:49]. Inspection per the Code of Standard Practice requires verifying concrete thickness, testing concrete compressive strength, and inspecting welded studs for quantity and weld quality, with a start-of-shift bend test of a welded stud to a minimum 15 degrees from horizontal and a suggested one bend test per 100 studs [36:42][38:16][38:48]. A non-uniform or locally thin slab is flagged as a defect because it degrades deflection prediction, the UL fire rating, and stud cover [33:37][34:08].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The Code of Standard Practice section 5 covers estimating and lists the items that must appear on the plans for the manufacturer to prepare an accurate bid; a design parameters checklist in the catalog is faxed to the manufacturer with depth, span, spacing, slab data, and loads [37:14][43:34][44:05]. The headline economics: a worked 30-inch, 50-foot joist at about 37 to 39 PLF compared against a non-composite joist at about 56 PLF, roughly a 35 percent weight reduction, and field reports of 20 to 30 percent savings [46:40][47:11][50:20]. Spacing joists wider, on the order of 6 to 10 feet rather than 2 feet, yields larger top chords that speed stud installation and improve economy [41:29][52:26]. Secondary savings come from reduced floor-to-floor height (helpful under municipal height limits), lighter building weight cutting foundation cost, column-free spans, and faster erection [50:20][54:58][55:28].
