## Plate Girder Design and Stability

### Core Concept
This is session 2 of AISC's "Introduction to Steel Bridge Design" series, presented by Todd Helwig (UT Austin), covering plate-girder design and stability for steel highway bridges [00:01]. The through-line is that built-up plate girders are slender-plate assemblies whose strength is governed by stability limit states in two distinct stages: the non-composite construction stage where the bare steel girder carries the full deck-placement load, and the finished composite stage where the steel girder acts with the concrete deck [02:34, 03:05]. The talk works through local plate buckling (flange and web), lateral-torsional buckling, composite plastic-moment design, preliminary girder sizing, and the system (global) buckling mode of narrow twin- and three-girder units [01:33, 75:23]. Most stability checks control during construction, not in the finished bridge, because the cured composite deck provides continuous lateral and torsional restraint [85:47].

### Technical Details
All elastic plate-buckling stresses share the form K*pi^2*E / (12*(1-mu^2)*(b/t)^2), with K the plate-buckling coefficient set by stress type (axial, bending, shear) and edge support; mu = 0.3 gives the 0.9 coefficient seen in the web bend-buckling equation [10:11, 19:25]. Flange slenderness is bf/(2*tf): lambda-p ~ 9.2 and lambda-r ~ 16.1 for Grade 50, with the elastic cutoff at 0.7*Fy assuming 30% residual stress [14:17, 15:18]. Web slenderness is 2*Dc/tw with lambda-r ~ 137 and a compact limit ~ 90 for Grade 50; designers target webs near lambda-r, roughly 120 to 137 [16:19, 17:21]. Torsional resistance splits into Saint-Venant (GJ) and warping (Cw = Iy*d^2/4 for doubly symmetric) terms [22:32, 26:07]. Composite plastic-moment design uses a Whitney stress block at 0.85*f'c, locating the plastic neutral axis by comparing C* (max slab compressive resultant = 0.85*f'c*beff*ts), Py (steel area times Fy), and Py-web; the PNA lands in the deck, the top flange, or the web [38:54, 41:58]. Worked examples gave Mp = 5865 kip-ft (PNA in deck) and 5645 kip-ft (PNA in flange, using a modified stress diagram) [46:04, 51:13]. Shear-stud strength is 0.5*Asc*sqrt(f'c*Ec) capped at Asc*Fu; a 3/4-inch stud in 4 ksi concrete carries ~26 kip, and the mid-span example needed 184 total studs at uniform spacing [52:17, 55:23].

### Fabrication Notes
Bridge girders are built-up welded plate sections, not rolled shapes, so the fabricator sets plate thicknesses to standard increments (1 inch, 1-1/16, 1-1/4) and the designer iterates flange and web plates to hit slenderness targets [61:33]. Practical flange proportioning keeps bf/2tf near the compact limit (about 9 for Grade 50, AASHTO allows up to 12) and flange-width-to-depth around 1/4 rather than the slender 1/6 limit; singly symmetric girders typically run the bottom flange area about 1.5 times the top [61:02, 122]. Stay-in-place metal deck forms supported on cold-formed angles are common and stay on the bridge; the angles let the contractor adjust form elevation for flange-thickness variation and differential camber, and Texas permits welding those supports to the girder where some states do not [57:25, 58:25]. Longitudinal web stiffeners are added when web slenderness exceeds 150, with an ideal location at 2*Dc/5 [20:27].

### Erection Notes
Stability during construction is the controlling concern, since the bare non-composite girder carries the full self-weight, wet concrete, formwork, and construction live load before the deck cures [66:39, 67:41]. Lateral-torsional buckling during deck placement is resisted by cross frames or diaphragms spaced along the length; the worked example solved a max unbraced length of ~417 in (~35 ft) and adopted five segments giving 30-ft bracing for a 150-ft span [72:16, 73:16]. AASHTO applies a 1.4 construction load factor to cover contractor-method uncertainty [67:10]. The system buckling mode is the key erection hazard for narrow twin- and three-girder units: it is insensitive to cross-frame spacing because the girders buckle as one half sine-wave unit, and the speaker cites real construction failures (a tub-girder pedestrian bridge in Marcy NY and a twin-girder bridge widening in Houston where a flange moved ~10 in during casting) [75:23, 76:23, 76:53]. AASHTO caps applied moment at 50% (raised to 70% in the newest version) of the system-buckling critical load, else temporary bracing or larger girders are required [77:58].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The named failures are construction-stage stability losses rather than weld or bolt rejections. Two field collapses anchor the system-mode discussion: the Marcy NY tub-girder pedestrian bridge that failed during slab casting because it lacked a top lateral truss and behaved like two close eye-girders, and a Houston twin-girder widening whose flange swept ~10 in laterally during deck placement [75:53, 76:23]. The speaker flags that omitting a top lateral truss on a tub girder, or under-bracing narrow girder units, invites a sudden system buckling failure, and that bracing failures generally are the one buckling case that is sudden and catastrophic rather than gradual [06:38, 76:23]. On cover-plating an existing composite girder he warns the real risk is fatigue at weld terminations, evaluated by AASHTO fatigue categories, not loss of yield capacity [87:18].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Bridge girders use custom built-up plates, so there is no rolled-shape table to price against; the estimator must derive depth from span-to-depth ratios (simple spans 25 to 30, continuous ~35), then size web and flange plates from stability limits before any tonnage is known [56:55, 116]. Cross-frame and diaphragm quantity is driven by the construction-stage LTB check, and the worked example showed that trying to drop one cross frame (going from 30-ft to 37.5-ft spacing) failed the appendix LTB check at 30.5 ksi versus a 32 ksi demand, so the cheaper bracing layout was not buildable [74:51, 75:23]. Shear-stud counts are real material and labor: 184 studs on one 150-ft girder in the example, set at uniform spacing [55:23]. Narrow twin- or three-girder units may need temporary construction bracing, an added cost line when the system-buckling demand exceeds the AASHTO fraction [77:58].
