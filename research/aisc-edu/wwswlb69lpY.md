## Constructability of Structural Steel Buildings: Part 3
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wwswlb69lpY | Duration: 1:10:02 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This Ruby and Associates webinar (Jay Ruby and John Matusca) teaches constructability for fabricators, walking through Chapter 5 of AISC Design Guide 23, which addresses fabrication issues [06:43]. The central thesis is that early fabricator and detailer collaboration from project inception, not value engineering after bid and not field means-and-methods, is where a fabricator influences total project cost and schedule [07:13][15:34]. They cite the 1993 Construction Industry Institute definition: constructability is the optimum use of construction knowledge and experience in planning, design, procurement, and field operations to achieve overall project objectives [07:13]. The recurring point is that price per ton is the wrong lens; the real driver is man-hours, piece count, how many times a piece is handled, and number of shipments [03:04][05:07].
### Spec References
The talk is process-oriented and cites few exact sections. Captured references: AISC Design Guide 23, Chapter 5, with named subsections 5.1 complete and coordinated documents [09:18], 5.2 material procurement and shop drawing fabrication [20:39], 5.3 standards [52:00], 5.4 connections [28:53], 5.5 surface preparation [36:12], 5.6 galvanizing and coatings [36:44], and 5.7 shipping and delivery / piece sizes [38:16]. SSPC surface prep was cited as "SSP5 white blast cleaning," a misstatement of SSPC-SP 5 white metal blast [36:44]; treat the speaker's wording as approximate.
### Technical Details
On seismic, the geotechnical engineer sets the seismic site class, then the engineer has latitude in choosing R; a higher R lowers tonnage but drives expensive connections, while a lower R raises tonnage but reduces connection cost, a trade-off where fabricator input is valuable [26:49][27:20]. Member grouping was illustrated with a 20 ft bay of 200 beams where the analysis program optimized to four different sizes (W10x15, W12x14, W12x16, W14x22 as spoken); grouping cuts shop and field tracking overhead even at a small tonnage penalty [17:37]. A truss job with 120 pieces had 56 different truss types; cutting that count saves real money [18:38]. Camber was flagged: software cambers many beams an inch each, when a deeper, stiffer member can avoid camber altogether [19:08].
### Fabrication Notes
Bolted joint considerations include snug-tight versus fully tensioned versus slip-critical, and whether to use AISC standard connections [31:59][32:30]. Welded joint considerations include joint type, how many times a piece must be turned in the shop, welding process selection, and the weld-all-around symbol that is common on drawings but seldom necessary [35:40]. Surface prep and coatings were called frequent overspecification: interior steel not exposed to moisture may need little prep and no paint [36:44][37:14]. Case-study fabrication wins: dropping floor beams a couple inches so deck bears on top rather than coping, which on a roughly 450-beam job saved about 900 copes [46:00]; shop-welding short cantilevers instead of field-welding [45:29]; and a crane-girder design iterated from 60 tons down to 45 tons via a fishbelly so a shop with a 40-ton lift could handle it with an engineered lift [53:16].
### Erection Notes
Erector input is treated as part of the same early team. A laced crane column at about 13.5 ft column-line spacing grew to 14.5 ft over the flanges; the fabricator and erector jointly weighed shipping it as field sticks versus tightening the column gauge and sending an escorted oversize load [38:16]. On the charter steel melt shop, the fabricator laid wide-flange truss top and bottom chords on their sides with same-depth wide-flange web members so a gusset welds on flat with no spacers or shims, which also removed lateral bracing during erection of those trusses [52:14]. The melt shop, roughly 100,000 SF in three structures with the melt bay 110 ft tall housing two 200-ton overhead cranes, went from start of design in January 2005 to substantially erected in November 2005 [50:40][51:10].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The named failure mode is incomplete and uncoordinated bid documents: missing dimensions, missing members, and connection information reduced to general notes, which produce inaccurate bids, RFI floods, extras, schedule delays, lost profit, and an over-budget unhappy owner [10:52][11:24]. A concrete example: a partial fifth-floor plan of a 30-story building showed only a single sketch for a connection carrying significant horizontal force into a concrete shear wall; the real solution was an embedded shear plate detailed in Tekla that bidders did not price [29:56][31:28]. Inspection-cost traps were named: slip-critical connections require continuous special inspection while snug-tight require only periodic, and certain weld types require continuous versus periodic inspection, a large cost the owner bears [32:30][35:40]. Tolerance mismatch was flagged: bidding to AISC tolerances when AIST TR-13 or Factory Mutual applies causes disputes [58:00].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The whole talk reframes bidding around man-hours, piece handling, and shipments rather than dollars-per-ton [03:04][05:07]. An accurate model handed to the fabricator early lets him push a button for material list, piece count, and tonnage, freeing time to plan the work rather than rebuild the takeoff [22:42][44:58]. The headline number: a 144,000 SF four-story hospital under the constructability method versus a similar traditional design-bid-build hospital saved 17 weeks of total project duration (design 10 weeks, zero bid/estimate time, four weeks of award time gained, ABM prep under a day), with a stated steel cost saving that excludes the cost of money over those weeks [48:35][49:08][50:10]. Tightening method, surface prep level, R-value choice, and member grouping each move both fabrication cost and downstream inspection cost.
