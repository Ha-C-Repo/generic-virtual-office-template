## Night School 6, Session 2:  Uniform Force Method
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6OmazwL3dAE | Duration: 1:25:58 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This session, presented by William A. Thornton (Cives Corporation, TR Higgins 1995 and AISC Lifetime Achievement 2003), develops the Uniform Force Method (UFM), the geometric method for distributing forces in a vertical (diagonal) bracing connection gusset [03:01]. The method evolved from AISC-sponsored finite-element research by Ralph Richard at the University of Arizona in the mid 1980s [03:31]. Its core idea is to force the brace force, the gusset-to-beam force, and the gusset-to-column force to intersect at a single concurrent point so the gusset is in equilibrium with no couples [03:31, 04:01]. The controlling constraint is alpha minus beta times tangent theta equals e-sub-B times tangent theta minus e-sub-C, where alpha and beta locate the gusset connection centroids, e-sub-B is the beam half depth, e-sub-C is the column half depth, and theta is measured from vertical [05:38, 11:53]. When the actual connection centroids alpha-bar and beta-bar do not satisfy that constraint, real couples (moments) appear on the gusset edges that must be designed for [06:09].
### Spec References
Thornton cites the AISC Manual Part 13 (the bracing-connection / UFM section) repeatedly as the home of all the UFM formulas, special cases, and the optimization routine, stating it is identical in the 13th and 14th editions and traces back to the 2nd and 3rd edition LRFD manuals [25:17, 30:29, 39:48, 77:06]. The worked example is run to the 14th edition Manual as an ASD problem [15:29]. He references his own 1991 AISC National Steel Construction Conference paper and his 1995 TR Higgins paper [54:47], a forthcoming AISC Design Guide for the non-orthogonal case (number not stated) [58:20], and a book edited by Akbar Tamboli (Claude/Akbar Tamboli, 2nd edition, ~1999) for the non-orthogonal UFM derivation [57:50, 67:07]. No specific AISC 360 section numbers, AISC 341 seismic sections, AWS D1.1 clauses, or RCSC sections are spoken; bolt callouts are given as ASTM A325 (3/4 inch and the example tab at 7/8 inch) [15:29, 47:32].
### Technical Details
The constraint is derived live by writing the three line equations (brace line, gusset-to-beam force line through control point A, gusset-to-column force line through control point B), solving pairs for the common point Q, and equating [09:46, 11:22]. Edge forces are written as P over R times a length term: H-sub-B, H-sub-C, V-sub-B, V-sub-C, where R is the geometric distance from the work point to where a vertical from the gusset-to-beam centroid meets a horizontal from the gusset-to-column centroid [13:26, 14:26]. A critical computational rule: R must be calculated with the constraint-satisfying alpha and beta, never with alpha-bar [19:39, 22:12]. Four special cases are covered: case 1 (non-concentric work point, e.g. at the gusset corner, which always produces couples in the beam and column distributed by an alpha factor of I/L) [32:33, 35:07]; case 2 (transfer some brace vertical V-sub-B to the column via Delta-V to relieve beam shear) [36:08]; case 3 (very shallow or very steep brace connected to only one member, leaving an unavoidable M-sub-EC couple) [41:53]; and the newly named special case 4 (a column-web connection treated like a flange connection by setting e-sub-C equal to the distance out to the first column of bolts rather than zero) [43:29].
### Fabrication Notes
The economic core of the session is special case 4 versus the conventional kiss-method double-clip approach, compared in a force-and-quantity table [48:33]. Using extended and conventional shear tabs in place of double clips eliminated drilling holes in jumbo columns with four-inch flanges [45:32]. The optimized design used 24 standard holes versus 14, one-inch plate versus half-inch, 7/8 inch bolts versus 3/4 inch, giving only 21 percent of the hole-drilling volume, an 80 percent savings in drilling time [48:33, 50:38]. Weld comparison: a 5/8 inch fillet is a six-pass weld, so 30 inches of 5/8 fillet equals 180 inches of single-pass weld, while the optimized 5/16 fillet at 52.3 inches stays single pass, yielding roughly 50 percent or more savings in welding consumables and the optimized tab weighing about 71 percent of the standard [49:34, 50:38]. Thornton notes there is no commercial software for these connections; even his firm calculates each from scratch [65:36].
### Erection Notes
Erection is treated mainly through the connection-type tradeoff and the truss examples rather than sequencing or rigging. He cites a 60 percent (possibly 70 percent) labor savings from the tab approach on the case-4 job [50:38]. The headline jobsite economics: the engineer of record reported the special-case-4 job finished about two million dollars below the estimated cost, in part by replacing traditional double clips at jumbo columns to cut both fabrication and erection cost [45:01, 45:32]. A Boston building example shows a 38-story tower where 30-plus stories sit on main trusses, with a jack-truss gusset slotted through a near-side main-truss gusset to land roughly a 2,000 kip vertical component dead center on the main-truss vertical [59:22, 59:54]. No crane, bracing, or shoring procedures are detailed.
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The repeated error called out is substituting alpha-bar for alpha when computing R, which violates equilibrium and is caught by the check that V-sub-B plus V-sub-C must equal the brace vertical component (P cosine theta) and H-sub-B plus H-sub-C must equal the horizontal component (P sine theta) [22:12, 22:43]. On X-bolts (oversize / bolts in shear plane usage), Thornton notes extra inspection can be avoided if the ply next to the nut is thick enough to keep threads out of the shear plane; for a one-inch bolt with flush nut and no washer the ply needs to be about 13/16 inch, dropping to about 5/8 with a 5/32 washer or 3/8 with a quarter-inch stick-through, otherwise N-type (threads-included) bolts are used on thin webs or plates [44:31, 46:32, 47:02]. The limit-state list validating the UFM against six full-scale tests (University of Alberta tension tests and three NIST tests by John Gross and Geraldine Cheok, ~1989) included bolt fracture, Whitmore buckling, Whitmore yield, block shear with the tearout-versus-bearing distinction, with test-to-predicted ratios near 1.01 to 1.11 [54:47, 55:48, 56:19].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Thornton states connections can account for up to 50 percent of the cost of erected structural steel, though a simple brace connection like the first example is closer to 15 to 20 percent [64:34]. The quantified savings on the case-4 job (about two million dollars under estimate, 80 percent less drilling, ~50 percent less weld consumable, ~60 to 70 percent less labor, ~29 percent lighter tab steel) show that connection-method choice, not just member tonnage, drives erected cost [45:32, 48:33, 50:38]. He warns that specifying beam shear as a percentage of UDL (uniform design load), such as half or three-quarters UDL, inflates the design shear far above real floor loads (he estimates implied loads of 1,000 to 2,000 psf versus a real ~200 psf), forcing special case 2 and expensive doubler plates that real gravity loads would avoid [36:39, 40:51, 41:53]. Because no software exists, every non-standard bracing connection carries real engineering labor that must be priced.
