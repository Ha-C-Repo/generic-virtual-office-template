### Core Concept
This segment covers the mechanics of bolted shear connections: how a bolt resists shear, what pretension actually does, and why pretension does not change a bolt's ultimate shear strength. The speaker frames everything as resistance R subn (knocked down for uncertainty) having to be greater than or equal to required strength, the common term that both LRFD and ASD calculate before splitting into their own factors [00:33], [01:03]. The unifying idea is that whether you work in LRFD or ASD you calculate the same bolt shear resistance, then apply either the resistance factor or the safety factor Omega [01:35].

### Technical Details
Bolt ultimate shear strength is about 62% of bolt tensile strength per von Mises, then the design rule discounts that by an additional 20%, with further reductions for threads in the shear plane and for long joints (three to four feet) [14:52], [15:24]. Bolts can be in single or double shear, with threads included or excluded from the shear plane [07:44]. Bearing always governs in the connected plate, not the bolt, because bearing capacity of the bolt is always greater than bearing of the plate, so only the plate is checked [07:44], [08:14]. Force is not shared equally: with three or more bolts in a line the end bolts carry more than the average, so total load divided by bolt count is invalid for long bolted splices in tension or compression members, though a beam-web-to-column-flange transfer can be assumed equally loaded [22:07], [24:10]. The full load-deformation curve shows an elastic region, slip when frictional resistance is overcome (about 3 mm), then yield, strain hardening, and fracture with large ductility (roughly 18 to 20 mm) which is desirable for warning and load redistribution [04:41], [06:43], [07:13].
### Fabrication Notes
Holes are made 1/16 inch larger than the bolt diameter for standard holes; oversized and slotted holes are options discussed later [08:46]. Total possible slip is two whole clearances (2 x 1/16 = 1/8 inch), but field and lab measurement shows real slip averages about half a whole clearance, roughly 1/16 inch, about the thickness of a dime [09:16], [10:18], [10:48]. This is geometry the shop controls through hole punching and reaming. No explicit fit-up tolerance table is cited.
### Erection Notes
The spud wrench is described as the erector's tool, an open socket with a pointed end used for spearing and lining up holes to bring members into installation condition [02:36], [03:07]. Bolts in the field are either pretensioned (snug-tight plus a defined turn) or not pretensioned, and the choice drives the inspection method that follows [03:39], [04:10]. Turn-of-nut values shown are snug, half turn, and one and a half turns, with the note that one and a half turns is not a permissible value but was tested to make a point [25:11], [26:17].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The recurring error the speaker warns against is over-specifying slip-critical connections when they are not needed: do not specify slip-critical just because it seems more conservative, because it adds cost and most buildings do not need it [10:48], [11:18]. Slip-critical is required where slip matters, the clearest case being fatigue loading such as a bridge crane structure where rubbing faying surfaces are not permissible [09:16], [09:47]. A second misconception corrected is assuming a shear yield point exists; the real test curves for A325 and A490 show no shear yield, so a rule must not be based on one [16:25], [16:55]. He also corrects the intuition that pretension raises shear strength.
### Bid / Cost Implications
The explicit cost message is competitive: steel structures should not be made more expensive than they need to be because the industry competes with other materials, so unnecessary slip-critical connections waste money [10:48]. Choosing bearing-type connections where slip is acceptable reduces installation and inspection burden versus slip-critical. The long-joint and threads-in-shear-plane discounts mean connection capacity, and therefore bolt counts and labor, depend on detailing choices an estimator should not assume away.
