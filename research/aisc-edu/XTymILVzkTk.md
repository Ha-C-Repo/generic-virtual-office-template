## Development and Large Scale Testing of Long Span Floor Systems
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XTymILVzkTk | Duration: 59:44 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This is a two-part AISC webinar on long span composite floor systems for multi-story residential steel framing. Devin Huber presents Purdue research (Dr. Varma, AISC Faculty Fellowship) on deep composite deck systems that span up to 30 ft and eliminate intermediate filler beams to cut floor-to-floor height [01:36][04:43]. The driver is vertical real estate: a conventional 1.5 to 3 in deck plus filler beams and girders runs 17 to 21 in total system depth, while the deep deck systems get structural depth down to 10 to 11 in [02:37][32:25]. Erica Winters Downey then surveys deep composite floor deck products available in the market and their span, fire, acoustic, and vibration ratings [33:27].

### Technical Details
Systems tested: a composite cellular deep deck, 7.5 in deep with a cellular bottom panel, 10 in overall depth on lightweight concrete, no shoring required, spanning 20 to 30 ft [09:52][10:23]. A non-cellular deep deck variant ("candidate 1A") uses a single line of shoring at midspan to push past 30 ft, since deflection scales with span to the fourth power [11:54][12:26]. A third "load balancing" system jacks the bare beam up about 1 in before the pour to counterbalance dead load, letting a W8x35 replace a W16x31 or W18 at a 30 ft span, 8 ft filler spacing [13:58][14:28][14:48]. Five specimens were tested at 30 ft spans, loaded at third points to approximate uniform moment [17:35][18:36]. Measured first natural frequency was 5.8 to 6 Hz across specimens [22:43]. A 27x86 girder dropped a 6.4 Hz floor to about 5.6 Hz, so the girder matters [24:13]. Self weights run 50 to 60 psf [32:25].

### Fabrication Notes
End attachment is either shear studs or a 5/8 in puddle weld; shear studs delay deck-to-concrete slip and preserve composite action [10:53][11:23]. AISC-spec 9 in shear studs are required for these deep profiles and are readily available [11:23]. The thicker cellular deck cannot be reliably stud-welded through: arc blow and blasted galvanizing drop weld quality, so holes were pre-cut with a plasma cutter and studs shot through to the beam with the deck held by bearing [11:23][49:31]. Rule of thumb stated: you can shoot through up to 16 gauge without a problem, anything thicker needs a pre-cut hole [50:02]. One marketed product uses a crimping tool that forms a shear key at the side lap to lock slip without studs [50:02].

### Erection Notes
The presenters stress erection is not a big departure from conventional practice: end conditions use simple closures, seated connections at columns, and standard closure panels [30:55][31:25]. Eliminating infill beams reduces the number of crane picks an erector bids, since erectors bid by picks [33:58]. Shoring, where used, is one line at midspan or at quarter points, not closely spaced lines like cast-in-place, and was left in roughly 7 days before stripping [05:14][19:08]. Panels are light enough for two men (each about 24 in wide), and there is interest in assembling several panels on the ground and craning them up as a unit to save crane time [44:49][47:27].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The named failure modes are structural test behaviors, not field inspection rejections. Specimens without shear studs showed a brittle shear-bond failure: once the deck started to slip it could not be arrested, the concrete cracked under the load point and slid back toward the support, and composite action was lost completely [26:19][27:19][27:50]. Stud-welding through thick deck risks arc blow and a poor weld through the galvanizing [49:31]. Bare unprotected deck warped severely in the heat transfer test (bottom plate reached about 650 C), while a protected specimen stayed near 200 C [28:20][57:09].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Cost was the hardest figure to pin down and was debated openly. The Corus slim deck material was quoted around $5 per square foot delivered [44:17][52:35]. An audience member estimated the finished CSI system at $10 to $12 per square foot all in (deck plus concrete) versus precast plank installed near $8 per square foot, so the system is not automatically cheaper on a slab-only basis [52:35]. The real offset comes from removing filler beams, their connections, and their fireproofing, plus saving 6 to 8 in per floor that compounds to roughly 10 ft of cladding and piping over a 20 story building [33:58][34:30][48:29]. Thinning the deck to 18 gauge where shoring or span allows saves real steel cost versus 14 gauge or cellular [48:29]. Lead times run 4 to 6 weeks on long span deck, so it is an early design decision and can beat precast plank where plank availability is poor [40:10][54:36].

### Your Company Application
This is residential deep-deck floor technology outside Your Company's typical conventional structural steel and is informational, not a doctrine change. For Ivan's takeoff and estimating, the durable lessons are that deck gauge and shoring choice swing cost materially (18 vs 14 gauge), that erector pricing is per-pick so removing infill beams is a real line-item saving, and that eliminating filler beams also drops fireproofing scope. For Mario and AWS D1.1 welding, the arc-blow caution on shooting studs through thick galvanized deck is directly transferable to our field stud and puddle-weld work; pre-cut holes are the fix above 16 gauge. For our in-house PE stamp, note the floor-vibration check should account for girder flexibility, not just the deck, and the AISC design-guide frequency method is not calibrated for deep decks (about 3.5 percent damping was needed to stay conservative).
