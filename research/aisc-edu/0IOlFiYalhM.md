## Bolting & Welding Primer - Part 2

### Core Concept
This webinar segment covers the remaining welding fundamentals: gas tungsten arc welding (GTAW/TIG), hydrogen cracking control, the AWS D1.1 framework for electrode and process selection, the taxonomy of weld joints and weld types, groove weld geometry and throat, and the role of weld backing including the North Ridge lesson on steel backing removal. The unifying theme is that AWS D1.1 already governs most of these choices, so the engineer should specify D1.1 and expect compliance rather than adding redundant requirements [08:41]. A second unifying principle is that there are no secondary members in welded construction: a crack can transfer through a weld from one member into another, so anything welded on can become part of the load path [27:55].
### Spec References
The speaker cites AWS D1.1 throughout, naming Table 3.1 of D1.1 for steel groupings and allowable electrodes [07:39], group one (lower strength, low alloy steels, low hydrogen not required) and group two (higher strength, low hydrogen electrodes required) [08:10]. He references the AWS pre-qualified steel list in D1.1 and the AISC listings [18:35], and the AISC seismic pre-qualified connection details from the "cprp" document (spoken; the SAC/AISC prequalified-connections work) requiring steel backing removal [30:01]. He names the American Welding Society joint and weld-type definitions [12:50]. Steel grades cited verbatim: A992, A572, A36 (under ~70 ksi, low concern), and A514/A517 quenched and tempered (T1) and SAE 1045 as elevated-concern materials [19:09][20:09].
### Technical Details
Five joint types are defined: butt, T, corner, lap, and edge; a joint only describes relative positioning of members and is not yet welded [11:47]. Weld types are groove (subdivided into complete joint penetration CJP and partial joint penetration PJP), fillet, plug, and slot [13:22]. A CJP groove weld develops the full capacity of the base material in static loading and its throat equals the thickness of the thinner joined material [20:39]. Groove geometry terms: included angle (sum of bevels, e.g. 15 plus 15 equals 30 degrees), root opening R, and backing [16:29]. Hydrogen cracking requires three coincident factors: sufficient diffusible hydrogen, sufficient applied or residual stress (residual stress assumed at the yield point), and sufficiently sensitive material; hydrogen comes mainly from moisture and oil [03:36][05:38].
### Fabrication Notes
Process-to-location mapping: submerged arc for long straight automatable welds such as plate and transfer girders, gas-shielded flux core as the shop workhorse, self-shielded flux core for field welding, and shielded metal arc (slow, expensive) for small repairs and tack welds [10:44]. Low hydrogen electrodes ship in hermetically sealed containers and must be held in a heated holding oven at a prescribed temperature with limits on exposure time out of the oven; wire left to rust and reused submerged-arc flux contaminated by condensation reintroduce hydrogen [04:38][05:08]. Welders must be qualified by process, so a shop qualified only in flux core cannot switch to shielded metal arc without requalification and possibly new equipment [09:43]. Groove preparation (bevel, V, J, U, flare bevel, flare V) is generally left to the fabricator since CJP develops the same strength regardless of detail [22:11][23:12]. CJP groove welds in tension require matching strength weld metal [22:11].
### Erection Notes
Field beam-to-column connections rely on steel backing to absorb fit-up tolerance: the beam is cut to length while columns are held plumb, and steel backing lets the erector split the difference on the root opening and still make a quality weld [25:20]. Self-shielded flux core is the expected field welding process [10:44]. In high seismic zones and some reduced seismic zones, steel backing at the bottom beam flange to column connection is required to be removed, typically by air arc gouging or grinding followed by a reinforcing fillet weld; this is a complicated, costly field operation [29:59][30:30].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Hydrogen cracking in the heat affected zone is the primary metallurgical failure discussed, with cracking called the worst outcome in welding [03:36]. Obsolete and incorrect terminology is flagged: CP and FPS for CJP, PP for PJP, and calling an unwelded butt joint a "butt weld" [13:22][12:50]. Specifying CJP where it is not needed is named as an abuse that adds cost [21:41]. The North Ridge (Jan 17 1994) beam-to-column fractures initiated at the root where steel backing was left in place, exploiting the naturally occurring lack-of-fusion plane between backing and column under seismic tension [28:25][29:28]. Copper backing melted into the weld can segregate and crack; ceramic backing being non-conductive causes arc loss if the welder strikes on it [32:02][33:03].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Shielded metal arc is explicitly called expensive due to low deposition efficiency, so process choice drives shop labor cost [10:44]. Requalifying welders for a mandated process and buying new equipment is a real cost the engineer can trigger by over-specifying processes, which the speaker discourages [09:43]. Over-specifying CJP where not required adds avoidable cost [21:41]. Steel backing removal in seismic work is described as very complicated and costly, a field-labor line item that copper or ceramic backing can sometimes avoid, though copper is very expensive and not reusable once used [30:30][32:32].
