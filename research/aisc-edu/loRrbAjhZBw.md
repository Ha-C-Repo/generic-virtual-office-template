## Steel Material Properties

### Core Concept
This is NSBA Night School Session 3, "Steel Materials," presented by Dr. Carl Frank, chief engineer at Hirschfeld Industries [00:00]. The talk explains where structural steel strength comes from and ties it back to fabrication and welding. Strength is a function of chemistry and heat treatment: steel is hot rolled in the austenite (gamma) phase, then cooled through a transformation to a ferrite-pearlite microstructure, and strength can be raised by alloying, by quench-and-temper, or by inline thermo-mechanical controlled processing (TMCP) [11:50][12:52][13:24]. The recurring theme is martensite control: cooling too fast forms hard, brittle martensite, and the same risk appears in the weld heat-affected zone, which is why chemistry is controlled for weldability and why preheat slows the cooling rate [14:24][20:36][24:45]. The scope is bridge steel, where toughness, fracture-critical sampling, and bolt rotational-capacity requirements go beyond ordinary building practice [01:31][28:24].
### Spec References
Cited verbatim by the speaker: ASTM A709 as the consolidated bridge-steel specification with seven grades (36, 50, 50W, HPS 50W, HPS 70W, HPS 100W) [26:49][27:19]; legacy specs A7, A36, A514 (T1, 100 ksi), A572 Grade 50, A588/A242 weathering, and rolled-section spec A992 [25:49][27:54][58:00 ref]; the new bolt spec ASTM F3125 that consolidates A325, A490, and twist-off grades, ordered as "F3125 Grade A325" [55:22][80:00]; ASTM A1010 (stainless-type corrosion-resistant bridge steel) [52:13][72:32]; HSS specs ASTM A500 (noted not for dynamically loaded structures) and the newer ASTM A1085 with Charpy V-notch, carbon-equivalent, corner-radius, and tighter wall-thickness control [53:49][73:07]; anchor-rod spec ASTM A1554 (now F1554 in practice) grades 36, 55, 105 [65:15]; and ASTM A354 Grade BD high-strength rod, flagged as stress-corrosion sensitive [66:20]. Temperature zones 1, 2, 3 set Charpy test temperatures [50:10].
### Technical Details
Steel transforms from face-centered-cubic austenite to body-centered-cubic ferrite plus carbide (cementite), forming the lamellar pearlite microstructure; structural steel sits near 0.2 percent carbon [09:16][11:19]. Fracture toughness is the controlling property for bridge tension members: a Charpy V-notch (CVN) specimen, 10 mm square with a center notch, measures fracture energy versus temperature, and toughness in ksi-root-inch is estimated as the square root of CVN times 12.5 [39:13][40:16]. A temperature shift converts impact-rate Charpy results to bridge loading rate (about 75 percent of the shift), so the Charpy is tested above the lowest anticipated service temperature [37:40][39:44]. A worked example: 50 ksi steel, lowest service temperature 0 F, gives roughly a 105 F shift, so the steel is tested near room temperature (70 F) and must average about 25 ft-lb across three specimens to tolerate a 1-inch through-crack [40:47][41:17]. Bolt installation tension is 70 percent of tensile strength; a 7/8-inch A325 has a 39 kip minimum pretension [58:36].
### Fabrication Notes
A709 carries fabrication-side requirements beyond building steel: Charpy V-notch testing for tension members (not compression), extra material requirements and weld-repair limits for fracture-critical members, and heavier sampling [28:24][35:36]. Sampling frequency steps up by product: heat-based Charpy sampling for Grade 50 and 36; plate-based testing for heat-treated grades (HPS 70W, HPS 100W) because each plate has unique heat-treatment character; and P-frequency testing at both plate ends for all fracture-critical grades unless normalized [51:11][52:13]. Quench-and-temper is done by reheating into the austenite range, water-quenching to martensite, then tempering at 800 to 1200 F, with higher tempering temperature giving softer, tougher product [15:28][16:30]. HPS 70W is the workhorse, available to 4 inches, generally quenched-and-tempered by one producer over 1 inch, with TMCP grades reaching longer plate lengths (heat-treat furnaces limit plate length to roughly 50 feet) [31:28][33:32].
### Erection Notes
The strongest field-procedure point is snugging: the purpose is to draw the plies into firm contact so that tightening adjacent bolts does not loosen already-tightened ones, and this crosstalk control is the single most important step regardless of the final tightening method [62:42][63:13]. For turn-of-nut, match-mark the nut and bolt after snugging so final tightening can be verified visually; for direct-tension indicators the snugging is done the same way [63:13][63:44]. Checking installed bolt tension with a torque wrench is called unreliable and unnecessary [63:44]. The San Francisco Bay Bridge case shows the erection-sequence trap: A354 BD rods were stored in the pier cap during construction, sat in trapped rainwater for a couple of years, then were tensioned by hydraulic jacking and fractured by stress-corrosion cracking [66:52][67:54][79:26].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The Point Pleasant (Silver) bridge collapse of 1967 is the anchor case: heat-treated 1060 high-carbon steel eye-bars with low toughness, where a brittle fracture grew from a corrosion crack only about an eighth of an inch deep after 40 years, too small to find in service inspection, and the two-eye-bar member had no redundancy [42:52][46:04][48:08]. This drove the Charpy V-notch toughness requirement and the fracture-critical concept [48:38][49:10]. Galvanized-bolt failures are named: over-tapped nuts without lubrication cause thread galling and stripping, so the rotational-capacity test in F3125 Appendix 2 verifies the nut-bolt assembly together [58:03][60:40]. Using torque to control tension is shown failing: a poorly lubricated bolt reads high torque with little tension [61:41]. A325 above 1 inch historically dropped in strength, now corrected to 120 ksi for all diameters [56:26].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Material selection drives cost directly here. Weathering steel costs about 10 percent more than non-weathering but offsets that by eliminating painting and field maintenance, netting roughly the cost of painted steel [29:56]. A1010 corrosion-resistant steel costs substantially more and is limited to 2 inches, so it is reserved for hostile coastal or near-water spans, not general use [52:13][72:32]. HPS 70W enables hybrid girders, pairing a lower-strength web with a higher-strength flange where strength is needed for flexure, which is a material-optimization and weight play at the pier and in long-span tension flanges [31:58][32:29]. Plate-length limits from heat-treat furnaces (about 50 feet for Q&T versus longer TMCP) can force splices on long members and should be checked before committing a section [33:32]. Galvanized fasteners carry over-tapping and rotational-capacity testing overhead [57:01].
