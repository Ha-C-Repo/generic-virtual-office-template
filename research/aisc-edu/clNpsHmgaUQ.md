## Design of Reinforcement for Steel Members - Part 2

### Core Concept
This webinar by Bo Dowswell PE PhD covers how to reinforce existing steel beams and columns that are already carrying load, with the central problem being the preload, defined at [02:34] as the portion of total load present on the member at the time reinforcement is added. Because you usually cannot strip a member to zero load before welding plates or angles onto it, the reinforced section only resists the post-reinforcement load, so superposition must combine pre-reinforcement deflection on the bare section with post-reinforcement deflection on the built-up section [05:07]. The session strings together several short interrelated topics (deflection, torsional constant J, stitch welds, welding to loaded members, built-up column shear flow, plate buckling between welds) and closes with a 25-minute composite beam reinforcement example [61:26].

### Spec References
Cited verbatim by the speaker: Section D4 (tension members) and J3.5 for stitch weld spacing on faying surfaces [17:28]; the maximum stitch-weld spacing P equals 24 times the thickness of the thinner part joined with a 12-inch cap [18:31]; Chapter J intermittent (stitch) weld minimum length of 4 times weld size and 1.5 inches minimum [16:21]; Table B4.1A case 7 with lambda_r equals 1.4 times sqrt(E/Fy) for column local buckling [45:26]; Section E6.2 for stitch-weld spacing in built-up columns, non-staggered P_max equals 0.75 times t times sqrt(E/Fy) capped at 12 inches, staggered allows 1.12 and 18 inches [48:28]; Appendix 8 Section 8.2 amplified first-order analysis with B1 [42:14]; Manual Table 3-23 for moment and shear as a function of X [41:12]; the Code of Standard Practice out-of-plumb tolerance delta_o equal to L/1000 [38:06]; Chapter F has no provision for plate buckling between welds on beams, so a Chang and Johnston era reference is substituted [51:11]; composite beam example I.1 from the AISC design examples and Commentary Section I3.2 for effective moment of inertia [62:26][79:39].

### Technical Details
The torsional constant J differs between individual behavior (sum the J of each plate and the wide flange) and integral behavior with a continuous weld, where the larger moment arm produces a much greater J, sometimes 3 times higher [09:14][13:17]. Stitch-welded members fall between these limits, and shape-builder software computes integral J which is unconservative for lateral-torsional buckling when welds are intermittent [12:47]. For welding to loaded members, elevated base-metal temperature drops modulus, yield, and ultimate strength; an ineffective zone W_y of 1 inch for stick welding and 2 inches for self-shielded flux core is knocked out of the section, measured from the far face, and section modulus rather than plastic modulus is used to avoid a residual kink [28:20][29:23]. Built-up column shear flow VQ/I and anchor force MQ/I cannot be found directly because columns have no known transverse shear, so a notional uniform load W_n equal to P_r/(125L) is applied to mimic the L/1000 out-of-straightness moment, then amplified by B1 for second-order effect [38:37][42:14].

### Fabrication Notes
Stitch welds lower cost through less deposited weld and produce much less shrinkage distortion, which matters because weld-induced out-of-straightness sharply cuts column strength [14:50][15:21]. Minimum stitch length is 4 times weld size and at least 1.5 inches so the arc has room to establish a sound weld [16:21]. To limit heat into a loaded member the speaker recommends small-diameter electrodes, low current, stringer beads instead of weave beads, controlled interpass temperature (300 F for thin material, commonly 600 F max on a WPS), letting passes cool, and monitoring with temperature crayons [20:06][21:40][22:43]. Shelf dimension matters for fit-up: leave about 0.5 inch of horizontal shelf for a 3/16 fillet so the welder can reach the joint, more if the part may run out of tolerance [67:35].

### Erection Notes
The presentation is field-reinforcement focused, so erection content is woven into the welding discussion rather than crane or sequencing guidance. The speaker stresses getting the erector's WPS in advance and reviewing it to confirm the heat-input process will not overheat a loaded beam or column [20:06]. Overhead welds are roughly 25 percent as productive as horizontal welds, so reinforcement is detailed to allow horizontal welding where possible [67:04]. Long reinforcement is a handling problem in the field: a 45-foot 2.5 by 2.5 angle is very flexible and hard to handle, which drove the decision to use partial-length reinforcement [76:32][84:24].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The named failure mode is corrosion entering the gap between back-to-back double angles with no stitch plates, where trapped moisture (the speaker suspects coal dust held it) corroded the steel and the expanding corrosion product cracked the connecting weld [18:00]. The spacing rules in J3.5 and D4 exist specifically to keep the faying-surface gap small enough to prevent that corrosion. A second pitfall is trusting shape-builder software for J on stitch-welded members, since it returns integral J that is unconservative for the actual intermittent condition [12:47]. A third is using plastic section modulus near a hot weld, which can leave a permanent kink as the weld cools [29:23].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Stitch welds reduce welding cost and outweigh the small added cost of laying out and measuring the intermittent pattern [14:50]. Partial-length reinforcement is more economical than full-length and is preferred, with the theoretical cutoff point selected by iteration to minimize weld and material length [33:55][76:32]. Overhead welding at about 25 percent productivity is a real labor multiplier to price against horizontal welding [67:04]. The composite example shows the iteration cost directly: a strength-only cutoff at 15.5 feet (rounded conservatively to 14) failed the deflection check and had to grow to a 39-foot reinforcement length, which the speaker would avoid in practice due to handling difficulty [78:07][84:24].
