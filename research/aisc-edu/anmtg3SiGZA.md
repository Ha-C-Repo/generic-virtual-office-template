## Column Base Connection
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=anmtg3SiGZA | Duration: 1:28:13 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Dr Amit Kanvinde (UC Davis) presents a multi-year AISC-funded experimental and finite-element study on exposed steel column base connections, testing whether AISC Steel Design Guide 1 (base plate and anchor rod design) is accurate against real test data [00:38, 03:05]. The work runs in two phases: phase one is shear transfer at the base (braced-frame condition, high axial and shear, low moment) [05:47], phase two is axial-plus-flexure (moment-frame base, axial and moment, low shear) [21:34]. The headline finding is that these connections are far more ductile than design assumes, reaching 8 to 10 percent column drift with no brittle failure, and that Design Guide 1 is accurate for friction and anchor-rod bearing but unconservative for shear lugs and overly conservative for compression-side plate yielding [20:57, 56:00].

The speaker cites these by name: AISC Steel Design Guide 1, the base plate and anchor rod design guide, throughout [03:05]; AISC 2010 Seismic Provisions, which already carried some of these findings and were downloadable from the AISC website at the time [09:42], with a questioner naming seismic provisions section 8.3.3 on connection ductility [25:56]; the AISC steel specification, which absorbed the shear-transfer findings [09:51]; ACI 349 (the nuclear standard) as the source of the concrete size-effect penalty method [18:39]; and ACI 318 Appendix D for anchorage to concrete, which the speaker says penalizes headed anchors versus rebar [11:51]. A slide shows the Design Guide 1 shear-key cone equation Vn45 = (4 sqrt f'c) A45 [16:34 frame], and the proposed size-effect equation min of phi-CCD (1/sqrt c)(40/9 sqrt f'c) A35 and phi45 (4 sqrt f'c) A45 [19:20 frame]. Anchor rods are ASTM F1554, called "1554" verbally [25:25], Grade 36 and high-strength 105 ksi. The shear-bracket reference equation on slide 84 cites Mattock and Gaafar 1981, Marcakis and Mitchell 1980, and Harries et al 1997.
### Technical Details
The exposed connection tested is a W8x48 column (about two-thirds scale of a W14) on a base plate over grout over a concrete footing, with anchor rods embedded in the foundation and leveling nuts under the plate for plumbing before grouting [23:00, frame slide 33]. Phase-two test matrix had seven specimens varying plate thickness (1, 1.5, and 2 inch), nominal rod yield (36 and 105 ksi), rod count (four corner rods, one test with eight), and gravity load (0 to 152 kips); rods were 3/4 inch diameter, headed with two nuts sandwiching a washer at the bottom, set deep so concrete pullout did not control, grout 8 ksi [Test Matrix frame slide 37, 48:59, 53:21]. The load-displacement loops are pinched with an intermediate plateau caused by the plate lifting off the yielded anchor rod, with residual moment from the axial pre-stress effect [38:39]. Strain gauges on two sides of each rod read equal, proving the rods carried near-pure axial tension with negligible bending and negligible prying [52:09, 17:58]. The eight-rod test showed the two interior rods do almost nothing; the connection behaves like a four-corner-rod plate [43:55].
### Fabrication Notes
The column-to-base-plate weld in the test specimens was a partial-joint-penetration weld on the outside of the flange with a reinforcing fillet weld on the inside of the column flange, all toughness-rated, sized so the total weld throat develops the column flange rather than the weld fracturing first [14:49]. The closing demonstration shows a base-plate weld fracture where a PJP weld at a column flange corner cracks and the whole flange tears, the failure mode this detailing is meant to prevent [13:42, 14:08]. Phase-one friction specimens deliberately included mill scale on the base plate and shim stacks under the plate to match real erection, conditions earlier test programs had omitted [10:14]. Plate thickness is the fabrication lever that shifts the failure mode between base-plate bending and anchor-rod yielding [49:55].
### Erection Notes
The connection model follows standard exposed-base erection: anchor rods cast in the foundation, leveling nuts set and adjusted to plumb the column, then grout placed under the base plate for uniform bearing [23:29]. Shim stacks under the plate are a real erection practice the friction tests reproduced [10:14]. The footings were stepped in the lab only to hook the strong floor and to keep the bearing confinement realistic, not a field condition [22:00, 27:07]. Anchor-hole size was standard slightly-oversized per Design Guide 1, with washer plates to prevent dishing of the washers, generally not welded in the flexure tests [36:53].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The central design-side error flagged is using the 45-degree cone method for shear-lug or shear-key bearing: it overpredicts blowout capacity by roughly a factor of two (test-to-predicted about 0.5) for foundations larger than about 6 inches edge distance, because concrete fracture, not full stress-block development, controls; the size-effect penalty fixes this to a test-to-predicted of about 1.1 with COV 0.2 [16:20, 19:20, frame slides 24 and 29]. A second design error is treating these bases as fixed on a strength check alone, which does not guarantee stiffness; the base alone contributed more than half a percent of drift at design-level load, so they are between pinned and fixed (pinned is the conservative assumption for column sizing) [33:00, 37:39]. The physical failure shown is a brittle PJP base-plate weld fracture tearing the column flange [13:42]. Biaxial loading dropped major-axis strength 30 to 40 percent, an effect no design guide covers [07:48].

The speaker observes that Design Guide 1 is so conservative for compression-side plate yielding (off by up to 100 percent) that a designer who drops that limit state and checks tension-side and anchor-rod mechanisms instead can justify thinner, lighter base plates [56:00, 06:00]. ### Your Company Application
The hard takeaway for our anchor-rod work is that the Design Guide 1 shear-lug 45-degree cone method is unconservative; if Ivan or our in-house PE ever sizes a shear lug or shear key for high base shear, the design must apply the concrete size effect, not the bare cone equation, or it can be off by a factor of two. For Tekla detailing and Mario's shop, the validated base-plate weld is a PJP on the flange outside plus a reinforcing fillet inside, toughness-rated, sized to develop the column flange, which matches conventional structural-steel practice and should be the default note over an undersized weld. For anchor-rod design and setting, the study confirms ASTM F1554 rods with leveling nuts, standard slightly-oversized holes, and adequate embedment so concrete pullout does not govern, and confirms Design Guide 1 is fine for friction (mu = 0.45) and anchor-rod bearing. Tell estimators and erectors that exposed bases are flexible, not truly fixed, which matters for drift and for any ACP frame analysis assuming fixed bases.
