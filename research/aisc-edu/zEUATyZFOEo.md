## Connection Design as the Fabricator's Representative

### Core Concept
This is AISC Night School 18 Session 4, presented by Chad Fox PE SE of Ruby and Associates, on the role of the delegated connection designer who works as the fabricator's representative [01:14]. The thesis is that when the engineer of record (EOR) selects delegated design under the AISC Code of Standard Practice, the fabricator's connection engineer must design every connection as its own free-body diagram with a clear understanding of load and load path, while continuously steering geometry toward shop and field efficiency [37:54]. The whole bid-to-build workflow is framed around counting connection types, writing RFIs to remove costly conditions, and choosing repetitive sizes because the cost of a steel building is more about labor time than material weight [57:35].

### Technical Details
COSP gives the EOR three options: design all connections, let an experienced detailer select prequalified Manual-table connections, or delegate design (Option 3), with 3A and 3B newly clarified in the 2016 COSP for member reinforcement at connections [05:35, 09:31]. Main-member reinforcement is not connection material, so under 3A the EOR designs it, and under 3B the EOR shows project-specific concepts plus estimated quantities so all bidders price the same scope; if neither is shown, reinforcement is excluded from the bid and later designed by the connection-design PE in final configuration [08:59, 09:52]. Connection-type families covered include simple shear (single and double angle, shear tab, extended shear tab), bracing (gusset, uniform force method), and moment connections (bolted flange plates, extended end plates, false-flange transfers). The uniform force method in Manual Part 13 and Design Guide 29 breaks brace force into gusset-to-beam and gusset-to-column components, and Special Case 2 lets the designer shift the vertical brace component through the gusset-to-column connection to relieve an overloaded beam-web-to-column joint [72:47].
### Fabrication Notes
Move fit-up and assembly to the shop because shop labor is more efficient, tools are available, access heights are lower, and weather is controlled, which usually means shop welding or bolting and field bolting [57:05]. Select readily available material from Tables 2-4 and 2-5: A992 for W and WT shapes, A36 default for channels and angles, and either A36 or A572 Gr 50 for plate since plate cost differs little [56:50]. Fillet welds are preferred, single-pass at 5/16 inch or smaller, and efficient to about 3/4 inch [36:43, 61:43]; PJP welds rank next and a groove weld with a 1/8 inch land plus thicker base material is often cheaper than a CJP of the same size because no backer bar is needed [63:50, slide pg 73]; CJP welds are least preferred because of material prep, backer bars, stringent inspection, and certified welders [64:58]. Watch K-detail dimension for clearances versus K-design for calculations, use standard flange gauge from Table 1-1, and respect fillet radius when squeezing material via Figure 10-3 (riding the fillet) [68:34, 67:11]. Flange plates are oversized in depth to absorb mill rolling variation, then filled with shims, and made wider or narrower than the beam flange to allow corrective fillet welds if bolt holes misalign [76:06, 77:06].
### Erection Notes
The designer must keep the ironworker safe; OSHA erectability requirements sit in Manual Part 2 and govern minimum bolt-up when erecting members [51:43]. Opposing beams framing into column webs or girders over columns require a staggered connection or an erection seat so the first beam stays supported while the second is brought in and bolted, shown in Fig 10-2 [53:25, slide pg 60]. Freestanding columns need four anchor rods and must resist a 300 lb force at 18 inches from the outer column face in any direction, a load case the base-plate designer (usually the EOR) must include [52:45]. A real lesson learned: gussets for fillet-welded HSS braces detailed too tight could not be rotated into position, forcing a week of redesign, so the fix is a maximum-setback dimension at the brace end so the detailer can place it for field fit [70:26]. Slip-critical bolts with oversized or slotted holes ease erection because they tolerate fit-up error, whereas bearing bolts erect faster only when holes line up [85:34].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Envelope-loading confusion is the most common issue: a building with 50 load combinations yields max and min member loads from different combinations, so nodes show apparent through-forces and often lack equilibrium, triggering RFIs to confirm true forces [37:51, 39:15]. Eccentric work lines are a red flag, illustrated by an 8 inch offset producing M = 347 ft-k with no moment listed on the design drawings, which may be a modeling error needing member up-sizing (slide pg 47). Long copes where a shallow beam (W8, 3/16 web) frames into a deep girder (W36) over a 12 inch cope length risk flexural buckling and block shear, fixed by up-sizing to W12 or reinforcing the web per Manual Part 9 [42:09]. Other recurring problems are details copied from prior projects that may not tell the whole story [22:02], stocky beams with no room for shear material, four-inch-flange moment beams with no bolting room, and too many members at one node, which Design Guide 23 and 3D BIM help avoid [50:12].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The slide cost-breakdown pie for erected structural steel is roughly material 28 percent, fabrication 46 percent, erection 26 percent, so less weight is not less cost and up-sizing for repetition is generally favorable [29:13, slide pg 35]. Connection material alone can be 5 to 30 percent of total tonnage, which estimators must not ignore [50:38]. The connection designer's fee is built by counting each connection type, estimating design time per type, and summing; cost drivers that push the fee up are lack of information, lack of clarity, obvious errors, and main-member reinforcement, while repetition of sizes and forces lowers design, detailing, and fabrication cost [27:04, 27:22, 27:55]. The bid-phase count example slide shows W33x152 girders and HSS8x8 braces with axial loads tagged per location (for example A = +/- 80k), demonstrating that loads belong at each node for an efficient bid [35:00, slide pg 35]. Transfer forces released by RFI after bid, or RFI-driven member up-sizing, can constitute a change to contract documents and a legitimate change order [88:23].
