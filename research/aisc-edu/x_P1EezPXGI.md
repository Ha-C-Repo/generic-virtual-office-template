## Got Stiffness? Designing Better Base Plates
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x_P1EezPXGI | Duration: 54:48 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
Barry Arnold (P.E., S.E., SECB, ARW Engineers, Salt Lake City) argues that lateral-force-resisting and moment base plates fail because engineers oversimplify them with P over n, the applied load divided by the number of anchor rods [07:57]. He says base plates are one of the most important elements in a steel structure, that if the base plate fails during a seismic event it is "game over" regardless of how well the beams, columns, and braces are designed [00:79], and that the real behavior must be captured by modeling the whole assembly (plate, anchor rods, grout, gusset) for relative stiffness, not by a single force divided across bolts [42:38]. The controlling idea is that base-plate and anchor-rod stress distributions are driven by the stiffness of all interconnected elements, so small geometry changes (gusset length, anchor-rod position) produce large force swings [35:44].

### Spec References
Speaker and slides cite: AISC Design Guide 1 and Design Guide 7 as the standing base-plate and anchor-rod references [47:22]; the ACI anchor-design provisions shown on the Allowable Anchor Rod Loads slide as ACI Appendix D, with D.5.2 "Steel Strength of Anchor in Tension" (phi-Ase-futa) and D.5.3 "Pullout Strength" (Np, phi-Np) [frame, ~39:00]; ASTM A563 for the nut bearing surface and ASTM F1554 anchor-rod grades 36, 55, and 105 [frame; 44:00]; and the University of Minnesota base-plate synthesis (free, web) as supplemental reading [47:35]. The speaker references "the steel manual" for nut width but cites no AISC 360 section, AISC 341 seismic section, AWS D1.1 clause, or RCSC clause by number, so weld and bolt detailing in this talk is general principle, no AISC/AWS/RCSC section cited.

### Technical Details
Concrete and grout are modeled as a compression-only spring P=kx; for 3,000 psi concrete on a 1-inch cube ACI implies about 0.003 in displacement under 3,000 lb, giving k of roughly 1,000,000 lb/in, not the 3,000 to 15,000 value most engineers assume [10:14][frame]. A higher spring constant lowers base-plate bending stress but raises anchor-rod force by about 16 to 18 percent through prying [11:35]. Anchor rods are modeled fixed at the base plate and pinned at the embedded end (length set by embedment, grout depth, rod diameter) to capture secondary shears and moments [13:01]. Prying force is governed by material strength (Fy, Fu), plate thickness, leg length, anchor-rod hole location, gauge between rods, applied load, and the spring constant [16:30][frame]. Moving anchor-rod gauge from 4 in to 8 in raised plate stress about 80 percent and anchor-rod force from about 4 percent over P/n to about 30 percent over [18:13]. Concentrating all rods on one side of the column drove a weld peak to 60 kips/in and overstressed the closest rods about 100 percent above the instantaneous-center-of-rotation value [30:56][32:41]. In the worked example, P/n gave 133.33 kips (1200k/9) but the stiffness-based FEM required 218.6 kips, a 63 percent increase; 2-in F1554 grade 55 became grade 105 [frame].

### Fabrication Notes
The anchor-rod capacity table flags a fabrication-relevant limit state that is routinely missed: bearing on the head of the nut or bolt, taken on the partial flat surface of an A563 nut, often controls over steel tension strength even for grade 36 rods [38:18][40:01]. For a 7/8-in rod the partial-nut bearing pullout (phi-Np) is only about 18.4 kips while a grade 105 rod develops 43.3 kips, so the nut bearing, not the rod, sets capacity [frame; 38:32]. Lengthening the anchor rod does not change the bearing area and does not fix this; the fixes are a plate washer, higher-strength concrete (3,000 to 7,000 psi), or a larger rod diameter [40:42][45:00]. The speaker requires continuous special inspection of grout installation because partial or voided grout (truncated cones with a hollow center) destroys the compression bearing the base-plate model assumes [15:35].

### Erection Notes
Grout placement quality is the field item the speaker stresses most: an interview with the grout installer revealed grout shoved in by hand with no packing around the rods and the job left half-grouted, which would leave roughly 50 percent voids under the plate [14:51]. He shows a field photo where a gusseted brace base plate was erected without approved or stamped calculations under contractor schedule pressure, with the supporting calculation later found to be dated 1964, and the connection had to be cut out and re-detailed in place [51:53][52:55]. He also shows a brace base plate with no stiffener on one side and no compression element to push against, which changes the rod and weld stresses [49:53][51:23].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
A study of 426 buildings after major earthquakes found 26 percent of moment-frame base plates and 29 percent of braced-frame base plates with substantial damage [05:52][06:01]. Observed failures were unexpected early anchor-rod failures, excessive anchor-rod elongation, large plate yielding leading to brittle fracture (the speaker calls the base plate a prime breeding ground for brittle fracture), crushing of grout and concrete, and weld failures between the gusset plate and the base plate [06:20]. Named design errors: using P over n, ignoring prying action, ignoring elastic properties and overall assembly stiffness, guessing a low concrete spring constant, arbitrary anchor-rod placement, assuming a thick plate cures everything (it takes over 2 in to negate prying in his simple model), and applying the uniform force method without checking that the base plate and column face are actually rigid [07:57][20:04][21:28]. The single most overlooked check he names is bearing on the nut/bolt head [40:57].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The talk is a design-method study, not a costing talk, so there are no rates or estimating figures, but several findings carry direct cost weight. Designing rods to true stiffness-based demand can mean a 63 percent higher rod force, pushing material from F1554 grade 55 to grade 105 and adding plate washers, thicker plates, or higher-strength concrete [frame; 44:00]. Trimming gusset length to save a little steel can raise anchor-rod force nearly 50 percent and weld peaks substantially, so a small fabrication economy can force a larger, costlier connection [29:18][30:56]. Continuous special inspection of grout adds a real field cost line that the design assumptions depend on [15:35].
