## Underlying Concepts to the Seismic Provisions
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y0uufRnkYhQ | Duration: 1:29:29 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Dr. Xiaoming Wong presents the two ideas that the entire AISC seismic system rests on: ductility design and capacity design. Because high-seismic ground motion can reach roughly 1g and a building designed to stay elastic at that level would be uneconomical, codes deliberately trade strength for ductility, reducing the elastic force by a ductility reduction factor (R-mu, equal to mu under the New Mark equal-displacement rule, [20:14]). The method is to strategically place "structural fuses," also called deformation-controlled elements per ASCE 41, at chosen locations that are allowed to yield and dissipate energy, while every other member is a force-controlled element kept elastic by capacity design [22:19], [25:21]. Wong's repeated thesis is that the hard, judgment-heavy part of the provisions is capacity design, not ductility detailing, and that a designer must first identify whether each member is a fuse or a force-controlled element before reading any system chapter [29:27], [60:30].

### Technical Details
For special moment frames the target is a plastic hinge in the beam, with the reduced beam section (RBS) the only connection currently prequalified for high-seismic, weakening the beam rather than strengthening it because drift, not strength, governs [37:08], [37:39]. Concentrically braced frames make the diagonal braces the fuse, expected to buckle out of plane (preferred over in-plane) and yield in tension, requiring seismically compact sections with limited b/t, and HSS members tear early at cold-formed corners if b/t is too large [23:20], [48:44]. Eccentrically braced frames make a short beam link the fuse yielding in shear; the EBF is the only system with an explicit deformation check, comparing link rotation demand (gamma) against capacity, where a short shear link gives about 0.08 radian and a long flexural link only about 0.02 radian [54:19], [57:26]. The three code factors are explained physically: R combines the ductility reduction factor and system overstrength Omega-zero (from redundancy and reserve strength), and Cd amplifies elastic drift to estimate inelastic deformation [66:40], [69:47].

### Fabrication Notes
The RBS hinge region and other fuse locations are designated protected zones in every system, where nothing should be attached or welded; Wong specifically warns against shot pins or powder-actuated fasteners fired into the protected zone, because the resulting field repairs may do more harm than the original problem [44:37], [45:08]. Fuse sections must meet seismic compactness tables for flanges and webs to delay local buckling, so material and section selection at the shop matters for ductility, not just strength [42:00]. A key discipline is not to oversize the fuse: an oversized brace or link develops more force, which then drives up the demand on the surrounding braces, beams, and columns under capacity design [83:40].

### Erection Notes
Field detailing must make the intended buckling mode actually happen rather than betting on chance; for braces the gusset must be flexible enough to allow out-of-plane hinging, enforced by the 2T clearance from the corner so the plate can bend [46:40], [50:14]. The commentary notes that the 2T clearance is defeated once a concrete slab is cast against the gusset, so a soft compressible material should be placed next to the gusset to preserve its ability to flex [52:18]. Link lateral bracing is required to stabilize EBF links, with specified strength and stiffness, and lateral-torsional bracing of moment-frame beams limits the maximum unbraced length to ensure the hinge forms [42:31], [59:58].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The central failure shown is a "Pittsburgh style" or pre-Northridge welded moment connection where the intended beam plastic hinge never formed and the welded joint fractured instead, which Wong calls embarrassing because those welds were supposed to be capacity-protected [40:23], [82:00]. Other named problems: thin doubler plates buckling in shear in the panel zone during testing, HSS braces tearing at cold-formed corners when b/t is too high, and shot pins violating protected zones [44:05], [48:44]. He also flags a code-application error he has heard from engineers and building officials: using the amplified (special) seismic load to design the structural fuse itself, which is conceptually wrong because the fuse is designed at the level-two force, not level three [73:23].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Wong states plainly that ductility is not cheap and you have to pay for it through redundancy and detailing in exchange for the force reduction R provides [69:15], [69:47]. Capacity design means braces, beams, columns, and connections must be sized for the expected (not nominal) yield strength of the fuse using a material overstrength factor plus cyclic hovering, so those surrounding members carry real cost premiums driven by fuse capacity, and oversizing the fuse multiplies that cost across the frame [76:27], [83:40].
