## Column Design: Past, Present, and Future
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L62qt8KcTcI | Duration: 1:28:53 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This is a historical and theoretical lecture by Dr. Joseph Yura (UT Austin, 32 years on the AISC specification committee) tracing how steel column strength has been predicted from Greek stone columns to the current AISC formulas [00:31][01:34]. The two through-lines are the axially loaded column curve and the beam-column interaction equation, which Yura presents in the order they evolved because that order predicts where design is heading [01:34][02:05]. The central modern idea is that the column curve is governed by residual stresses (locked-in stresses from rolling) and initial out-of-straightness, not by load eccentricity as the 1800s methods assumed [40:15][57:22][72:31].

### Spec References
The talk is conceptual and names few section numbers, but it does cite specific AISC documents and editions by year. AISC was established in 1921, the first specification in 1923, the Code of Practice in 1924, and the first manual in 1925 [33:25]. AISC switched from a Rankine-type to a parabolic column equation in 1936, used parabolic equations through 1985, adopted the single Galambos-based column curve in the 1985 LRFD spec [35:30][59:58][60:03], and the Johnson parabola of the late 1800s became the AISC column curve adopted in 1961 [23:36][46:55]. The current code uses the direct analysis method in the main body of the AISC specification with the effective-length (K-factor) method moved to an appendix [69:58]. The Eurocode multiple-column-curve approach (1978, table from the 1993 Eurocode) is cited for contrast; AISC rejected it [58:27][58:58]. The SSRC guide to stability design chapter 15 and the AISC ninth edition are named by an audience questioner [80:59]. No AISC 360 J or F section, AISC 341, AWS D1.1, or RCSC clause is cited; general principles otherwise.

### Technical Details
Residual stresses of roughly 0.3 Fy compression at the flange tips (the Ketter distribution) cause the tips to yield first under axial load, shrinking the effective elastic cross-section and reducing stiffness, which is why a stiffness reduction factor (tau) appears in the tables [41:15][44:21][91:00]. Because weak-axis moment of inertia depends on the cube of the elastic-section depth while strong-axis is linear, residual stress hurts weak-axis buckling far more, so weak-axis controls [45:25][62:40]. Yura's key forward-looking finding: modern cold rotary straightening of rolled shapes (now up to 370 lb/ft sections) leaves virtually no compressive tip stress and often slight tension at the tips, which would raise the column curve roughly 10 to 15 percent and would let designers use P/PY in the first interaction term with a plain second-order elastic analysis [72:31][73:33][76:45][77:15]. The direct analysis method reduces elastic stiffness via tau to mimic an inelastic second-order analysis so K can be set to 1 [68:55][70:29].

### Fabrication Notes
The fabrication content is about rolled-shape production, not shop fit-up. Yura explains that continuous casting, continuous rolling, and standard cold rotary straightening now plastify members as they pass through a series of rolls to come out straight, which changes the residual-stress pattern from the 1960s assumption [72:31][73:03]. A visible diagnostic he shows on a W12x65: mill scale flakes off where the cold straightening reached yield, and light surface corrosion (rust marks) then appears across that flange width, indicating the section was rotary straightened [73:33][74:35]. No shop QC, fit-up tolerance, or weld-process fabrication procedure is covered.

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The errors discussed are historical design and analysis errors, not shop or field rejections. Tredgold's 1822 first metal column formula wrongly assumed the axial-load deflection shape was the same parabola as the end-moment shape [11:30]. Two named structural failures illustrate the cost of weak or isolated specifications: railroad bridges failed at about one per week averaged over a decade partly because individual engineers each used their own column curves, and the 1907 Quebec Bridge collapsed from a compression-member failure under one engineer's unusually liberal specification, which triggered the move to consensus design specs [22:00][30:21][30:53]. A common modern oversight named is that first-order analysis ignores how axial load changes frame moments [48:01].
