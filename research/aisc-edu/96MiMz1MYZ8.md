## Night School 6, Session 3: Bracing Connection Details and Prying Action
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=96MiMz1MYZ8 | Duration: 1:27:53 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
William A. Thornton (cives, TR Higgins 1995) teaches the three common bracing-connection details at a column and the AISC 14th edition manual prying-action method that governs two of them. The three details are double clip angles, shear tabs or shear plates, and end plates [02:08]. Prying action is the additional bolt tension Q that develops when a relatively flexible fitting (column flange, WT, clip angle, end plate) bends, so the real bolt force P+Q exceeds the applied demand T [10:25][12:01]. Clip-angle and end-plate connections require a prying check; the conventional single-column shear tab does not, which is its main advantage [05:13][14:33].
### Spec References
Speaker-cited references: the prying requirement appears in the 2010 AISC Specification (14th edition manual) and in every spec since the 7th edition (about 1970), worded as required tensile strength shall include any tension from prying produced by deformation of connected parts [09:22][09:54]. Detailed method is Part 9 of the 14th edition manual [10:25][22:51]. Appendix 3 paragraph 3.2 (fatigue) requires elastic stress including prying, and the manual prying method is NOT elastic [33:16][68:00]. Bearing shear-tension interaction is Specification Section J3.7 [44:36]; slip-critical interaction is Sections J3.8 and J3.9 [57:03][62:48]; bolt strengths from Table J3.2 (also Manual Tables 7-1, 7-2) and pretension from Table J3.1 [42:31][65:28]; slip-critical shear from Manual Table 7-3 [58:08]. He also names AASHTO 10.32.3.3.2 for elastic fatigue prying Q [33:48]. The F-sub-y to F-sub-u change traces to Swanson, Engineering Journal 2002.
### Technical Details
The simplified model is the Struik and de Back two-hinge model (Delft, 1969), with the bolt force placed at the inner edge of the bolt and moments M1 at the stem line and M2 at the bolt line [14:33][15:35]. Geometry: a is toe-to-bolt-center, b is bolt-center-to-fitting-face, with a-prime and b-prime adjusted by half a bolt diameter, rho equals b-prime over a-prime, and delta equals 1 minus hole-width over tributary length p [16:06][49:13]. T-sub-C is the fitting thickness that fully develops the bolt design strength B; any thickness beyond T-sub-C wastes material [23:54][50:49]. Since the 13th edition, F-sub-u (not F-sub-y) is used in the flange-bending limit because strain hardening lifts capacity, while phi stays 0.9 [22:19][23:23]. Alpha-prime locates the design in admissible space: greater than 1 means the fitting controls (use alpha=1), less than 0 means the bolts control (use alpha=0), between 0 and 1 means both control [34:19][35:22]. Two reciprocal algorithms, analysis and design, return the same connection [38:55][55:28].
### Fabrication Notes
Clip angles are praised for ease of fabrication because the beam is cut slightly short with a setback of about half an inch, and angle placement absorbs over-run or under-run [03:09][03:40]. Rolled angles have a maximum thickness around an inch; common stocked thicknesses are quarter, five sixteenths, three eighths, half, five eighths, three quarters, seven eighths, and one inch, so you cannot always hit an exact required thickness [04:11][52:53]. End plates are hard to fabricate: the beam must be cut to exact length and the plate tends to curl from weld heat, which can compromise the out-to-out dimension; fillers correct under-run or over-run [07:47][08:19]. Shear tabs eliminate drilling thick column flanges, a major fabrication saving on heavy columns with three to four inch flanges [05:13][76:25].
### Erection Notes
Shear tabs erect the easiest: the beam with gusset attached is slipped in sideways, set on a drift pin at each end with a bolt or two, the erection crew moves on, and the bolt-up crew finishes [05:13][78:30]. Double clips and end plates need the piece slid down between or onto column flanges, which is slower. End plates minimize pieces but leave little erection tolerance [68:00]. Thornton cited a power-plant job where switching from double clips to shear tabs let the customer report the job came in about two million dollars under estimate, and an erector offered a deductive of fifty dollars per beam to convert clips to shear tabs [76:25][78:30].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The recurring design error is ignoring prying and assuming the bolt sees only the applied tension when the real force is T+Q, which can be much higher on a thin or flexible fitting [12:32]. Failing to check the column flange itself: even an adequate clip or end plate can be governed by a thinner column flange, so prying must be evaluated on both sides of the connection [08:51][13:32]. A real field consequence named was buildings ending up wider at the top floor than the bottom because end-plate weld curl and column over-roll were not managed [74:50]. He warns against welding double angles at their toes to a column, calling it ineffective with no published method, and advises designing as if column stiffeners are absent rather than relying on partial stiffening [82:36][70:14].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Detail choice is the cost lever. The shear tab is the most economical of the three: easiest to fabricate (no flange drilling), no prying analysis, and easiest to erect [77:27]. Avoiding drilling of thick column flanges is the largest fabrication saving on heavy jobs, with the two-million-dollar power-plant example and the fifty-dollar-per-beam erection deductive as concrete figures [76:25][78:30]. Over-thick fittings beyond T-sub-C add no capacity and only waste material and money [50:49]. The worked example showed a connection that looked like it had four to five times margin on raw bolt tension (29.6 vs 6.5 kips) drop to a real available strength of 10.4 kips per bolt once prying was applied, so screening on raw bolt values overstates capacity [43:35][51:51].
### Your Company Application
For Ivan takeoff and estimating, the detail-selection logic is the cost driver: default to conventional single-column shear tabs at columns to cut flange drilling, prying analysis, and erection time, and flag clip-angle or end-plate details as higher fabrication and erection cost. For Tekla detailing and the in-house PE stamp, the Part 9 prying method (T-sub-C, alpha-prime, rho, delta, F-sub-u not F-sub-y, J3.7 bearing and J3.8/J3.9 slip-critical interaction) is the governing check on every brace-to-column connection carrying axial plus shear, and the column flange must be checked on both sides. For Mario shop work, the half-inch setback on clips absorbs beam length tolerance, while end plates need exact-length cuts and weld-curl control to avoid out-to-out drift. For Paul safety and field bolting, the shear-tab drift-pin-then-bolt-up sequence is the cleaner erection method. Anchor-rod and base-plate prying and seismic anchor design were explicitly deferred by the speaker to a later session, so do not treat this video as guidance on anchor-rod prying [84:09][85:10].
