## Steel Design After College - Part 5

### Core Concept
This segment covers composite beam design under the 2005 AISC spec, focused on deflection limits, the revised shear-stud capacity equation, and using composite action to control moment-frame drift [02:04][16:29]. The speaker walks through why the US lowered stud capacities after Virginia Tech testing, how to place studs in the strong versus weak position, and how to strengthen existing composite beams when a floor's live-load requirement increases [22:40]. The throughline is that composite floors are robust and conservative in practice but the stud equation and connection forces still need disciplined detailing.

### Spec References
The speaker cites AISC Design Guide 3 for serviceability deflection limits on beams supporting CMU, brick, or cladding [01:01][01:33]. He references the AISC 2005 specification and its Chapter I commentary for the revised shear-connector equation and the lower-bound moment of inertia equation [06:12][07:16][19:04]. The earlier 1999 spec is named for comparison, including its now-removed 10-kip LRFD and 6-kip ASD minimum connection reaction requirement [15:29]. Bolt grades A307, A325, and A490 are cited by grade [24:43][25:14]. Specific deflection ratios stated: L/240 total load, L/360 live load with a 1.5 inch cap, 3/8 inch under live load plus 50% superimposed dead for cladding, and L/600 under live cladding plus 60% superimposed dead [00:01][01:01][01:33].

### Technical Details
The new stud equation keeps the historic upper bound of 0.5 times stud area times the square root of f-prime-c times E-concrete, then applies two reduction factors: Rg (stud geometry condition) and Rp (stud position factor) [05:40][06:12][06:44]. Rp is 0.6 in the weak position, 0.75 in the strong position, and 1.0 only for a stud welded directly to steel with no deck [07:16][07:47]. Rg is 1.0 for a single stud per rib, 0.85 for two studs per rib, and 0.7 for three or more [08:18][08:48]. The net effect is roughly 5 to 15% lower stud capacities than the 1999 spec, but capacity is not very sensitive to small shortfalls in connector count [08:48][11:50]. For drift, the beam is split into three zones (bare-steel negative-moment zone assumed over half the span, a composite positive-moment zone using the lower-bound I, and a bare-steel zone next to the column defined by a 45-degree line to the effective flange width), then reduced to one effective I that raises bare-beam stiffness about 10 to 30% [18:03][19:35][20:07][21:39].

### Fabrication Notes
The deck stiffening rib in the flute center forces the field welder to place the stud on one side, creating the strong-versus-weak position problem; deck manufacturers will not remove that rib [03:37][04:08]. Strengthening an existing composite beam can be done by coring down, welding a stud by hand, and grouting back with non-shrink grout to add connectors, or by welding a wider cover plate with a downhand stitch fillet weld, or welding a tee to the bottom [22:40][23:11][23:43]. If the beam already has deflection, a tee profile must follow the bow, sometimes needing a shear tab to close the gap [24:13]. The speaker references David Ricker's and John Miller's Engineering Journal papers on strengthening and welding to existing beams [23:11][24:43].

### Erection Notes
Stud placement in the field is the central erection issue: a welder told to alternate strong and weak will reliably alternate, but if told to put all studs in the strong position he may carry over to the weak side near the end unless watched, so the engineer must show the placement scheme on the drawings [04:08][04:38][05:08]. Shear-connector spacing limits are unchanged at 3D, 4D, and 6D [13:26]. For uniformly loaded beams studs go on uniformly; for girders with three-point loading, the constant-moment center region theoretically needs no studs, so the drawings must state whether the program upsized for uniform placement or gives only the minimum count [11:50][12:24][12:55].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The named risk is a field welder placing studs in the weak position when the design assumed strong, leaving insufficient shear connectors for the intended live-load capacity [05:08]. Software that auto-designs moment-frame beams as non-composite is conservative but leaves drift capacity unclaimed [16:29][26:46]. A questioner raised a real case where 4 feet at the end of a 36-foot span had no slab; the answer is to draw the moment diagram and confirm the bare steel beam carries the moment at the non-composite location, accounting for moment development past the opening [25:44][26:15][27:16]. Quality checking of beams and cambers in the shop is normally the owner's testing laboratory under a plan prepared by the engineer of record, not the engineer's own job [27:49][28:19].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Counting composite action in moment-frame drift can lower beam sizes 10 to 30% for free since drift, not strength, usually governs moment frames [17:00][21:39]. Putting actual reactions and axial loads on the drawings rather than load-table values avoids the conservative inflated forces fabricators dislike and keeps connection material from being oversized [14:26][14:56][15:59]. Upgrading an existing floor's live-load rating may hinge on the connection, for example replacing 3/4 inch A307 bolts with A325 or A490, switching slip-critical to bearing, or going bolted to welded, each carrying different shop and shoring cost [24:43][25:14].
