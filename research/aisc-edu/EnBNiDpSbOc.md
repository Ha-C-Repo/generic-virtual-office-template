## Bolting & Welding Primer - Part 5

### Core Concept
This segment teaches two things: material toughness measured by the Charpy V-notch (CVN) impact test, and five practical principles of welded connection design that prevent fracture. The CVN portion explains the ductile-to-brittle transition temperature, where lower transition temperatures mean tougher steel at low temperatures [01:33]. The connection portion drives one repeated lesson, that connections are where structures fail and that most weld failures are really connection-design failures, not welder workmanship [05:37], [06:08]. The five principles are: provide a load path for the force to enter the member parallel to the force, do not put welds in bending, there are no secondary members in welded construction, nothing welds a good for nothing (avoid welds that look full strength but are not), and avoid joints whose performance depends on the skill of the welder.
### Spec References
The speaker cites AWS weld-metal toughness requirements of 20 foot-pounds at minus 20 degrees F for the post-Northridge electrode in this example [02:34], and AWS gap limits for filet-weld fit-up of 3/16 inch for most members and 5/16 inch for thicker members, with the rule that a gap over 1/16 inch requires increasing weld size by the amount of the gap [23:09], [23:41]. Fatigue category B versus category C details are named for an attachment-to-flange weld [16:00]. Grade 50 steel (50 ksi yield) and grade 70 weld metal (70 ksi tensile, about 58 ksi yield) are discussed as a matching combination [03:04], [03:35]. No AISC 360 section, AISC 341 section, RCSC clause, or numbered Design Guide is cited in this segment.
### Technical Details
The Charpy specimen is a notched bar nominally 0.394 by 0.394 by 2.165 inches (stated as 38 by 38 metric and 2-1/2 long in the talk) struck by a calibrated pendulum hammer, with energy absorbed in fracture measured in foot-pounds [00:00], [01:02]. Toughness is high at high temperatures and low at low temperatures, separated by the transition range [01:02]. Stress concentration at connections is quantified: Dr Ralph Richards found peak stress as high as 4.2 times average stress at the center of a weld opposite the beam web for some shapes [12:22]. Northridge cracks initiated at the weld center and propagated outward (peak stress opposite the web); Kobe 1995 box-section cracks initiated at the outside edges and propagated in [11:21], [12:22], [12:53]. Throat stress rises sharply with unfilled gap: 21 ksi at proper fit, 28 ksi at a 1/16 gap, 42 ksi at 1/8, and 84 ksi at 3/16 if weld size is not increased [24:11].
### Fabrication Notes
Weld toughness for a given electrode is documented in the manufacturer certificate of conformance, which also lists the AWS requirement, yield, elongation, and the test temperature [02:34], [03:04]. Fit-up gap directly reduces effective throat, so the fabricator must increase weld size by the gap amount when the gap exceeds 1/16 inch to recover the design throat [23:41]. Backing must be continuous for the full length of a CJP groove joint; interrupted backing creates a lack-of-fusion plane perpendicular to the stress field that behaves like a crack and is also a code violation [19:35], [20:05], [20:35]. A "nothing weld" is one that looks full strength but lacks fusion across the joint, detectable only by NDE, or a filet weld where the welder melted away the corner instead of leaving it [22:07], [22:38].
### Erection Notes
No field-erection sequencing, bracing, crane, or rigging content appears in this segment; the material is connection-design and shop-weld focused.
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named failure modes include unfilled craters and undercut at weld ends where load is concentrated, multiplying the local overload (the speaker walks an example to four times anticipated load plus a workmanship defect at the weld end) [10:18], [10:49]. Lack of fusion across a CJP groove (the "nothing weld") and interrupted weld backing are flagged as fracture initiators and the backing case as a code violation [22:07], [20:35]. Real-world cracks cited: Northridge beam-flange weld cracks, Kobe box-section edge cracks, out-of-plane web cracking from unattached transverse connection plates on a bridge, a tow-truck boom that broke at interrupted backing, and a cement-truck wheel beam and laminated-arch press rack that cracked from welds loaded in bending [11:21], [12:53], [15:29], [20:35], [18:02], [21:36].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The speaker notes a designer could leave an ineffective weld off entirely to save money, provided the remaining welds are proportioned correctly, since a transverse weld doing almost no work still costs to deposit [08:13]. CJP groove welds were specified in a box-girder corner that probably did not need them, an example of overspecified joints adding cost without benefit [19:04]. The throat-stress table implies that accepting a fit-up gap without enlarging the weld is a false economy that drives the joint to failure stresses [24:11].
