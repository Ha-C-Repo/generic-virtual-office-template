## Pedestrian Bridges: Unique Analysis and Design

### Core Concept
This webinar (session B2) presents two long-span steel pedestrian bridges and centers on pedestrian-induced vibration as the controlling design problem rather than strength. The Fanny Appleton Bridge in Boston is a jointless 550 ft elevated steel structure with a 220 ft divergent pipe arch and a Vierendeel-truss main span, where the team had to push the frequency and acceleration response into a comfortable range [02:01][04:35][12:50]. The 41st Street Pedestrian Bridge in Chicago is a pair of 240 ft inclined steel pipe arches with an S-curved deck hung on cables, where torsion from single-side support and low natural frequency drove the design [38:13][43:18][45:25]. The recurring lesson is that for slender pedestrian spans the natural frequency falls near human walking pace, so designers tune stiffness, mass, and damping and then physically test the finished bridge to confirm acceleration [12:50][18:52].

### Technical Details
Fanny Appleton uses a divergent pipe arch with HSS spandrel columns forming a Vierendeel truss, paired continuous box (tub) girders running end to end, floor beams, and a cantilever bracket carrying a continuously curved steel fascia plate [04:35][05:05]. The Y-piers are built-up sections at the base transitioning to HSS arms, joined at the Y with a steel casting fabricated by CastConnex and co-designed by the engineer [05:36][06:08]. To fix vibration, the team changed the deck from 4,000 psi normal-weight to 5,000 psi lightweight foam-filled stay-in-place forms, increased spandrel-column offset from 7 to 15 degrees from plumb, stiffened selected pier foundations from a two-pile to a three-pile battered configuration, and added internal stiffeners through the column base plates [16:21][17:22]. The Chicago bridge models all steel as frame elements and hangers as cable elements in CSI Bridge, with the inclined hangers producing very large torsion and lateral force in the deck (lateral about 70 percent of vertical at the end transverse girder), addressed with hold-down devices, a special high-lateral bearing, and a permanent jacking device for redundancy and maintenance [45:25][52:09][53:42][54:13].

### Fabrication Notes
The continuously curving fascia plate on Fanny Appleton required tolerances comparable to architecturally exposed structural steel, and those tolerances propagated back into the box-girder fabrication because the girders carry the fascia through cantilever brackets and had to match for fit-up [07:40]. Bolted splices on the tub girders were kept unobtrusive by putting splice plates internal to the tub and nuts on the inside, and splice locations were positioned to be least visible [07:08]. Short spandrel columns were shop-welded to the arch sections, but longer spandrel columns could not be shop-welded due to shipping limits, so alignment studs were added to help field setting of the skewed, non-square geometry [10:46][11:17]. Arch sections shipped in two pieces and were spliced on site; the stair fascia was upsized after the original detail proved poor for fabrication [10:15][17:52]. On the Chicago tubular sections it was difficult to place internal stiffeners inside the pipes and boxes, so the hanger plate itself acts as the stiffener, and internal stiffening was used only at the high-concentrated-force deck connection [50:08][55:43].

### Erection Notes
Fanny Appleton main deck framing was fabricated in three sections and the center section was lifted onto the shorter spandrel columns atop the arch [11:48]. Erection tabs on the spandrel columns held pieces in place and were then field welded, field metallized, and field painted, and cap plates were field-welded to the tub girders [11:48][12:20]. The defining erection problem on the Chicago bridge was railroad access: six active tracks carrying about 270 trains a day, with only a 1 to 3 a.m. work window, so the team designed a temporary bridge threaded into roughly 5.5 ft of clear space between the proposed deck and the overhead catenary lines, giving the contractor 6 to 9 months of nearly uninterrupted work [34:37][39:14][40:16]. Two temporary-support options (spanning all six tracks or using a mid shoring tower within 25 ft of the clear zone) were left on the plans because the railroad would not commit, and the contractor chose the no-middle-support scheme [40:46]. The contractor built the Lake Shore Drive span first and the railroad span second to work out problems before the high-risk railroad area [58:52].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
No bolt rejections, weld-inspection failures, or NDE findings are named; this is a design-and-analysis webinar, not an inspection talk. The closest analog to a defect is the original architect-handed 45-degree arch geometry, which produced an incorrect and uncomfortable natural frequency and would have required an over-robust pipe to carry the curving deck, forcing a redesign to a 30-degree arch with added basket-handle kickers and hangers [36:10][37:11]. On Fanny Appleton the only condition that failed the maximum-comfort acceleration target was deliberate vandalism with people jumping on the structure, not normal pedestrian loading [23:27].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Both projects were design-build with fixed budgets, which directly shaped technical choices: Fanny Appleton carried a budget line for tuned mass dampers and then spent that money on physical structural improvements instead, because the owner preferred to avoid recurring TMD maintenance and retuning every couple of years [27:36][28:07]. The same fixed design-build budget is why no long-term vibration monitoring was funded after opening [26:33]. The Chicago temporary bridge was a high-ticket item that the team said paid its premium back many times over by avoiding years of railroad flagging cost and coordination [39:45]. A repeated estimating point is that engineering a detailed, constructible, biddable plan set, vetted by talking to multiple fabricators and suppliers on a level platform, let the contractor follow the planned erection sequence almost exactly and cut field guesswork and RFIs [56:51][57:21].
