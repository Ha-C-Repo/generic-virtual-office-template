## Field Fixes and Solutions

### Core Concept
AISC Night School Session 7, presented by Dr. James Fisher (December 3, 2018), is a working catalog of how a fabricator, erector, and engineer of record diagnose and repair steel that does not fit or perform in the field. Fisher's organizing rule is that labor, not material, drives the cost of a fix, so the cheapest fix is the one prevented by good detailing and the second cheapest is the one solved by adding steel [03:56], [04:19]. He frames every problem as get accurate information first, act immediately to limit delay charges, determine whether a fix is even required, then coordinate the paperwork sign-off before engineering the repair [03:19], [04:55]. The bulk of the session is anchor rods, which he spends a half hour on because the problems recur far too frequently [02:43], [29:00], followed by columns and beams, camber, shear studs, fit-up, reinforcing, and member-selection guidelines.

### Spec References
ASTM F1554 governs anchor rods, with color-coded product marking A36 blue, grade 55 yellow, grade 105 red, grade 55 available to 4 in diameter and grade 105 to 3 in, plus optional supplement S1 for weldable grade 55 [24:16], [24:46], [25:02]. AISC Manual Table 14-2 (15th edition) gives anchor-rod hole, washer, and washer-thickness sizes [25:18]. AISC Design Guide 1 addresses base-plate and anchor-rod design including pier development [26:52]. OSHA 1926.755 sets the four-anchor-rod minimum and the 300 lb at 18 in eccentric iron-worker load [27:42]. AISC Code of Standard Practice section 6.4.4 gives camber tolerances and the column plumb tolerance is H/500 [41:02], [31:41]. AISC Specification Appendix 6 gives stability bracing forces for out-of-plumb columns [31:08]. AISC Manual Table 1-22 lists ASTM A6 mill tolerances on beam depth, up to 1/4 in over theoretical [50:04]. AISC Spec Table J3.3 caps reaming at oversize-hole diameters, with the standard 1/32 in per ply and note 3.3 allowance [65:15]. AISC Spec sections J1.8 and J1.9 permit bolt-weld and weld-rivet load sharing, J1.9 requiring weld strength at least 25% of required [62:57], [64:11]. AWS section 7.6 covers shear-stud qualification [44:24].

### Technical Details
Anchor rods that are too short can be repaired by welding a threaded extension, a coupling nut, cutting and epoxy anchors, or welding the base plate to the rod, with the hard limit that you cannot weld to high-strength 105 ksi rods [09:33], [09:54]. Fisher repeatedly steers to larger-diameter A36 (F1554 grade 36) rods over high-strength rods precisely because A36 is weldable and ductile enough to cold-bend or heat-straighten when bent [10:14], [16:59]. A nut is acceptable when the rod top is at least flush with the top of the nut; below that you must check thread shear strength [91:20]. Weld shrinkage shortens a CJP weld by roughly 1/8 in, which accumulates into out-of-plumb columns on long column lines [32:27]. ASTM A6 mill tolerance lets a beam over-run theoretical depth by up to 1/4 in, the root cause of continuity-plate and wing-plate misfits [50:04].

### Fabrication Notes
Camber is measured in the unstressed position in the shop and cannot be verified once the member is on the job site, so any camber dispute is settled at the fabricator [41:22]. Members to camber are filler beams, composite floor beams, girders, and trusses; do not camber spandrels, cantilevered or moment-connected or braced beams, torsion-loaded members, beams under 20 to 25 ft, beams with webs 1/4 in or thinner, or any camber under 3/4 in, and use a heavier beam instead [34:11], [35:43], [36:36]. Continuity plates should be detailed thicker than the beam flange, Fisher's example being upper stiffener 1/4 in greater and lower 3/8 in greater, to absorb A6 tolerance [47:46]. End plates are detailed short about 1/8 in each end for A6 and fabrication tolerance [33:48]. Embed plates use 1/2 in minimum for deck bearing and 3/4 in for beam bearing as heat sinks, and should always be oversized in plan because they are routinely mislocated [53:06], [52:50].

### Erection Notes
The four-rod OSHA minimum gives erectors a false sense of security; partly erected steel under wind must be evaluated separately, since anchor rods sized only for the 300 lb at 18 in load will not hold a frame [27:58]. Fisher cites a 40 ft HSS-column building in Utah that collapsed in a 30 to 35 mph wind because a joist girder was left sitting unattached on a column top, and notes that an oversized anchor-rod substitution shifted the failure from rod breakage to footing tip-over [07:43]. Out-of-plumb columns beyond H/500 are braced using Appendix 6 forces, or the column is freed by cutting and re-setting anchor rods with epoxy [31:01]. Anchor-rod templates must be wood or steel firmly fastened to the formwork, often just 2x4s spanning to the foundation edge, so concrete placement does not drift the pattern; problems arise when the concrete contractor rather than the erector sets the rods [22:24], [88:05]. Leveling nuts suit light one- to two-story loads with a thick washer to prevent punching; heavier loads use shim stacks or setting plates, and shims can be left in place [89:01], [23:26].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Recurring failures named are anchor rods too short, too long, bent over (a bulldozer over snow-covered plates), rotated 90 degrees, or set in the wrong position, including a 1,300-rod project that had to be redone after a detailer dimensioned the setting plate wrong [20:46]. Base plates punching through leveling nuts is fixed by jacking and grouting [23:26]. Shear studs that snap during the bend-over inspection (bent at least 30 degrees, often by sledgehammer) signal a miscalibrated stud gun, qualified per AWS 7.6 [43:43]. Fit-up failures include weak-axis moment connections, continuity plates the same thickness as the beam flange, bolted holes that do not align on heavy bracing connections, and beams that do not fit between wing plates because two fabricators built columns and beams without coordinating beam over-run [45:58], [49:12]. Crane-runway fatigue cracking concentrates at tieback locations [78:41].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Fisher's central estimating message is that on a fix, labor is the expense and material is cheap, so the engineer should sharpen the pencil to see if a fix is even needed before money is spent and an argument over who pays begins [03:56], [04:01]. He recommends designing beams for about 10% extra concrete thickness because over-screeding is predictable, since extra steel is cheaper than chasing an overstressed member or a field reinforcement [42:26], [42:50]. He flags expensive specification habits: CJP welds are easy to call out but costly, doubler plates should be designed out by picking a thicker-web column, and minimum edge distances detailed too tight leave no room to ream, so add 1/8 in to edge distances as insurance [72:42], [73:11], [66:54]. He also notes engineering fees on a fix are legitimate and should be discussed openly with the team [04:55].
