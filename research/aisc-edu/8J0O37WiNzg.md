## Night School 6, Session 1: Basic Principles

### Core Concept
William Thornton, the 1995 TR Higgins lecturer and longtime chair of the AISC Committee on Manuals, teaches the foundation for bracing-connection design: the lower bound theorem of limit analysis and the principle that a connection model must stay true to the main-member design assumptions [27:54], [38:23]. The central claim is that a designer may choose any internal force distribution that satisfies equilibrium and does not exceed any limit state, and provided the steel is ductile enough to redistribute, that distribution yields a safe connection [43:33], [45:04]. The corollary is that the admissible force field which maximizes connection capacity is closest to the true collapse solution, so a smarter load path is both safe and cheaper [47:45]. He frames connection design as the "last bastion of rational design" because complex bracing connections cannot be solved by frame-analysis software and must be built from free-body diagrams and first principles [29:28], [33:03].

### Technical Details
The Code of Standard Practice gives three options for who designs connections: option 1, complete connection design shown on the structural drawings by the engineer of record; option 2, an experienced detailer selects from manual tables for simple connections; option 3, connections designed by a licensed engineer working for or hired by the fabricator [22:41], [25:49]. Under option 1 the EOR must show everything: bolt sizes, spacing, edge distances, gauge, pitch, and weld size and type, and a bare CJP symbol no longer means CJP under current AWS D1.1, it only means a weld that develops the strength of the base metal [23:11], [23:43]. The uniform force method gives one of infinitely many statically admissible force distributions for a highly indeterminate bracing connection, defined by the geometric parameter r and four edge forces [36:46], [37:18]. A three-bar tension example shows that every equilibrium-satisfying split is safe but only the compatible split (one third each) reaches the true 100-unit capacity, demonstrating the corollary [49:17], [53:26]. Bolt gauges for angles are placed on the workable gauge line from Table 1-7A, not the gravity axis, and the small resulting eccentricity is ignored for bolted angles by long-standing practice, while welded connections balance to the gravity axis [39:56], [74:21].
### Fabrication Notes
The economic comparison is the fabrication-cost heart of the session. A single bracing connection by the uniform force method ran about $664 versus about $844 by the kiss method, roughly $182 per connection, which on a 40-story building with 32 connections per floor reaches about a quarter million dollars in material and fabrication alone, before erection and shipping [65:23], [65:55]. On a Georgia job the optimized load path saved about $2 million against the owner-engineer budget, with 80 percent savings in column drilling time because fewer columns had to be drilled, 50 percent savings in weld consumables, 60 percent savings in welding labor, and 30 percent material savings from thinner plates [68:36], [71:40]. The optimized detail used 5/16 inch fillet welds instead of 5/8, a 1/2 inch plate instead of 1 inch, and 3/4 inch bolts [70:09]. He notes X-type (threads-excluded) bolts give higher shear value and are warranted once plate thickness near the nut is about 3/4 inch or more, with a thickness table in a "practical connections" reference [69:06].
### Erection Notes
Shear tabs are favored mainly for erection: a beam with a shear-tab/gusset can be brought in laterally and set easily, whereas double-clip angles must be jammed between column flanges or dropped down a column web, which becomes a problem when stiffeners are present [81:43], [82:15]. The tab trades a single-shear bolt pattern (sometimes more bolts) for a faster field set, and the shop bolt cost is more than offset by erection savings [82:15], [82:45]. Double clips have the advantage that the beam can be cut slightly short and the slack taken up in the setback dimension on the clips, while an end plate must be cut nearly exact or the piece is stuck [83:17], [83:49]. He also notes OSHA has had structural-connection erection requirements in place for roughly 15 years that the engineer must respect [21:08].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The recurring failure mode is an inadmissible force field that does not satisfy equilibrium, which voids the lower bound theorem and leaves the connection unverifiable [60:12], [62:14]. Historical American Bridge and Bethlehem practice ignored gusset couples and work-point location, and Thornton warns that detail would be caught today by a building inspector or peer reviewer and rejected [63:48], [64:19]. He cites real consequences of bad detailing: a ferry-terminal ramp connection done by hand outside the frame software failed and killed about half a dozen people, and the Hyatt Regency 1981 walkway collapse is referenced as engineers citing the Code of Standard Practice without knowing its contents [30:29], [22:09]. A named drafting defect is referencing a bare weld symbol without size, or omitting bolt pitch, on an option-1 mandated connection [24:15], [24:45]. A common knowledge gap is engineers who cannot draw a free-body or shear/moment diagram without software [33:03].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Thornton estimates that about 50 percent of the cost of erected steel is tied to connection design once you count design, detailing, shop drawings, fabrication, shipping, erection, and finishing [66:33]. The per-connection and per-building dollar comparisons make method choice a real bid lever: for one or two connections the method barely matters, but across hundreds or thousands of repeated connections the uniform force method's cheaper load path compounds into six- and seven-figure differences [63:48], [65:55]. The Georgia job's $2 million saving came from choosing the admissible force field that minimized welds, plate thickness, and column drilling [68:36], [71:40]. The takeaway for estimating is that a load-path choice, not just member tonnage, drives connection cost and should be considered when comparing bids.
