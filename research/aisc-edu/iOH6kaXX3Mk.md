## A New Approch to Design for Stability

### Core Concept
This is the 2007 T.R. Higgins award lecture by Shankar Nair of Teng and Associates, explaining the stability provisions of the 2005 AISC specification and the new Direct Analysis Method [00:00]. The thesis is that buckling as a pure bifurcation is a theoretical abstraction that does not occur in real structures, because every real member or frame carries an initial imperfection, an out-of-plumbness or out-of-straightness, that produces a starting displacement y-sub-0 [02:02][05:38]. Failure of a member loaded in nominally pure compression is therefore always a combined compression-and-flexure event, never pure crushing and never pure buckling [08:09]. The lecture argues the profession had been designing for stability "by rote" and walks through why the traditional method of neglecting imperfections and inelasticity in the analysis, then compensating with an effective-length factor K greater than one, is unconservative on moments [00:31][24:36].

### Technical Details
Three prescriptive methods account for P-Delta effects, geometric imperfections, and residual stresses [32:50][33:22]. Direct Analysis applies to all structures and makes no distinction between braced frames, moment frames, or shear wall systems [40:03]. It requires a second order elastic analysis capturing both P-Capital-Delta (load through displaced node location) and P-small-delta (load on deformed member shape) [33:52]. Geometric imperfections are handled by a notional lateral load of 0.002 times the story gravity load, equivalent to an out-of-plumbness of 1 in 500 [34:54][35:24]. Inelasticity from residual stress, of order 0.3 times yield, is handled by reduced stiffness EI-star and EA-star using a 0.8 factor plus a tau-b factor on flexural stiffness that drops below one when axial force exceeds half of yield [13:17][38:00]. With these in the analysis, members are checked at K equals 1 using the ordinary Chapter E column curve [39:33]. Delta over Delta-0 above 1.5 forces Direct Analysis as the only option [40:33].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The central named error is the traditional pre-2005 practice of neglecting imperfections and inelasticity in the analysis, which understates member demand on sidesway frames and yields a reported base moment of zero on a loaded column that in reality fails largely in flexure [24:06][24:36]. That understated moment propagates into underdesigned beams and connections, an unconservative result [25:06]. The second named failure is misuse of the sidesway alignment chart: few real buildings meet its simultaneous-buckling assumption, and leaning columns, unequal restraint, unloaded columns, and unsymmetrical plans all violate it, so K is rarely computed correctly for a real three-dimensional structure [26:08][27:40]. The speaker states he does not know anyone who calculates K correctly for a real 3D frame [28:10].
