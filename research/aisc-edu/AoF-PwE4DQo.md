## Fatigue and Fracture Design
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AoF-PwE4DQo | Duration: 1:29:11 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This is AASHTO bridge session L4, presented by Dr. Robert Connor of Purdue, covering the limit state of fatigue and fracture for highway steel bridges [00:02]. The central distinction is that fatigue is a slow, stable, cyclic-load process that progressively cracks the steel by loss of section while the material strength itself never changes [03:39], whereas brittle fracture is a sudden catastrophic failure that runs through material faster than the speed of sound with no plastic-deformation warning [22:13]. Fatigue life is governed by stress range, number of cycles, and the detail category, and is cubed-sensitive to stress range: doubling the stress range cuts life by a factor of eight [09:20]. The design curve uses a safe-life basis of mean minus two standard deviations, a 2.5 percent probability of cracking [13:30].
### Spec References
The standard cited throughout is AASHTO LRFD bridge design, the eighth edition discussed as forthcoming [48:59]. Named provisions: the detail-category fatigue tables titled "detail categories for load-induced fatigue" [57:48]; fatigue load combinations Fatigue I (infinite life) and Fatigue II (finite life) [54:40]; the load factor change from 0.75 to 0.8 on HS-20 for the fatigue truck and from 1.5 to 1.75 (Fatigue I shown as 2x effective range becoming about 2.2) [74:43]; fatigue-adjusted single-lane load distribution factors [55:46]; the constant-amplitude fatigue limit (CAFL) [07:47]; and the provision allowing fatigue to be ignored where compression dead-load stress exceeds the fatigue-limit-state stress range [82:25]. Fracture-critical inspection at 24-month arm's-length intervals is set by the CFR Code of Federal Regulations, with special AASHTO/AWS fabrication requirements [67:33]. An NCHRP report from about 1990 underlies the fatigue truck [45:21]. No AISC 360 chapter, AISC 341 section, or numbered Design Guide was cited; this is a bridge (AASHTO) talk, not a building-code talk.
### Technical Details
Detail categories run A through E-prime: category A is base metal as it comes from the mill with a 24 ksi fatigue limit, while E-prime is the worst at about 2.6 ksi, nearly an order of magnitude lower [11:22]. The categorization, developed by John Fisher at Lehigh, bundles three effects that resist numerical analysis: weld discontinuities, weld-toe stress concentrations, and residual stresses [16:34]. Finite-life equation is N = A divided by stress range cubed, where A is the detail constant from the AASHTO table [08:50]. Stress flows like water, so concentrations occur at attachment terminations, and a smooth radius can upgrade a detail from E-prime to category B while a blunt square end stays poor [35:34]. Residual tensile stresses at a weld toe approach yield (taken as 50 ksi), which shifts the real mean stress so the full stress range, including the nominal compression portion, drives crack growth [40:09]. Fracture toughness is measured directly by compact-tension or single-edge-bend tests, or indirectly by the Charpy V-notch impact test (10mm by 10mm by 50mm specimen) and inferred across a temperature transition curve; zone 3 bridge service is minus 60 degrees [27:50].
### Fabrication Notes
The talk shows residual stress directly in the shop: a CJP complete-joint-penetration weld joining a flange to a girder web requires the fabricator to first pre-bend the plate with V-bevels, then the cooling weld metal pulls the plate flat, locking in stresses near yield strength; this is correct fabrication, not a defect [37:36]. Welds are assumed fabricated to normal quality with members in good condition for the categories to apply; severe corrosion, impact damage, or any existing crack voids the category and forces a fracture-mechanics analysis instead [59:20]. Weld-toe cracking is the dominant fatigue mode when welds are left in place and not ground; grinding a weld toe smooth, as on a CJP butt weld, improves the category but the longitudinal portion is still always checked [62:26]. Fracture is controlled through material selection, welding consumables, and detailing rather than any fracture calculation [31:24].
### Erection Notes
This webinar is bridge design and inspection focused and does not cover field erection sequencing, crane or rigging, or bracing. The closest field content is in-service inspection: fracture-susceptible details cannot be made safe by inspection because the failure is sudden, so the risk must be designed out ahead of time [23:14]. Fracture-critical members are subject to hands-on inspection every 24 months [67:33].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The recurring failure named is weld-toe cracking at attachment and cover-plate terminations, perpendicular to the primary stress range, growing into the base metal where the stress concentration peaks [56:46]. Abrupt or square cover-plate and attachment terminations are flagged as E-prime poor details to avoid [64:28]. Two design misuses are corrected: you cannot treat an already-cracked member as merely a worse category (the categories assume no pre-existing crack) [59:50], and you cannot use the fatigue truck (0.8 HS-20) for an infinite-life check because many real trucks exceed it, which would falsely predict infinite life; Fatigue I with HS-20 at 1.75 is required for that [50:36]. Quiz answers clarify that tensile strength and yield strength do not influence fabricated-bridge fatigue life because welding, cutting, and drilling erase any strength benefit [76:50].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Designing for infinite life is described as economical: because stress is linear, lowering stress by 10 to 15 percent often just means a slightly deeper girder or a more fatigue-resistant detail rather than a large steel increase [54:10]. The speaker recommends infinite-life design as the default for permanent structures, reserving finite-life design for temporary bridges with a known short service window [79:52]. A practical cost point on inspection: the large sums spent inspecting fracture-critical members may not improve safety, and lane closures for inspection create real traffic-fatality risk, motivating a move to risk-based inspection [71:38].
### Your Company Application
The transferable principle for Tekla detailing and Ivan's takeoff is that connection geometry, not steel grade, governs fatigue and stress concentration: terminations and abrupt section changes are where cracks start, so smooth transitions and ground welds matter when a member sees cyclic load. For Mario as AWS D1.1 welding lead, the residual-stress and weld-toe discussion reinforces why CJP weld quality, grinding where specified, and toe condition are inspection-critical, and why a cooling CJP weld can distort or pre-stress a plate.
