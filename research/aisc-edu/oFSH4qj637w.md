### Core Concept
This is session one of an eight-session AISC Night School course taught by Rafael Sabelli of Walter P Moore, covering the principles of effective seismic design in steel [00:00]. The central idea is that steel structures resist earthquakes not by remaining elastic but by accepting controlled inelastic damage, which lets the engineer design for reduced forces using a response-modification factor R [33:57][41:38]. The chain that produces a ductile system runs from ductile material to ductile section to ductile member to a properly proportioned system, where strong connections force yielding into predictable fuse elements [62:46][127:00]. Proportioning, not raw strength, is the controlling discipline: seismic design is described as keeping inelastic demand at locations that can yield and away from locations that would fracture [56:35][114:00].

### Technical Details
The session is conceptual: response spectra built from single-degree-of-freedom oscillators, the constant-acceleration, constant-velocity, and constant-displacement regions of a spectrum, and the relation that increasing stiffness shortens period [24:08][71:05]. Yielding effectively lengthens period and adds hysteretic damping, both of which lower acceleration demand and are bundled into the R factor [69:00][71:00]. Material behavior is tied to restraint: a smooth coupon yields and necks, but triaxial restraint (notches, thick beam-flange-to-column-flange welds) suppresses yielding and pushes the steel toward brittle rupture per the von Mises yield surface [45:47][48:54]. Section ductility requires seismically compact members with tighter b/t and h/tw limits than ordinary compact sections so full yield develops before local buckling [52:29][53:00].
### Fabrication Notes
The fabrication-relevant content centers on the highly restrained beam-flange-to-column-flange weld, which the speaker repeatedly flags as a location where ductile A992 steel cannot deliver ductility because of triaxial restraint and thick column flanges [48:54][49:25]. The design answer is to move yielding off that joint using cover plates, haunches, or a reduced beam section (RBS) where the flanges are cut back so the reduced beam, not the connection, is the weaker fuse [55:33][56:04][56:35]. Access-hole geometry must be configured to avoid local rupture, and holes or other cross-section reductions are to be avoided in yielding regions [51:58][105:00]. Expected material overstrength of 10 percent up to as much as 60 percent must be carried into capacity-design proportioning, with strain hardening of roughly 10 percent for ordinary braces and around 40 percent for BRBF and EBF elements [57:06][59:10][59:40].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The dominant failure named is the pre-Northridge beam-to-column moment connection, which did not deliver the anticipated ductile behavior at the joint and drove the post-1994 testing requirements [86:39][87:11]. The session also names brittle fracture at the restrained beam-flange-to-column weld, premature local buckling of non-compact or slender sections, flange rupture, and gusset/brace connection fracture at slotted brace-to-gusset connections that historically failed in tests and in earthquakes [49:25][55:02][55:33]. A specific proportioning error is called out: after Northridge, owners made every beam the same W36 to economize on connection testing, which overloaded the lower stories and concentrated all damage in the first couple of floors [86:39][89:14]. Column plastic hinging top and bottom of a first-floor column is shown as a soft-story mechanism to avoid [65:53].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The economic argument is explicit: designing every structure to stay elastic (R of 1) is reasonable only for facilities like nuclear plants and is uneconomical for normal buildings, so codes accept inelastic damage to save construction dollars [28:46][41:38]. Higher R systems carry lower design force but require more detailing and tighter section limits, so the cost trade is detailing effort and section selection against member size [64:19]. The owner conversation flagged is that code-minimum seismic design protects life but does not guarantee continued operation or low repair cost, so hospitals, manufacturers, and asset-sensitive owners may pay for performance above code [40:35][41:05]. The Northridge example shows that economizing on connection qualification testing by standardizing one beam size produced a worse, more damage-prone structure, a false economy [88:12][89:14].
