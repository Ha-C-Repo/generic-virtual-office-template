## Metallizing - From A to Zinc
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WTs3tSGrEco | Duration: 57:47 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
This NSBA webinar by Brian Raph and Tony Mudis of Can-Am Bridges teaches thermal spray metallizing (arc spray of zinc or aluminum wire) as a corrosion protection system for steel bridges, presented as an alternative to paint and hot-dip galvanizing [00:03][04:09]. Metallizing is a cold line-of-sight spray where two electrically opposed wires arc, melt, and are atomized by compressed air onto a blasted steel surface, where overlapping molten "splats" cool into an armor-like sacrificial coating [05:11][12:49]. Zinc protects galvanically by sacrificing itself to the steel substrate, giving an essentially indefinite service life with minor maintenance [04:41][05:11][27:41].

### Spec References
The presenters cited the following verbatim: SSPC-SP10 near-white blast as the minimum surface prep [06:42][31:17]; ASTM B833 for 99.9% zinc wire [21:35]; the FHWA Tech Brief on slip and creep of thermal spray coatings, authored by Dr. Justin Ocel, October 2014, finding unsealed metallized surfaces meet Class B [40:04][44:09][51:54]; ISO 2063, SSPC-CS 23.00 / AWS C2.23M, and NACE No. 12 as related metallizing standards [44:09]. RCSC Class B slip-critical with a minimum 0.5 slip coefficient was described but not cited by RCSC section number [39:32]. Applicator qualification was attributed to NACE or SSPC certification, no clause number [47:16]. The forthcoming AASHTO/NSBA Steel Collaboration TG8 guide specification for metallizing was referenced with a draft targeted for January [40:35][49:51].

### Technical Details
Adhesion is a mechanical bond into a sharp angular anchor-tooth profile, ideally about 3.5 mils deep with 2.5 mils the minimum, achieved using steel grit rather than round shot [07:42][09:13]. This differs from galvanizing, which is a metallurgical alloy layer that becomes part of the steel [08:12]. Three consumable "flavors" exist for bridges: 99.9% pure zinc, 85/15 zinc-aluminum alloy (denser, not thicker, using about 20% more wire per square foot, roughly $1 to $2/SF more), and 99.9% aluminum (rarely used in bridges, preferred for high-temperature industrial work) [18:01][23:05][24:36]. Zinc gives greater galvanic protection and is easier to spray; 8515 adds chloride resistance for coastal/marine settings; zinc should not be used in service above 140 degrees F [19:34][22:35]. Sealers of low viscosity may be applied to preserve coating weight, though many states leave the coating unsealed and still expect about 30 years before consumption [25:39][27:11].

### Fabrication Notes
Blasting to SSP10 with steel grit is the single most important quality factor, and hand blasting is the dominant method for getting the angular profile; it is a slow, labor-intensive step [06:42][13:50]. Temperature does not affect the ability to metallize, but temperature and humidity control the allowable window between blasting and coating; Can-Am uses an 8-hour blast-to-coat window in its shops to limit flash rust [10:15][11:17]. Because it is a cold process keeping steel under 250 to 300 degrees F, there is no curing or cooling time, no risk of weld damage, and no camber or curvature distortion, so coated girders move through the shop quickly and can be inspected by hand immediately [03:39][14:57]. Overspray zinc is collected and recycled; one plant reported metallizing about a million SF/year yielding 50 to 60 tons of recycled zinc [29:43].

### Erection Notes
Metallizing has no size or shape limitation, so very large box girder units (25 to 30 tons) that cannot fit a galvanizing kettle were metallized, sealed, and erected with no field touch-up [03:08][34:48]. Several projects were coated fully off-site and floated or dropped into place where setting up site blast operations was not feasible, with any shipping damage repaired before reaching the inaccessible site [35:20][35:51]. The coating is rugged enough that shipping or field damage is unlikely [32:47].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
No formal rejection list was given, but failure modes named were: a peened or dimpled profile from round shot instead of the required sharp angular profile, which prevents proper mechanical bond [09:13]; flash rust from exceeding the blast-to-coat window in humid conditions [11:17]; and inaccessible stiffener snipes and acute plate angles that the spray gun cannot reach, which are instead repaired with an organic zinc product like a paint touch-up [45:41][46:11]. Inexperienced applicators struggle to develop the required anchorage profile and uniform application [47:16].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Third-party metallizing runs roughly $12 to $14/SF because the applicator must mobilize cabinets and crews into the shop, while an in-house fabricator that has climbed the learning curve is competitive with a three-coat paint system [52:26][52:57]. The 8515 alloy costs about $1 to $2/SF more than pure zinc and consumes about 20% more wire for the same coverage [23:05]. Small members such as cross frames are not economical to metallize and are better hot-dip galvanized as a complement to metallizing the large girders [54:29]. Metallizing wire is exempt from Buy America (BABA) because it is neither iron nor steel [18:32]. The schedule advantage is throughput: no cure or cool time means immediate handling and inspection [14:21].
