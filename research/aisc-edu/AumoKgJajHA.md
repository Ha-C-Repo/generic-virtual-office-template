## Constructability of Structural Steel Buildings: Part 4
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AumoKgJajHA | Duration: 56:46 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
This webinar, presented by Jay Ruby and Jeff Gasr of Ruby and Associates, teaches AISC Design Guide 23 (Constructability for Structural Steel Buildings), focused on Chapter 6, constructability of erecting steel [06:12]. The thesis is that erectors hold field knowledge that should be brought to the design team as early as possible, ideally before bid, to cut cost and schedule through the constructability influence curve, where early decisions cost the least and late changes cost the most [03:38]. A repeated theme is that the least-weight structure is not the least-cost structure [51:05], and that shop and erection labor (not material, which has settled at 25 to 30 percent of total cost) now drive steel delivery cost [04:39].

### Spec References
The speakers cite AISC Design Guide 23 throughout and walk its section numbers aloud: 6.1 complete coordinated construction documents [11:57], 6.2 site constraints [15:32], 6.3 season of construction [17:37], 6.4 sequence of construction [20:11], 6.5 construction schedule [22:15], 6.8 temperature adjustments [35:39], 6.9 special tolerances [37:39], and 6.11 general erection tolerances [51:05]. They cite the AISC Code of Standard Practice repeatedly, naming Section 7.1 (owner's designated representative for construction setting sequence) [20:11], Section 7.10.1 (SE shall define erection-sequence requirements: shores, jacking, elevations) [25:51], and Section 7.10.3 (erector responsibilities, structural stability maintained at all times) [41:48]. They reference ASTM A6 mill tolerances (sweep, camber, flange roll) [37:39], AWS for welded-assembly tolerances distinct from AISC [52:39], and AISE Technical Report 13 for mill buildings with overhead cranes [53:09].

### Technical Details
Connection and member types discussed: anchor rods and base plates, with a base-plate critique that four-bolt patterns can leave corners unshimmed (a two-tier column on leveling nuts and a plate washer is shown) [23:47]. Lateral systems covered concrete cores versus brace-frame substitutions, where Ruby replaced a 2-foot-thick concrete core in a 10-story Detroit building with a brace frame to avoid cure time [15:01]. The stadium case used 90-foot cantilever plate girders (26 of them), a tension tie cable, and an uplift strut redetailed from a welded knife plate to a 3-inch-diameter pin at the bottom and a 3-inch slot at the top for single-pick erection [29:58]. Trusses, double-angle and tee top cords, joists, perlins, and roof deck diaphragms are all treated as stability-critical members [88:00 region near 43:18].

### Fabrication Notes
Steel is fabricated workpoint to workpoint with no temperature input, cut at roughly 60 to 70 degrees in the shop [35:39]. Design Guide 23 Section 6.8 gives the field rule: a 100-foot truss changes length about one eighth of an inch for every 15-degree shift from its fabrication temperature, so a 65-degree shop piece set in a 120-degree desert afternoon must have connection allowance for that growth [36:39]. Fabricators should account for ASTM A6 mill shape tolerances (sweep, camber, flange roll) since special tolerances tighter than A6 are hard to hit [37:39]. Primer weather resistance and whether a top coat is applied are flagged as fab-and-field coordination items affected by season [19:40].

### Erection Notes
Erection guidance centers on single-pass erection, keeping the crane moving west to east or north to south with no comeback work [21:43], and just-in-time delivery so steel goes up fast and consumes less laydown [21:13]. Site constraints per the Code of Standard Practice require access for delivery, laydown space, and room for boom and tail swing; soil bearing for the crane is checked (the example is a Manitowoc 16000 with 220 feet of boom and 180 feet of luffer) [16:36]. Special erection procedures (shores, shore adjustment, jacking, elevation control) belong to the SE per Code Section 7.10.1, and the erection engineer should share building models to catch load reversals from temporary conditions [25:51]. Sun cambering is noted: as the sun heats one side of a column or cantilever, the structure bows away from the sun, which the erector must distinguish from a real elevation error [37:09].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The clearest failure shown is the industrial-facility roof where three bays were erected about 3 feet out of plumb because the 120-ton truss had an L-over-b slenderness of 180 against a target of 80, in a building with no sway frames, a cantilevered bottom-cord diaphragm system, a million square feet under roof, 3/4 mile long by 240 feet wide [45:21]. The fix required an auxiliary crane to set three joists per truss, temporary sway frames every three bays, cable horizontal bracing at the top cord, and cable bracing to supplement permanent vertical bracing that could not take temporary erection loads [47:25]. Other named errors: unshimmed base-plate corners on tall columns [23:47], stripping bolts out of an erected beam below the safety-connection minimum [24:20], and over-tightening a two-point bridle so the top cord goes into tension and bottom cord into compression, reversing the design [44:18].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Design-bid-build offers no reward for quality erectors: bidding against 16 erectors and 12 fabricators, the low bidder wins and hopes there is enough fat left to make money [08:17]. Material cost has flattened at 25 to 30 percent while shop and erection labor climb, so labor is where margin is won or lost [04:39]. Cost drivers to price include temporary shoring towers, welding enclosures (and how many times a welder must be re-enclosed for field welds) [19:09], crane sharing with the concrete contractor on a shared tower crane [22:46], and soil improvement timing that can be pulled forward to save crane mat and schedule cost [16:36]. The hospital case showed schedule compressed from 52 weeks to 35 weeks on an identical design purely by getting erectors, concrete, and crane users at the table early [54:42].
