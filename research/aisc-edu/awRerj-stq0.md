### Core Concept
This session four of the AISC Night School, presented by Rafael Sabelli of Walter P Moore, teaches the seismic design of buildings as a system of interlocking codes rather than a single document [00:32][02:04]. The central concept is the risk-targeted maximum considered earthquake (MCE sub R), a ground motion defined so that a code-conforming building has no more than a 10% chance of collapse if that shaking occurs, calibrated on paper to no more than a 1% chance of earthquake-induced collapse in a 50-year period [09:52][11:23][12:24]. The design force chain takes the MCE spectrum, multiplies by 2/3, divides by R over I sub E, and the result is the very reduced level structures are actually proportioned to, with real performance coming from ductility and overstrength embedded in the R factor [13:55][24:40].

### Technical Details
The code hierarchy is layered: ASCE 7 gives loads and analysis, AISC 360 gives element strengths, and AISC 341 gives proportioning and detailing, a division the speaker confirms in a poll [78:23][79:25]. R factors run to 8 for the most ductile steel systems; special concentrically braced frames carry R of 6 with much of that from overstrength, and cantilever column systems carry low R because of low overstrength [26:14][26:44]. Risk category (one through four) drives the importance factor I sub E (1.0, 1.25, 1.5) and the drift limit (2% H for risk categories one and two, lower for three and four) [16:57][19:33][20:04]. The structural period is taken from the approximate formula T sub A = C sub T times height to the X, capped at CU times T sub A for the strength check, with CU near 1.4 in high-seismic regions [27:46][29:49][30:19]. The omega sub O overstrength factor (ranging two to three) amplifies forces for collectors and elements supporting discontinuous frames and walls [33:22][33:52]. Stability is checked through the stability coefficient theta capped at 0.25, and redundancy gives rho of 1.0 for well-distributed systems or 1.3 for few or poorly distributed elements [35:24][37:28][37:58]. Diaphragm forces are applied one level at a time, not simultaneously, plus separate transfer forces in dual systems [53:19][54:20].
### Fabrication Notes
This session is loads and analysis, so direct shop fabrication content is light, but two items carry to the shop. Collector members are typically wide flange with discrete bottom-flange bracing and are checked for constrained-axis flexural torsional buckling where a composite or perpendicular steel deck restrains the top flange, which changes the controlling unbraced length the detailer and shop must honor [55:20][56:22][58:22]. Stair connections in the seismic system are detailed with slotted holes so the stringer can move in its stiff direction during story drift, and the shop must fabricate those slots so they do not bind and still carry gravity load [68:08][68:40]. The seismic weld toughness rules that the shop and welder follow come from AWS D1.8 as adopted by AISC 341 [08:47].
### Erection Notes
Direct erection sequencing and rigging are not covered in this loads-focused session. The relevant field-side point is that diaphragms brace the gravity columns at every story so the columns can use the story height as their unbraced length; that load path must exist in the completed structure, meaning deck and its connection to the framing are part of the lateral system, not just gravity [51:46][52:16]. Seismic separation joints at property lines and thermal breaks must preserve a designed gap so adjacent structures do not pound, which the field has to lay out and protect [69:41][70:43].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The session frames conceptual design errors rather than inspection rejections. Named pitfalls: reading a too-flexible period from an analysis model and using it unbounded, which understates required base shear, so the code caps the period at CU times T sub A [29:19][29:49]; designing lower-level members only to the ELF force distribution, which underloads them and leads to failure there, the reason the supplemental diaphragm force exists [53:19]; placing a non-ductile moment connection in a ductile special moment frame, which the speaker says he would not do [64:01]; hard-connecting stiff stairs so they act as unintended non-ductile braces and then fail [67:37][68:08]; and taking maximum modal drift at one floor minus another in a modal response spectrum analysis, which is invalid because signs are lost and drift must be tracked mode by mode [46:39][47:09].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Several choices in this session move cost. Modal response spectrum analysis tends to produce far less overturning than the equivalent lateral force procedure, which can yield real economy on overturning-resisting elements [46:39][47:09]. Heavy cladding tends to be seismic-governed and light cladding wind-governed, so the controlling load case and therefore the steel weight shifts by cladding type regardless of what the bare base shear suggests [74:48][75:18]. Seismic systems carry top-heavy overturning and omega-amplified collector and column forces, so even when wind and seismic base shears look similar the seismic overturning demand can drive heavier members [75:48]. Higher risk category means lower drift limits and higher importance factor, which stiffens and therefore adds tonnage to the lateral system [19:33].
