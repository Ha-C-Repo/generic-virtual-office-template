## Introduction and History of AASHTO LRFD Steel Bridge Design
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5g7ul4CVhxA | Duration: 1:35:00 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This is session 2 of an 8-part AISC Night School introductory course on steel bridge design, presented by Michael Grubb [00:00]. It traces the history and evolution of the AASHTO bridge design specifications from the 1931 first-edition Standard Specifications for Highway Bridges through the present-day AASHTO LRFD Bridge Design Specifications, and walks the three design philosophies in sequence: allowable stress design (ASD), load factor design (LFD), and load and resistance factor design (LRFD) [02:03]. The controlling theme is that LRFD, adopted by AASHTO in 1993 and first published in 1994, is the first calibrated probability-based bridge specification in the US, using a statistical calibration to a target reliability index rather than judgment-based safety factors [52:34][53:39]. This is bridge design under AASHTO, a different code family from the AISC 360 building code Your Company works under, so its direct application to the shop is limited.

### Spec References
The talk cites bridge codes, not building codes. Named references: AASHTO Standard Specifications for Highway Bridges (1st ed 1931, 17th and final ed 2002, sunsetted Oct 1 2007) [05:35][32:27]; AASHTO LRFD Bridge Design Specifications (1st ed 1994, 7th ed 2014, 8th ed due 2017) [25:39][30:51]; AASHTO/AWS D1.5 Bridge Welding Code, which now houses the Fracture Control Plan [18:27]; ASTM A709 and the parallel AASHTO M270 material standard for bridge steels [38:07]; the LRFD resistance factors specified in Article 6.5.4.2 [65:05]; Appendix C2 to Section 6 flowcharts [70:44]; NCHRP Report 368 (calibration) and NCHRP Report/synthesis series, plus NCHRP projects 12-26, 12-33, 12-52, 20-7 task 31 [59:24][62:30]; AISI Bulletin 15 (1969 LFD criteria) [16:23]. The single explicit AISC tie is the note that AASHTO resistance factors were generally set 0.05 higher than the corresponding AISC values [65:05].

### Technical Details
Member behavior is organized by limit state and is broadly consistent in form with AISC 360 building provisions. Flexural sections are classified by web slenderness as compact-web, noncompact-web, or slender-web, using the same limiting lambda relations; AASHTO calls them lambda with Dcp/Dc while AISC uses lambda_p/lambda_r with Hcp/Hc [67:09][68:09]. The speaker stresses that a rolled beam "does not know whether it is in a building or a bridge," so the underlying equations match and only notation and organization differ [69:42][70:13]. Bridges skew toward slender-web composite plate girders, singly symmetric and hybrid sections, and moving-load fatigue, whereas buildings favor compact rolled beams [66:07][66:38]. The four LRFD limit states are service, fatigue and fracture, strength, and extreme event [75:23]. Composite action uses shear-stud connectors to prevent slip and force linear strain through the depth [89:16]. Service-level steel stress limits of 0.95Fy composite and 0.80Fy noncomposite trace to the AASHO Road Test [11:46][63:31].

### Fabrication Notes
Fabrication content is bridge-specific and light. The Fracture Control Plan, adopted 1978 after the 1967 Silver Bridge collapse, governs fracture-critical (nonredundant) members or FCMs and sets controls on material properties, initial flaw sizes, fabricator and NDE personnel qualification and certification, welding procedures, welding repair procedures, and required fracture toughness of steel and weld metal, all now inside AASHTO/AWS D1.5 [17:56][18:27]. Fracture toughness is verified by the Charpy V-notch impact test, reported in foot-pounds [79:30]. Curved-girder fabrication carries added labor and material cost to curve the flanges and fabricate the girders [86:12]. No bolt grades, preheat tables, or fit-up tolerances for building steel are given.

### Erection Notes
Erection notes are general and bridge-oriented. The speaker observes there have been more sudden bridge collapses from stability failures during construction, and from scour, than from fracture [19:00]. Curved-girder erection typically needs more temporary supports and bracing, and incurs greater handling, shipping, and erection cost than straight girders [86:12]. Skewed bridges drive large differential deflections between girders, forcing the girders to twist to stay rotationally continuous with the stiff truss-like cross frames, which complicates cross-frame detailing and field fit-up to reach the target geometry [83:38][84:08]. Narrow units of three or fewer girders are checked for global lateral-torsional buckling of the whole system in the noncomposite deck-placement condition [82:35].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
No shop rejection list or weld-defect catalog is given. The failure modes named are at the structure level: brittle fracture (the Silver Bridge, 1967) that prompted the Fracture Control Plan [17:56], construction-stage stability failures, and scour-induced collapse [19:00]. On the detailing side, distortion-induced fatigue is called out as a common problem: out-of-plane secondary stresses in small gaps at welded details when connection plates supporting transverse members are not positively attached to the flanges. The LRFD code addresses it through prescribed detailing (positive attachment of connection plates to flanges) rather than a calculation [78:29][79:00].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The cost commentary is about bridge type, not shop pricing. Curved girders add cost across the board: extra labor and material to curve flanges and fabricate girders, higher handling, shipping, and erection cost, design time slightly above straight girders, and more temporary supports during erection [86:12]. The historical thread that span-to-depth ratios were relaxed (to 34 for the steel section) once composite multi-stringer framing proved economical shows code change tracking fabrication economy [08:09]. The fabricator-side aside that fabricators would prefer ASTM material specs to govern, since AASHTO M-specs lag ASTM, is a real procurement friction point [90:17].
