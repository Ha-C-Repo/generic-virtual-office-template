## New Developments in Connection Design
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wUvOCLDJHQ8 | Duration: 1:28:35 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This webinar, presented by Bo Dowswell (PhD, PE, principal at ARC International, an AISC connection-design researcher), covers the revised design procedure for coped beams that moved from the 14th to the 15th edition AISC Steel Construction Manual [02:21][11:40]. The core change is the shift from an elastic allowable-stress-design approach to a strength-based procedure that lets the engineer take credit for the inelastic or potentially plastic strength of the cope, and removes the old limits on cope geometry [11:40][80:33]. A cope is the notch cut in a beam flange and web so the beam can frame to a supporting member, single cope when only the top flange is cut, double cope when top and bottom flanges are cut for equal-nominal-depth framing [03:25][04:26]. The talk treats shear, flexural, axial, and combined-load strength for both single and double copes, with single-cope flexure modeled as local buckling (a short WT cantilever) and double-cope flexure modeled as lateral-torsional buckling [42:20][80:33].

### Spec References
Cited verbatim by the speaker: AISC 360 Section J4.2 for shear yielding of connection elements [16:46]; Chapter F three-part local-buckling curve for WT members and the lambda_p / lambda_r slenderness transition [20:25][21:29]; Section J4.1 for tension yielding of the cope, designed as a tension member [43:53][75:53]; Section E7 for local buckling and effective area A_e of slender compression members [44:54]; Section D7 used to derive the simplified axial-strength equation [46:57]; Section F11 (added between the green book and the 2016 spec) for rectangular cantilever-beam flexural buckling, used for double copes [55:36][56:38]; Section J4.4 for flexural (column-type) buckling of double copes, with KL/r <= 25 allowing tension yield without reduction and KL/r > 25 sending you to Chapter E [76:23][77:27]. The buckling adjustment factor f carries over unchanged from the 14th edition Manual [28:50]. Source material is the Cheng, Yura and Johnson report from University of Texas (Ferguson Lab) and an AISC Engineering Journal paper "Plastic Strength of Rectangular Connection Elements" [08:31][26:10][83:31]. A known errata: the Manual C_B range should read greater-than-or-equal-to 1.84, not less-than-or-equal-to [64:36].

### Technical Details
Shear strength uses the 0.6 von Mises factor times F_y times gross shear area A_gv = H_o times t_w, where H_o is the remaining depth at the cope [16:46][17:19]. Single-cope flexure is a local-buckling problem on a short WT-like cantilever of length e (eccentricity from supporting-member face to cope face), with critical stress from classical plate buckling (Timoshenko / SSRC) using a plate coefficient that is theoretically 0.61 but corrected by the empirical adjustment factor f and a K_1 cope-length term [18:53][27:17][31:24]. Tom Murray's simplification set lambda_p = lambda_r / 2 from the experimental data so lambda_r need not be computed [23:33][24:36]. Axial strength uses an effective area A_e per E7 with its own K_a coefficient (1.28 for long copes where C/H_o >= 1.64, higher for shorter copes) [44:54][47:28][48:05]. Double-cope flexure goes to F11 after computing L_b and C_b from Manual equations developed by Dowswell and Robert White via finite-element models, where L_b is simply the average of top and bottom cope lengths (an estimate originally suggested by Professor Yura) [56:38][66:08][68:42]. Buckle shapes confirm the bottom cope barely affects strength [59:47].

### Fabrication Notes
The cope itself is the controlling shop-cut feature; longer and deeper copes than the standard cope are common in practice, driven by skewed framing (a 45-degree skew increased one cope length about 40 percent) and equal-nominal-depth double-cope conditions [04:57][05:28][06:29]. Dowswell flags that fabrication and handling can introduce the geometric imperfections the design curve cannot credit: welding distortion, getting bumped in shipping, or roughly a sixteenth of an inch of out-of-flatness from cutting the cope itself [73:52][74:22]. Because the test specimens used to set the curve did not have measured imperfections (out-of-flatness, initial twist, residual stresses), the Manual curve is held conservative rather than raised [73:21][73:52]. A galvanized double-cope example is shown, so coping interacts with galvanizing [04:26].

### Erection Notes
The presentation is connection-strength focused and does not cover crane, rigging, or sequencing. The one erection-adjacent caution is lateral-torsional buckling of the overall beam: with the long copes now permitted, the end connections will not restrain the beam the way the pinned-end assumption implies, so an unbraced (or not-yet-braced) coped beam has greatly reduced LTB strength and the engineer of record must check it separately from the connection [08:00][80:33][81:04]. This matters during erection before decking or bracing is in place.

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The talk is design-method rather than inspection-failure oriented, so no specific weld rejection or bolt-inspection failures are named. The governing failure modes discussed are cope buckling: local buckling of the single-cope web in compression, lateral-torsional buckling of the double cope, and shear yielding along the cope plane, with most real copes failing inelastically or elastically rather than reaching the plastic moment because H_o is too deep [05:28][07:30][52:01]. The combined-load checks show long copes fail by axial-flexure interaction and short copes by axial-shear interaction [49:13][50:19].

### Bid / Cost Implications
No dollar figures or schedule estimates appear. The practical takeaway with cost effect is that the 15th-edition strength procedure recovers inelastic capacity and removes the old cope-geometry limits (single cope previously limited to half member depth and twice member depth in length; double cope to 20 percent depth), which can qualify deeper or longer copes that the elastic method would have rejected, reducing the need for reinforcement on skewed and equal-depth framing [13:42][14:12][80:33]. The judgment-heavy axial and combined-load checks mean coped-beam connections on heavy or skewed members carry engineering effort that should be priced into connection-design scope [10:02][49:13].
