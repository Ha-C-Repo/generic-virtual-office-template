## Let's Talk Seismic -- In Language We Can All Understand
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zoHqvcN7osA | Duration: 1:00:00 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
This is a public-communication and risk-literacy talk by structural PE Brent Maxfield, not a steel detailing or connection-design session. The thesis is that earthquake magnitude alone tells you almost nothing about damage; what matters is the shaking intensity at a given site, which Maxfield calls "the shake in the quake" [03:41][12:40]. He argues engineers should stop saying "MCE earthquake" and instead say "MCE shaking intensity / response acceleration / ground motion" so owners and the public understand what code design actually buys [32:58]. The driving message for any builder or owner is that code-conforming buildings are life-safety designs that can be heavily damaged or made disposable after a major event, not earthquake-proof [31:22][50:35].

### Spec References
No AISC 360, AISC 341, AWS D1.1, RCSC, or Code of Standard Practice sections are cited; this webinar contains no steel-specific provisions. The standards and tools named are the 2000 IBC (origin of the MCE concept) [31:56], the risk-targeted MCER defined as a 1-in-5,000-per-year collapse risk with shaking having a 2 percent probability of exceedance in 50 years and a target of no more than 10 percent probability of collapse at MCER for a typical Risk Category II building [35:09][36:12], the USGS Modified Mercalli intensity scale 1 to 10 and ShakeMap product [12:40][13:13], the 84th-percentile (one standard deviation above median) collapse-prevention basis [29:49], FEMA P-58 with SP3 software for performance-based loss and recovery estimation [49:00], and the USRC building rating system (certified / silver / gold / platinum) tied to two-thirds MCER design-level shaking [48:30]. FEMA "functional recovery" is named as the coming code direction [55:13].

### Technical Details
Maxfield separates magnitude (affects rupture area and shaking duration) from site shaking intensity (acceleration, sometimes velocity, depending on structure period) [06:27][19:23]. He uses a percentile/natural-variation model (4th, 50th, 84th, 98th percentile shaking) to show that one fault rupture cannot be pinned to a single shaking level; distance to fault, fault type (strike-slip versus normal), hanging-wall versus footwall position, soil profile softness, and depth to rock shift the predicted range [26:37][56:47]. Two-thirds MCER is the "design" level event; MCER is roughly a 50 percent larger ground motion than the design level, which is why an essential building tuned to be operational at two-thirds MCER may not be operational at full MCER [42:22][43:53].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
No connection rejections or inspection failures are discussed because the talk is non-technical. The "errors" Maxfield names are communication failures: engineers letting owners believe code buildings are earthquake-proof, conflating magnitude with shaking, and not disclosing that cladding-failure checks are not required at MCER in the current code [40:47][45:57]. He flags that aftershocks can collapse buildings that survived the main event and that MCER-level buildings are often demolished afterward for economic reasons [40:47][41:18].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The cost framing is owner-facing risk versus cost: code sets the MCER level as a balance, designing to a higher percentile (toward 98th / "bulletproof") is uneconomical, and resilience or functional-recovery design above code adds cost, sometimes on the order of about half a percent of building cost per the FEMA P-58 / SP3 discussion, in exchange for less damage and faster recovery [29:19][34:06][49:33]. None of this changes steel takeoff quantities, AISC weights, or unit rates; it is a basis-of-design and owner-expectation conversation, not a pricing input.
