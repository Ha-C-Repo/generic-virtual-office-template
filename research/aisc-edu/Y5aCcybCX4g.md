## Basic Introduction to Nonlinear Analysis

### Core Concept
This webinar teaches how a structural analysis program actually computes nonlinear behavior, with the standing reminder that "the function of a structural engineer is to design not to analyze" and that analysis is a means to an end [00:01]. The speaker walks the audience through the direct stiffness method, the basis of every commercial frame program and of the finite element method, where the computer enforces equilibrium and compatibility at each degree of freedom and uses a force-displacement relationship F = K Delta as the translator between forces and displacements [16:30, 17:02]. He stresses that the only way these programs model real behavior is by modifying member stiffness, so material nonlinearity, second-order effects, and buckling are all just stiffness modifications [18:03, 88:38]. The lecture builds from material nonlinear analysis (plastic hinges) to geometric nonlinear analysis (P-Delta) to combined second-order inelastic analysis and elastic critical (buckling) load analysis. All these methods are described as approximate and a complement to, never a substitute for, good engineering [04:13].

### Spec References
The speaker cites the AISC steel specification Chapter C, which requires accounting for P-Delta (P-cap-Delta and P-small-Delta) second-order effects [01:35, 02:07], and Appendix 1, which permits inelastic analysis and design [02:07, 40:45, 41:15]. He references the direct analysis method as the future alternative to back-calculating K factors [78:54]. He names hand methods B1 and B2 moment amplification factors for second-order effects [03:10]. He mentions LRFD design reaching M-sub-p for a fully braced member [33:34]. No AISC 341 seismic section, AWS D1.1 clause, RCSC provision, table number, or numbered Design Guide is cited in this lecture; it is an analysis-theory webinar, not a connection or welding talk.

### Technical Details
The material model assumed throughout is elastic-perfectly-plastic: the program monitors member stress and, when it reaches yield (for example 50 KSI), switches the modulus of elasticity from 29,000 KSI to zero [26:45, 27:54]. A plastic hinge forms in axial members when P over the yield load Py equals one, and in flexure when the moment reaches the plastic moment M-sub-p (plastic section modulus times yield strength), with the ratio M over M-sub-p equal to one [29:29, 33:34, 35:06]. Combined axial force and bending are governed by a yield surface, where staying inside means elastic and reaching the surface forms a hinge; a section at the surface can shed bending moment to neighbors to take more axial force [46:25, 47:25]. Second-order stiffness equals first-order elastic stiffness modified by a geometric stiffness term that is negative for compression (the "backpacker" analogy) and positive for tension (the "guitar string" analogy) [61:18, 62:18, 62:50]. Buckling occurs when the stiffness matrix determinant goes to zero, solved as an eigenvalue problem where eigenvalues are load factors and eigenvectors are buckling modes [74:14, 80:58]. The worked example is a continuous W21x65, 24 ft and 12 ft spans, 100 Kip load, where moment redistribution let load grow about 16 to 17 percent past first yield before a three-hinge mechanism [37:09, 40:45].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The speaker flags analysis-tool errors rather than shop or field rejections. He warns that a program labeled "P-Delta" may capture only P-cap-Delta and miss P-small-Delta, so the user must confirm both are included [53:05]. He cautions that software must account for axial-force-bending interaction on the yield surface, since ignoring axial force in the W21x65 example overstated capacity by failing at 73 percent of load versus 100 percent [50:32, 51:02]. He warns against using a buckling analysis to back-calculate K factors and to be careful when the elastic critical load ratio approaches one [78:54, 79:25]. He also notes columns may need subdivision into two elements for adequate second-order and buckling accuracy [68:38, 81:29].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The cost theme is computational expense versus accuracy in choosing an analysis method. Concentrated-plasticity plastic-hinge models are cheaper and a good starting point, while distributed-plasticity (plastic zone) analysis is more accurate but computationally more expensive, with the gap between them shrinking as a structure becomes more redundant [35:38, 36:09, 36:39]. Full 3D brick-element finite element analysis is described as the "full enchilada," appropriate for component study but beyond what basic frame analysis needs [36:39, 37:09]. The practical bid-side lesson is that inelastic analysis can justify more capacity from the same steel (the example carried roughly 16 percent more load past first yield), but second-order effects cut the other way and reduced the same beam to about 67 percent of its first-order capacity [40:45, 70:10].
