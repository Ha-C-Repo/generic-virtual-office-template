## Stability Design - Advanced Applications

### Core Concept
This is session seven of an AISC Night School course on stability design taught by Dr. Donald W. White of Georgia Tech, focused on advanced application of the Direct Analysis Method (DM) from the AISC specification [02:35][03:05]. The DM models reduced stiffness (0.8 times tau) and explicit geometric imperfections so member checks can be done at the cross-section level without computing effective length (K) factors [05:41][16:00]. The session works four problem types: in-plane stability of a steel arch, erection stability of a long-span roof truss, stability bracing assessment (relative/panel, nodal, lean-on), and a rigorous inelastic buckling analysis that the speaker frames as a refined effective length method [03:05][03:36]. The controlling lesson for a fabricator is that stability-critical structures do not scale linearly: an arch at a 0.49 unity check reached 1.0 at only 20 percent more load [14:58].

### Spec References
The speaker cites these by name: AISC Design Guide 28 (stability bracing examples, including Appendix E and the lean-on example) [04:39][54:50]; AISC Appendix 6 column bracing provisions, with relative bracing being renamed panel bracing in the 2016 draft [31:53][34:28]; Appendix 6 equations A-6-1 (panel bracing strength, 0.4 percent of axial force) and A-6-2 (required panel bracing stiffness) [44:54][48:36]; Appendix 1 sections 1.1 and 1.2 for second-order analysis, referenced by Section A-6-1 of AISC 2016 (ballot 3 under public review) [36:30]; the AISC Code of Standard Practice out-of-plumb tolerance of 1 in 500 and out-of-straightness 1 in 1000 [20:43][49:37]; the AISC column strength curve equations and the 0.877 / phi=0.9 inelastic factors and tau-a derivation [80:39][82:10]. No AISC 341 seismic, AWS D1.1, or RCSC bolting clauses were cited; this is an analysis-method webinar.

### Technical Details
The arch is a two-hinge parabolic rib, 350 ft span, 80 ft rise, steel box section (50 by 1 inch webs, 20 by 2 inch flanges, Grade 50), checked at factored load Wu = 8.6 kip/ft with a 5 inch (span/1000) imperfection scaled to the buckling mode [04:39][06:43]. The example truss is a 120 ft simple-span roof truss, 11 ft deep, W12x65 top chord, W12x53 bottom chord, W12x45 web members, with chord splices near midspan and self-weight increased 20 percent for miscellaneous steel [16:30][17:34]. Modeling web connections as fully restrained (FR) gave a buckling ratio of 6.56 versus 1.3 if pinned, so the gusset-to-flange detail justified the continuous assumption [22:50][24:55]. The lean-on example ties four-story exterior W14x257 columns to an interior moment-frame core; floor beams were upsized from W18x40 to W21x44 and finally W30x90 to provide the required 56.5 kip/inch bracing stiffness [55:22][68:39]. All analyses ran in the MASTAN-style second-order/buckling tool the speaker calls "mast 10" [07:15].

### Fabrication Notes
The session is analysis, not shop process, so fabrication tolerance enters only as imperfection input. The arch construction tolerance was taken as span/1000 (5 inches), and member out-of-straightness as L/1000 per the Code of Standard Practice [06:43][20:43]. The chord splices near truss midspan are flagged as a fabrication and detailing decision: if they are temporary erection splices ahead of a final field splice, the temporary splice must be sized for the erection-stage forces from the analysis [18:35][19:39]. The 20 percent self-weight add for connection and miscellaneous steel is the kind of takeoff allowance an estimator carries before connection design exists [17:34].

### Erection Notes
Topic 7.2 is explicitly an erection-engineer problem: stability during lifting is usually not the driver, but stability after releasing the crane is [17:34][18:05]. The questions posed are whether the truss can be connected and the crane released, what wind magnitude causes failure, what temporary bracing (deadmen and cables to midspan) is needed, and what temporary splice forces result [18:05][18:35]. For erection imperfection the chords are swept L/1000 out of plane, and sweeping top and bottom chords in opposite directions (a built-in twist) is the conservative worst case for letting the truss off the hook [20:43][21:45]. For this truss the bottom chord unity check at 1.4 times self weight was only 0.07, so releasing the crane under self weight alone was fine, but wind still had to be checked separately [25:56][26:27].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
No bolting or welding inspection failures are named; the errors discussed are analysis and assumption errors. The central one is treating a stability-critical structure as if unity checks scale linearly with load, which the arch disproves (0.49 to 1.0 at only +20 percent) [14:58]. A second is mis-idealizing truss web connections as pins when the flange-to-flange gusset detail behaves as FR, which would wrongly fail a stable truss [22:50][24:25]. A third is using too few elements per member in second-order analysis: two elements per unbraced length on the exterior lean-on columns, three if the program has only P-large-delta capability, versus one for the lightly loaded interior columns [76:25][77:26]. A fourth is choosing an imperfection pattern that matches a buckling mode with no brace displacement, which understates the brace demand [93:34].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The bracing economy theme is the cost message: Appendix 6 hand equations are conservative starting points, and DM or inelastic buckling analysis can reduce brace stiffness and member sizes for partial nodal bracing, unequal brace spacing or stiffness, flexible braces, and tapered members [53:48][54:20]. The base brace strength requirement is 0.4 percent of column axial force at the minimum (2 times ideal) stiffness, dropping toward the 0.2 percent floor as brace stiffness increases, so over-stiffening braces lowers required brace force [48:06][50:41]. The lean-on example shows engineering effort trading against steel weight: beam upsizing from W18x40 through W21x44 to W30x90 was driven entirely by bracing stiffness, a real fabrication cost decided by analysis rigor [56:25][69:42].
