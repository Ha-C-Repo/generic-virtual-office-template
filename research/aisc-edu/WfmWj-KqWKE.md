## Steel Specifications Unraveled
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WfmWj-KqWKE | Duration: 59:07 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This is a one-hour AIA/PDH webinar by John Baier, principal at SMBH Inc. (Columbus, OH), on how a structural engineer edits and structures the steel project specification so it agrees with the general notes and the drawings [00:30][03:32]. The thesis is the three-part CSI MasterFormat section structure (Part 1 General, Part 2 Products, Part 3 Execution) applied to Division 5 metals, the goal being a short, coordinated, internally consistent spec where any single piece of information lives in only one place to avoid conflicting data [13:39][09:36]. It is a specification-writing and coordination talk, not a connection-design or welding-procedure talk.

### Spec References
Cited by name: CSI MasterFormat with 50 divisions, Division 5 metals (with Division 3 concrete, 6 wood, 9 painting referenced for coordination) [08:04][08:36]; section number 05 12 00 ("oh five one two zero zero") for structural steel [60s, 28:50]; AISC 341 referenced on seismic jobs [19:17]; the AISC Code of Standard Practice, specifically Chapter 10 for AESS categories now matching IBC 2016's reference [47:49][48:21][49:10]; SDI specification for steel deck and SJI specification for joists [19:47][36:02]; IBC Table 1604.3 for deflection criteria [37:06]. Material/product references: ASTM A1085 50 ksi HSS [33:57], A325 bolts [21:18], A490 for tension-control bolts [34:28], A307 bolts for shear and joist-to-column [34:59]. AISC fabricator certification and the IAS accreditation alternative [26:44]. SP2/SP3 surface-prep cleaning levels and galvanizing G60/G90 coatings [31:25][41:40].

### Technical Details
The talk covers material grade selection in Part 2 including the A1085 50 ksi HSS chicken-and-egg between engineers and producers [33:57], bolt-grade retention (keep A325/A490/A307 as the job actually uses, delete the rest), and the three tension-control installation options the spec can leave open to the fabricator: turn-of-nut, tension-control spline bolts, and direct tension indicators [34:28][35:29]. ASD versus LRFD must be stated so reported reactions and loads are unambiguous [20:17][36:32]. Joist topics include SJI camber (no camber, per-SJI camber, or engineer-specified, with a span/camber table) and the masonry-wall-adjacent deflection concern, plus bridging now left to the manufacturer rather than drawn row-by-row [38:36][40:07]. Deck topics include profile, depth, gauge, single/double/triple span, and the unshored-span trap where omitting yield strength (40 ksi versus a manufacturer's higher value) changes allowable unshored span from 8'-9" to 9'-9" on the same 2-inch form deck at a 5.5-inch slab, 20 gauge [43:44][44:48].

### Fabrication Notes
Quality assurance hinges on requiring an AISC-certified fabricator (or IAS-accredited alternative); if certification is not required, the engineer must add Part 2 source quality control because certification is what removes the need for an inspector to enter the shop and inspect welds [26:44][27:47]. For non-certified small or rural shops, the fallback is qualifying language such as ten years of successful experience on comparable projects [27:15]. Galvanizing requires specifying no quench for steel that will later be painted, since the water bath leaves impurities that defeat primer adhesion [31:55][32:25]. Relief holes in galvanized closed shapes must have a named party responsible for plugging, because trapped water that freezes can split the member, a quarter-million-dollar exposure on a big job if left as a change order [32:55][33:26]. Acoustic deck batts on cellular deck must be factory-installed [45:18].

### Erection Notes
Part 3 Execution governs the field: erection requirements, whether certified erectors are required, and field quality control [22:21]. Most owners now hire the testing agency rather than the contractor, driven by the special-inspections regime, and the spec must state this so the contractor does not carry it in the bid while still requiring the contractor to coordinate timely inspector access [22:52][23:23]. Field protection and repair of shop-primed steel is addressed, and tension-control connections with turn-of-nut require getting the inspector on site at the right moment to inspect [23:53][24:24]. A movement noted over the prior decade is leaving steel unprimed (plain) during erection where it is concealed in the final product [30:55].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The recurring failure mode is conflicting information held in two places (spec versus general notes versus drawings), which the talk addresses with a "strictest provision shall govern" clause naming all three documents [09:36][56:25]. Concrete pitfalls named: speccing deck without a yield strength and getting an unexpectedly short unshored span [44:48]; failing to assign relief-hole plugging [33:26]; quenching galvanized steel that must later be primed [32:25]; speccing double-span deck on a single-span canopy condition [42:44]; and unintentionally adding cost by requiring primed or painted roof deck that the project does not need [07:34]. The talk is about spec-drafting errors, not weld/bolt rejection statistics.

### Bid / Cost Implications
Cost discipline is a theme: on large warehouse jobs with acres of deck and joists, specifying unneeded primer or paint on roof deck adds real per-square-foot cost the developer scrutinizes [07:03][07:34]. G90 galvanizing versus standard G60 is usually a custom order that adds schedule time [41:40]. A spec that omits certification or testing requirements distorts a public bid because some bidders carry those costs and others do not, so a complete spec keeps the bidding field level [52:12]. Leaving the three tension-control methods open lets the fabricator pick the cheapest installation [35:29], and letting the joist manufacturer set bridging lets them compete on analysis and erection time [40:07].
