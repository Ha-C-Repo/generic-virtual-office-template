## Solutions for Vibration Issues-Evaluation and Retrofits
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2ayxtd9FjzU | Duration: 33:30 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Dr. Tom Murray (first author of AISC Design Guide 11) and Dr. Brad Davis teach how to evaluate and retrofit floors that occupants report as lively, plus floors whose human-induced vibration exceeds the tolerance of sensitive equipment [02:36][03:07]. The controlling problem is low-frequency floors, defined as floors whose first natural frequency is below about nine Hertz, where occupant footfall at a sub-harmonic of the floor frequency drives resonance and the perceived response [08:51]. The whole method compares a measured equivalent sinusoidal peak acceleration (ESPA) against the Design Guide 11 acceleration limit, and retrofits the floor when that limit is exceeded [14:59][15:30].
### Spec References
AISC Design Guide 11, second edition, published roughly two years before the talk, is the central reference, including its manual method for regular (rectangular) bays, its finite-element method for irregular bays, and its tolerance limits in "Chapter two" [04:41][05:13][05:44][31:36]. SJI Technical Digest Number 5 is cited as the alternate manual method for joist floors [04:41]. Dunkerley's equation is named as the Design Guide 11 way to combine the joist-mode and girder-mode frequencies into a system frequency [24:51]. No AISC 360, AISC 341, AWS D1.1, or RCSC sections were cited; this is a serviceability talk, not a strength or connection-code talk.
### Technical Details
Floor natural frequency is measured fastest by a heel drop (rise on the balls of the feet, strike with the heels), which injects content to about 40 Hertz and is processed by FFT into a response spectrum to read the dominant frequency [06:46][07:16]. Walking tests use a metronome at a step frequency in the normal range of 1.6 to 2.2 Hertz, chosen by dividing the floor's natural frequency by an integer so resonance occurs; for a 5.06 Hertz floor, divide by three to get 1.68 Hertz, about 101 steps per minute [09:52][17:03]. Records are bandpass filtered between roughly 1 and 15 to 20 Hertz, since humans are insensitive below 1 Hertz and above about 15 Hertz, and 30 or 60 Hertz building-equipment noise must be filtered out [13:55][14:25]. ESPA is computed as the maximum one-second rolling RMS acceleration multiplied by the square root of 2 to recover an equivalent sinusoidal peak, compared against the 0.5 percent of gravity quiet-environment limit [15:30][16:33][17:36].
### Fabrication Notes
The shop fabrication relevant to a retrofit is straightforward member supply, since the cited fix welds a wide-flange (a W10x15, 20 feet long in the example) to the bottom chords of composite joists at the panel points only [27:29][28:32][29:03]. Whatever is added must be very stiff because the target is reducing midspan motion from about ten thousandths of an inch to five thousandths, so bolted or turnbuckle-tensioned schemes were tried over the years and rejected as not stiff enough; welding is required [19:10][19:41]. The reinforcing member must also be strained into the structure, which means the floor is jacked up about a quarter to a half inch before the reinforcement is welded so the new steel picks up load [19:41].
### Erection Notes
Field retrofit work is done from below in an occupied building, requiring removal of the ceiling, jacking a set of three or four joists at a time, welding the beams to the bottom chords, then moving across the whole bay rather than only the locally complained area [29:03]. The tenant directly below had to be relocated to a temporary space because hot welding work over a computer-filled occupied floor is risky and usually not feasible [20:12][28:32]. An alternative field fix is installing posts (props) below the lively floor, but they must bear to grade; one job doubled the problem by propping between two suspended floors over a parking garage and the posts were pulled the next day [21:16][21:46].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The talk names several questionable testing and evaluation errors. Reporting peak particle velocity or peak particle acceleration (PPV or PPA) from a record badly overstates what people feel, because a single filtered peak (0.875 percent G in the example) is not representative while the persistent ESPA (0.531 percent G) is [30:36][31:06]. Long-term monitoring without paired video monitoring leaves the cause of each vibration event unknown, and even video can miss excitations like a person's leg jiggling [31:36][32:06]. Using cell-phone accelerometers fails because vibration measurement needs tight control of sampling frequency, input voltage range, and bandwidth that consumer hardware does not provide [32:37]. A field error shown was placing nine one-foot concrete cubes as added mass, which changed the predicted frequency by one hundredth of a percent and was a waste of money [24:21][25:53].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Adding mass is usually ineffective because it takes a large amount of concrete to move the response, and added slab helps only when it also raises moment of inertia and transverse stiffness, so block mass buys little [20:44]. The welded-stiffener retrofit is expensive and disruptive: it requires tenant relocation, ceiling removal, jacking, and field welding in an occupied space, which is the cost driver, not the steel weight itself [20:12][28:32]. The practical lesson for pricing serviceability work is that a cheap-looking fix (mass blocks, props) can fail or backfire, and the defensible fix is engineered stiffening plus measurement, which carries real labor and access cost.
