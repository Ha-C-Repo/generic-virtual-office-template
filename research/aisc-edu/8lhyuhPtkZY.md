## Erection Engineering: The Science Behind the Art
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8lhyuhPtkZY | Duration: 1:25:51 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This webinar by Will Jacobs and Clint Rex of Stanley D. Lindsey and Associates covers erection engineering, the supplemental engineering that keeps a steel structure stable in each intermediate stage of construction before it reaches its completed, contract-document form [02:36][03:39]. The premise is the standard structural-drawing note: the structure is stable only in its completed form, and temporary supports for all intermediate stages are the contractor's responsibility, which legally falls to the steel erector [03:08][04:09]. The talk argues that as building geometries get more complex and slender, with more long-span trusses and transfer elements, erector experience needs engineering help to confirm how many braces are needed and whether a member can be picked safely [04:41][57:38].

### Technical Details
Construction wind is the controlling load surprise: ASCE 37 gives a 25 percent velocity reduction for a construction period under six weeks (0.75 factor), but since pressure scales with velocity squared the force factor is about 0.50, then lattice drag factors up to 2.0 push temporary pressures back above the permanent case [16:53][17:55][18:26]. Running the AISC 14th Edition example building as a structure under construction took base shear from about 170 kips to about 600 kips, roughly three times the permanent wind [19:29]. ASCE 37 sets a 25 psf pre-composite construction live load for concrete placement by hose and its own load combinations with reduced factors such as 0.5 on construction live load [16:23][58:38]. Truss stability uses a smeared wL-squared/8 over depth to get chord force, then treats the compression chord as a standalone column swept L/1000, checked with B1 amplification; KL/r values of 272 and even 590 appear in real trusses and are tolerable because final bracing follows [33:56][41:42][55:34]. Whether truss web members are modeled pinned or fixed to the chords dramatically shifts buckling load (about 1.25 self-weight pinned versus 5.5 fixed), so the engineer scans web end moments (about 2.2 kip-ft at LF 1.4) to justify the assumption [50:22][52:28].
### Fabrication Notes
The speakers stress that temporary steel is hard to fabricate ahead of time so it lays out exactly as planned, which makes field-applied web stiffeners on cribbing beams cumbersome [24:36][25:07]. Instead of shop stiffeners they use the British-standard fake-length method, adding two times beam depth to the unbraced length to capture end distortion in lateral-torsional buckling [25:39][26:41]. The fabricator supplies the real truss weight including connections; comparing it to the bare analytical weight gives a connection factor, typically 1.1 to 1.25, below 1.0 for a fully welded tube truss and up to about 1.5 for a heavy bolted truss [33:56][40:09][41:12]. Field weld holes and bolt holes do not always land where needed, which is why industrial clamps with defined capacities are preferred for restraining temporary beams against roll-over [27:12][28:16].
### Erection Notes
Erectors care most about whether a freestanding truss can be released from the crane and for how long before bracing, which drives the wind question [31:24][32:24]. Long trusses arrive in two or three pieces with field splices that were sized for the final building, not the erection condition, so temporary loads on those splices must be checked; field CJP welds often get a temporary bolted connection so the crew can move on while welders finish [32:24][32:54]. On-hook stability for very slender 226-foot barrel trusses required an eigenvalue buckling analysis to seed a direct-analysis initial shape, weak springs at nodes to prevent numerical instability, and iterating support points until reaching about a 1.4 safety factor, ending in two cranes and four pick points [61:11][66:48][71:26]. The cautionary field story: a truss shipped on its side was picked at midspan, lifted 6 then 12 feet while the ends stayed grounded, and finished with a 7-foot kink matching the predicted buckled shape; the fix was heat straightening and a revised pre-compressed-arch rotation method [72:56][74:58][75:28].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
A box pedestrian truss (Pratt/Vierendeel-style, 14x38 chord turned strong-axis-vertical so weak axis resisted buckling, KL/ry 590) buckled with a kink at midspan as an experienced erector released it from temporary bolted supports; slings still attached caught it, no injuries, and it had to be heat straightened with bolts replaced [54:02][56:37]. The named failure modes are chord buckling from underestimated slenderness, ignored strong-axis and weak-axis moments in Vierendeel chords, and the unanalyzed on-its-side lift kink [55:34][56:05][74:28]. The recurring lesson is that stability problems are not visible to the naked eye on slender structures even to seasoned erectors [57:07].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The speakers note erection engineering pays better and carries liability that ends at job close, unlike some architect work [58:08]. Cost-relevant rules of thumb: assume a truss weighs about 25 percent more than analytical bare weight, and if a member looks too slender to pick it probably is [84:19][84:49]. Reducing assumed wind velocity (for example using 30 mph, where the 25 plf top-chord wind drops to about 4 plf, since crews do not erect above 30 mph) can avoid costly temporary bracing if the erector and owner agree on an alternate scheme on standby [47:52][48:22][19:59]. Deliverable scope drives engineering cost, from a hand sketch to a one-sheeter to a full staged 3D BIM package (one job ran about 70 sheets) [76:31][78:35].
