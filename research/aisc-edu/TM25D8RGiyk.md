## Rules of Thumb for Steel Design
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TM25D8RGiyk | Duration: 43:54 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Dr Socrates Ioannides (Structural Affiliates, Nashville) presents a set of closed-form approximations for preliminary steel sizing so an engineer can answer depth, weight, column-size, and tonnage questions on the spot in a design meeting and sanity-check computer output [02:01][04:38]. The rules are explicitly Allowable Stress Design based, developed before LRFD, and use service (un-factored) loads directly because the allowable-stress safety margin is already baked into each constant [06:38][07:08]. They are approximations, not always conservative, and the speaker repeatedly says to verify with full analysis [06:38][10:15].
### Spec References
No code section, table, or Design Guide number is cited verbatim; these are general design rules of thumb, no AISC section cited. The talk names the steel-shape weight tables generically ("the AISC table" of economy sections, "the steel book") at [09:13][20:07] and references UL fire-rating assembly designations spoken aloud for composite floors: UL 902, UL 925, and UL D916 ("9259 16") depending on the spray-fireproofing supplier [35:17]. It also references LRFD versus ASD as design methods generally [06:38][17:29], not a clause.
### Technical Details
Key approximations, all in conventional units (moment in foot-kips, depth in inches, weight in pounds per foot): area of any shape = weight / 3.4 [08:11]; moment of inertia I = d squared times weight / 2 [08:42]; elastic section modulus = depth times weight / 10 [09:13]; radius of gyration rx is about 0.45d, ry is about 0.25 times flange width [10:15][10:46]. The flagship beam rule is weight = 5M/d for 36 ksi and 3.5M/d for 50 ksi (the "5M over d" story) [13:52][15:25]; composite beams use 70 to 75 percent of that [16:26]. Shear-stud count is about 1.1 times the weight in ASD or 1.25 times the lighter LRFD weight, using 3/4 inch studs, and more than three studs per square foot signals a design problem [16:58][17:59]. Truss-chord force is about 1.3M/d, chord weight about 2M/d, total truss about three times the chord weight [21:08][22:13]. Column allowable stress straight-line fit: Fa = 22 minus 0.1(KL/r) for 36 ksi, with a 0.15 factor for 50 ksi [23:17]. Depth rule: roof members about half the span (inches per foot of span), floor beams a bit more, roughly half an inch per foot [24:53]. High-rise floor weight estimate: psf = stories/3 + 7 [31:07].
### Fabrication Notes
The talk is preliminary design oriented and gives no shop fit-up, tolerance, or QC procedure. The only fabrication-adjacent items are the 3/4 inch shear-stud convention and the rule that more than three studs per square foot is a red flag [16:58], and the fillet-weld capacity check that the weld strength is a little less than 1 kip per sixteenth of an inch of weld size per inch of length [33:46]. No NDE, WPS, or weld-procedure content.
### Erection Notes
One erection-rate planning figure is given: a crew erects roughly 40 to 60 tons per week, derived as an average that depends on piece count, typical piece weight, and connection type [32:44]. Worked schedule example: a 12-story, 36,000 SF building at (12/3 + 7) psf comes to about 2,000 tons, with about 10 weeks mill/delivery lead and about 16 weeks to erect, totaling roughly six months [33:15]. No bracing, rigging, or sequencing detail beyond the rate.
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
No inspection-failure or rejection catalog is presented. The speaker flags design-judgment errors instead: shear-stud counts above three per square foot indicate a flawed composite design [16:58], and engineers commonly over-estimate simple-beam deflection because real bolted "simple" connections provide rotational restraint, so actual deflection is about 80 percent of the calculated value [34:16]. He also warns the rules go unconservative on the heaviest sections in each column group [10:15].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Several rules feed estimating directly: tonnage from psf = stories/3 + 7 gives a fast structural-weight order of magnitude for a high-rise [31:07]; the 40 to 60 ton/week erection rate plus mill lead time gives a schedule estimate [32:44][33:15]. Economy guidance: optimum bay is about 30 by 40 for beam-and-joist framing (beams short way, joists long way) and 40 by 40 for joist-girder/truss systems [26:56]; composite framing runs about one shape size lighter than non-composite [17:29]. The speaker notes mill/service-center delivery is no longer 8 to 10 weeks but more like 14 to 20 weeks [32:12].
