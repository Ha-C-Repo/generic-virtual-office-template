## Buyer Beware:  Choosin the Right Architecturally Exposed Structural Steel Category
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oPdPXEAxBkU | Duration: 1:00:17 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
This webinar by Jacinda Collins (AISC structural steel specialist) and David Weaver (Moltech Technologies, detailing) teaches the AESS category system introduced in the 2016 Code of Standard Practice and the cost that rides with each step up [00:00][01:01]. The pre-2016 world had only two buckets, standard steel and "AESS," so a small bathroom application and a glass-house atrium were specified identically and priced unpredictably [02:33][03:03]. The new system defines AESS 1, 2, 3, 4, and AESS-C custom, escalating workmanship and labor with each level, and the central message is that buyers should specify the lowest category that meets the visual intent rather than defaulting to the top [06:05][52:08]. A 10-step selection program (understand levels, decide if standard steel works, visual map, budget map, think like an ironworker, address connections, finalize finishes) frames the buyer decision [05:35].

### Spec References
2016 AISC Code of Standard Practice, Section 10 (the AESS provisions and category definitions) [01:01][06:35]. AESS categories 1, 2, 3, 4, and AESS-C custom [06:35]. Surface prep is cited by SSPC number: SP6 commercial blast clean (the default AESS surface prep), SP2 (a lesser hand-tool prep used when an intumescent primer governed), and SP10 near-white blast (more stringent than SP6, called for in wet marine environments) [08:06][38:24][40:25]. The 15th edition AISC Manual is referenced as the edition that carried the expanded AESS language [22:30]. No AISC 360, AISC 341, AWS D1.1 clause, or RCSC section was cited by number in this presentation.

### Technical Details
Category 1 (basic elements) exceeds standard workmanship: ground sharp edges, weld spatter removed, continuous weld appearance (welds not ground), and SP6 surface prep [07:36][08:06]. Category 2 (feature elements over 20 ft away) cuts standard fabrication tolerances in half, removes markings, and requires uniform smooth welds that are still not ground or contoured [09:07][09:37]. Category 3 (feature elements within 20 ft) requires a mock-up, removes all mill marks, grinds and fills button/plug welds, orients and hides HSS weld seams, abuts cross sections, and minimizes open-gap tolerances [10:38][11:08]. Category 4 (showcase elements) contours and blends welds, fills and sands surface imperfections, minimizes weld show-through, and grinds the HSS coil seam smooth so it disappears under paint [12:09][12:41]. AESS-C custom uses the matrix to tick individual requirements line by line, ideal for mixed materials (steel with reclaimed wood), weathering steel, or historic-repair blending [14:12][14:43].

### Fabrication Notes
The cost driver is labor per ton, not material: material is under one third of a complete package, more than two thirds is labor [27:38][28:10]. Weaver's 30-ft beam versus 3-ft beam example shows the same handling, cutting, drilling, inspecting, and loading hours apply regardless of length, so a short heavy member carries vastly higher hours per ton [26:37][27:08]. A full-size visual sample is not a clean 10 percent add because economy of scale is lost when one column must be fabricated ahead of the batch; with 10 identical columns the sample exceeds 10 percent, with 100 columns it approaches 1 percent [30:45][31:15]. Web/beam penetrations are a hidden labor and machine-time cost the fabricator must be asked about [25:33]. Curved-member tolerances could not be priced at all (light angle in-house versus heavy wide flange rolled out of state, easy-way versus hard-way HSS radius) and were left as red zeros in the matrix [32:16][32:46].

### Erection Notes
Field work is harder than shop work, so the standing advice is to do as much as possible in the shop and ship assemblies to the erector [45:32]. The firefighter memorial with over 50 columns used prefabricated modular pieces so the fabricator welded at ground level and the field crew only set two pieces together, avoiding holding a crane in place during column welding [45:32][46:03]. Use bolting instead of welding in the field where possible, because field welding at awkward angles forces preheat, equipment handling, and overhead grinding that drive labor up [46:33][45:02]. On the galvanized walkway truss example, trusses were shop-welded as assemblies and galvanized while the horizontal infill was field bolted, since the design team did not want to field weld on galvanizing [34:48][35:18].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The recurring error is specifying a higher AESS category than the viewing distance or budget warrants, and assuming exposed steel automatically equals AESS [06:05][41:25]. A real conflict shown was a galvanized member spec calling for an SP6 blast clean, which cannot be performed after galvanizing and is wasted money before galvanizing since the galvanizer blast-cleans anyway [36:19][36:50]. Another was an AESS SP6 requirement colliding with an intumescent-primer SP2 requirement on the same 300 tons, resolved only by two mock-ups plus cost and schedule comparison [38:24][38:54]. Assuming a condition is covered by the Code of Standard Practice when it is not is a frequent specification gap flagged by the Steel Solutions Center [50:07]. On inspection: the Code of Standard Practice itself imposes no special AESS inspection or certification, so any inspection must be explicitly called out by the architect or engineer [53:39][54:09].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Category ranges are increases over non-AESS, not cumulative across categories: category 2 lands roughly 25 to 35 percent over non-AESS, with individual line items carrying ranges (for example 5 to 25 percent for a visual sample) [29:12][31:45][66:05]. The matrix is explicitly a conceptual budgeting tool and must NOT be put in contract documents or held against the fabricator; include the AESS matrix but exclude the relative cost matrix [05:05][50:38]. Least weight does not equal least cost, because lighter steel can introduce more craftsmanship and labor [43:27]. The strongest cost lever is mapping category by location on a single member: Bill Zimmerman's line is "the money isn't in the middle of the beam, it's at the ends, in the connections," so keeping connections at a lower AESS while the column shaft runs higher saves real money [58:32][59:02]. Bring a fabricator on board as early as possible, and if difficult work comes back cheap, ask questions because an assumption is probably buried [51:08].
