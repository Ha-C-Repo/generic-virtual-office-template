### Core Concept

### Technical Details
Connection types discussed are double-angle clip angles (bolted-bolted, welded-bolted) and shear tabs, chosen by shop equipment and skill rather than by load alone [17:44][88:26]. Column splices shown are a full-penetration flange splice with a bolted web [23:42]. Systems covered include multi-story moment frames, trusses, plate girders (built-up, with SAW and flange butt splices), a football stadium, and a pedestrian bridge [38:50][20:35]. The model-based detailing workflow generates the Advanced Bill of Material (ABM) for mill ordering and also spits out filled bolt quantities and erector point-to-point lists [10:11][85:48]. Cambering is by mechanical hydraulic ram or by heat (vees applied to web and flange, with temperature-indicating crayons to avoid overheating), to offset dead-load deflection; tolerances per COSP 6.4.4 [63:08][82:43].

### Fabrication Notes
Material identification and traceability run from receiving to the point of assembly per AISC 360 2.1 and COSP 6.1 [47:31]. On receipt, a visual inspection per A6 catches kinks, bends, and flange tilt; non-conforming pieces are set aside for QC disposition [47:55]. Each piece is marked with heat number, PO number, job number, and size, then staged by yard location; a pull ticket plus a nested cut list releases it to the floor, and mill test reports are filed behind the PO for traceability back to heat [48:27][51:34]. Cutting uses horizontal bandsaws for wide flange, channel, and large angle; CNC plasma and oxyfuel burn tables for plate (oxyfuel for thick plate); and an angle master (punch-and-cut) for angle, flat bar, and small channel [52:48][53:49]. Holes are drilled or punched per AISC 360 M2.5 and RCSC 3.3 [56:25]. Layout uses a flange-gauge template and soapstone, with automated scribing of part marks and weld symbols on more-automated lines [58:25]. The whole shop runs under an AISC-certification quality management system with documented, implemented procedures for every step, control of nonconformance, and corrective action [67:54].

### Erection Notes
Erection drawings are the "jigsaw puzzle" instructions tying piece marks to locations; the erector's needs (column splice type, perimeter cable holes, perimeter-railing bolt holes, and shop-versus-field assembly decisions) must be settled at kickoff so the detailer draws once [21:01][24:22]. Whether pieces ship as large shop assemblies or stick-build in the field drives crane sizing and field crew size [25:23]. Sequencing (which corner to start) is set at the project kickoff with the GC and erector in the room; Crosby recounts a large Indiana job that started in the wrong corner and caused major problems [33:34][88:13]. Shipping limits constrain assembly size: standard flatbed 8'-6" wide, 5'-0" bed height, 48 or 53 ft; legal load up to 8'-9" wide, 13'-6" high, 55 ft; permit/signage to 14 ft wide and 85 ft; super-loads beyond that need escorts, with very long pieces (130-135 ft) on special trailers or barge [65:48][slide 98].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The named field and process errors are scope-of-supply gaps (missed lintels, stair and rail items, davits) that surface as field calls [07:43]; starting detailing or erection in the wrong corner [33:34]; and web buckling when cambering, especially on superlight members, if the ram presses only at midspan instead of hitting three to five points along the beam [82:55][83:47]. Visual receiving inspection per A6 rejects kinked, bent, or flange-tilted mill material for QC disposition [47:55]. Crosby notes that bolts shipped to the field too early "grow legs and walk off," so bolt lists are pulled from the model only after shop drawings go out for approval [86:16].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Mill ordering drives schedule and cost: large-tonnage jobs go to the mill against published rolling schedules (which show roll dates but not whether tonnage is already sold, so you still phone the mill), in bundle quantities and minimum truckloads of roughly 42,000 to 48,000 lb; small jobs buy from a warehouse/service center [10:11][36:00][40:05]. Preferred grades matter because they are readily available at the mills; off-preferred grades cost time and money [13:30]. Standard stock choices (96-by-240 plate, 20 ft flat bar and angle, 21 ft HSS, mill-bundle clip angles) reduce drops and handling [37:30][38:14]. Connection-type choice is an economics decision: a highly automated drill-line shop bolts faster, a high-skill welding shop welds faster, so the standard detail is set to the plant, not the other way around [17:44][88:26]. CVN (impact) and other ASTM A6/A992 supplemental tests, when the OSDR requires them, add mill cost and must be ordered up front [35:23].
