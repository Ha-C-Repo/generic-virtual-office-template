## Fundamentals of Structural Stability for Steel Design - Part 3

### Core Concept
This is session five of AISC Night School, presented by Ron Ziemian of Bucknell University and the Structural Stability Research Council, covering beam-column behavior and the analysis side of system stability [00:02][01:33]. The central teaching is how a structure's required strengths (the P and M forces and moments you design for) must be computed from an analysis that captures four destabilizing effects, and how the resulting forces feed the AISC interaction equation [32:48][33:49]. It walks from cross-section yield surfaces through elastic and inelastic member strength to the two code-permitted stability methods. This is analysis theory and design methodology, not a fabrication or erection lecture.

### Spec References
The talk names specific provisions: the AISC interaction equation form p/py + 8/9 (M/MP) less than 1 when axial is over 20% of yield, and p/(2py) + M/MP less than 1 for light axial [08:45][09:15]; alternative interaction equation H1.D2 for the lateral-torsional buckling case [27:10]; the 0.866 factor on the AISC column curve from lecture one [26:39]; the B1 and B2 amplification factors and the C_m moment-gradient factor [42:32][54:54][61:01]; Appendix 1 for inelastic analysis [03:38][67:11]; Appendix 7 for the effective length method and the first-order method, the effective length method dated to the 1961 specification [66:41][69:13]; the RM factor between 0.85 and 1.0 [64:08]; the direct analysis method 0.8 stiffness factor with the tau-b factor, the notional load of 0.002 times the gravity load, and the 0.001 equivalent notional load [73:19][75:21][76:22]; out-of-plumb tolerance H/500 referenced to the Code of Standard Practice [74:50]; commentary table on page 273 from a Shankar Nair paper and the TC10 committee [81:03]. All quoted because the speaker stated them aloud.

### Technical Details
The lecture builds the yield/interaction surface for combined axial force and major and minor axis bending, showing axial force directly reduces the bending capacity below MP [15:23][17:24]. It derives the exact B1 moment amplification factor as 1/cos of a term in P/PE, then shows the familiar approximate B1 = 1/(1 - P/PE) and notes the approximation underestimates the exact by roughly 10% at 40% of Euler load [49:13][53:20][55:25]. The B2 story factor sums all column axial forces on a story over the story elastic buckling load, approximated through story shear stiffness rather than per-column K factors [61:01][62:33]. The four factors driving system stability are flexural/shear/axial and connection deformations, second-order P-large-Delta and P-small-Delta effects, geometric imperfections (member out-of-straightness and frame out-of-plumbness), and stiffness reduction from partial yielding accentuated by residual stresses [34:50][66:11].

### Erection Notes
No field erection, sequencing, bracing, crane, or rigging procedure is covered. The lecture touches erected geometry only through frame out-of-plumbness, which it ties to the H/500 out-of-straightness from the Code of Standard Practice and handles in analysis by directly modeling the distortion or applying notional loads of 0.002 times the gravity load on a level [74:20][74:50]. Sway versus non-sway framing is discussed analytically: a sway frame carries P-large-Delta plus P-small-Delta, a braced non-sway frame usually only P-small-Delta [56:27][57:28].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
This is a design-analysis lecture, so the errors named are engineering judgment errors, not weld or bolt rejections. The speaker repeatedly warns that practitioners misuse the B1 and B2 amplification factors and would be better served pressing the second-order elastic analysis button after learning nonlinear analysis [41:31][42:02]. He flags the alignment-chart K factors as the scary part: the charts rest on nine idealized assumptions that seldom exist in real structures, and back-calculating K from an eigenvalue analysis on a lightly loaded column produces a misleadingly large effective length [70:46][71:46]. He also notes that with the effective length method the design moment used for beams and connections can differ from the actual moment, a differential that can cause concern [78:25].

### Bid / Cost Implications
No direct cost, schedule, or estimating content. The implication for engineering labor is that the direct analysis method shifts effort into a more rigorous second-order analysis but removes the time and uncertainty of justifying K factors, so KL is simply the unbraced length [72:18][76:22]. The two methods generally yield similar final designs, the effective length method showing more axial strength while the direct analysis method produces larger moments, gaining in one place and losing in the other [80:01][80:32].
