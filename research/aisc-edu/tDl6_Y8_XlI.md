## The Erector's Perspective
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tDl6_Y8_XlI | Duration: 1:34:48 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Tim Duke (corporate QC and risk director, Williams Erection and Atlanta Steel Erectors, ironworker since 1998) walks the full erector workflow from bid through topping out. The thesis is that an erector reads the same drawing set a fabricator does but prices it against field reality: crane reach, weld access, lift weights, stability under construction, and weather. He shows how the AISC Code of Standard Practice allocates scope between erector, fabricator, owner's designated representative for design (ODRD), and general contractor, and how connection-detail choices and weld type drive both cost and schedule [01:24][11:30][39:56]. The video is light on verbatim AISC 360 section numbers and heavy on Code of Standard Practice Section 7, AESS, AWS weld type, and erection-engineering practice.

### Technical Details
The S001 General Structural Notes slide lists material specs by shape: wide flanges and WTs ASTM A992 Grade 50; miscellaneous plates and continuity plates A572 Grade 50; angles and channels A36; rectangular and round HSS A500 Grade B; bolts A325 N unless otherwise noted; anchor rods F1554 Fy=36ksi unless otherwise noted; threaded rods A36; steel joists per Steel Joist Institute; welding electrodes E70 with CVN per spec; welded studs A108 headed studs Type H4L or S3L [17:40 slide 26]. Note SS-2 designated members and connections as part of the SLRS, SS-3 required natural beam camber upward, SS-4 allowed splices only where indicated, SS-5 required hot-dipped galvanized finish for weather-exposed members and embeds, and SS-6 capped drain holes at 1 1/8 inch diameter [slide 26]. Systems shown include high-rise cores (W14x1440 core columns), bowstring trusses on bents, 350-foot transfer trusses carrying a nine-story building, brace frames, canopies, slab-edge bent plate, precast embeds, and elevator separator beams [26:54][58:55][66:00].

### Fabrication Notes
The erector asks the fabricator for things that move work into the shop where it is faster and safer: assemble frames and shoring in the shop, button-punch deck side laps, shop-attach angle supports and embed tabs so they are not field-fit, and punch a 13/16 inch diameter hole roughly 1 foot from each beam end for the safety-cable tie-off system [07:19][33:22][34:46 slide 56]. He flags details that fabricate hard: a tube elevator-separator beam cut to exact length to sit against a concrete embed almost always needs field cut or lengthening because cast concrete is rarely true, so request welded shear tabs with a couple of bolts or a seat angle instead [23:30]. One precast/galvanized embed held a 1/64 inch tolerance over the whole element, called out as exceptional fabrication given hot-dip galvanizing warping [48:52]. Bevel depth for the PJP option was set to satisfy the loading, using the 2020 AWS weld nomenclature.

### Erection Notes
Stability under construction is the erector's controlling concern. An engineered stability plan is required when the contract documents specify it AND when the structure cannot resist environmental plus erection loads before completion, so the correct answer to the poll was "both" [78:51]. Plans are stamped by an engineer licensed in the project state, carry general erection notes, temporary erection aids, and step-by-step sequences (X01.1, X03.1, etc.) with circle-slash place-keeping, and must involve the superintendent and general foreman so the field crew is bought in and does not improvise [58:55][72:25][93:00]. Wind limits are explicit: braces designed for 25 mph are expected to fail above it, and trusses cannot be set above a stated 15 mph until columns are guyed to dead men or taken down; cranes carry anemometers reporting gust and sustained wind [59:14][89:24][90:02]. Crane selection covers cost, capacity, boom length, and site prep; a 31000 lattice crawler at ~2500 ton capacity needs 137 trucks and five weeks to assemble [82:01]. Heavy picks are both calculated and weighed on the operator's load cell before swinging, since rigging, paint, bolts, and welds can push a pick over chart at the margins [68:28]. Specialty methods shown: Hillman-roller truss push at an arena (100-ton rollers, two per truss, bolted lifting saddle, no welds), strand-jack sky-hook for 300-ton transfer trusses, a derrick over a parking deck that could not carry a 500-ton truck crane, and a helicopter to remove a tower crane [44:01][55:37][65:46].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The recurring failures are detail-driven, not weld-quality counts. Overhead-position fillet welds at slab-edge closure plates and vent-plate supports are slow and access-limited, so the "not so good" versus "good" detail review pushes for a top-side weld, a plug or slot weld for uplift, or a slotted bolted connection that can be adjusted in and out [20:46 slide 33]. Brace gussets fished in after the beam bend many bolts unless an oversized erection hole is provided [25:44]. CJP welds get 100 percent ultrasonic testing, and a welder who blows out a heavy CJP may get two chances before going home, which is why interpass cleaning on CJP is religious [42:55]. Members found overstressed mid-erection trigger a thorough investigation; the first check is whether the stamped sequence was followed to the letter, with responsibility falling on the plan creator or the erector depending on findings [90:50].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Weld-type selection is the headline cost lever. A CJP column splice on heavy shapes was computed at 690 cubic inches, 193 pounds of weld, 230 pounds of wire at FCAW efficiency, about 35 pounds deposited per 8-hour shift, for a 6.5 man-day splice (roughly a week on a 10-hour shift) per connection [40:02]. Converting to a PJP sized to resist the indicated loads plus a 200 kip-ft erection moment, where research confirmed CJP was not required, cut it to about 1.9 days at ~50 pounds deposited per day, with magnetic-particle plus visual testing replacing UT [42:55][slide 56]. Erection-plan engineering itself runs from a few thousand dollars for one truss to several tens of thousands on large projects [84:16]. Erectors bid off piece counts, beam and column weights, bolt counts and sizes (long 1-1/8 inch bolts slow pin-driving), and weld weight rather than tonnage; AESS, seismic welding qualification, and special electrodes are explicit cost drivers [06:30][14:42][18:05]. Derrick rental at $3,500/month beat a 500-ton truck crane at $4,000/day [55:37].
