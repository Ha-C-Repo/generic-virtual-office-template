## Erection Engineering of Low-Rise Buildings

### Core Concept
This is part two of a three-part AISC series on steel erection, presented by Ben Miller of Ruby and Associates. Erection engineering is the design of a temporary system that maintains stability of the structure during each stage of steel erection [05:33]. The key teaching is that the engineer of record (EOR) designs only for the final completed condition, while the erector is responsible for stability of the bare steel frame at every intermediate sequence, and incomplete structures may not be stable without temporary measures [06:43]. There is no governing code that prescribes erection design loads, so the erection engineer works from standards and guides and applies significant engineering judgment [12:39]. A low-rise building is defined per ASCE 7-10 as having a mean roof height of 60 feet or less that does not exceed the least horizontal dimension [04:30].

### Technical Details
The construction-period wind reduction factor scales the 50-year basic wind speed: less than six weeks 0.75, six weeks to one year 0.8, one to two years 0.85, two to five years 0.9 (ASCE 37 section 6.2.1 table, slide 17) [14:42]. A worked Michigan example took a 115 mph strength-level speed to 92 mph at the 0.8 factor, yielding 15.7 psf at 60 ft with Kz 0.85, exposure B, directionality 0.85 [16:08]. Shielding of repetitive parallel elements applies 100 percent of wind on the first three rows and a 15 percent reduction on the fourth and later rows (ASCE 37 section 6.2.2) [17:39]. Design Guide 10 adds a stacking factor eta from Figure 2-2 keyed to spacing-to-depth ratio a/d and solidity ratio phi; for wide-flange beams and columns the solidity ratio is 1.0 [22:48]. A worked bay (W21x50, 40 ft span, 10 ft spacing, 20 members) gave eta about 0.36, rounded up to 0.4 for conservatism [25:02]. Two ASCE 7 methods (solid-sign Cf 1.9 and lattice-framework Cf 1.8) gave comparable base shear, roughly 7,000 lb per grid for sequence 101 [38:56].

### Fabrication Notes
The presentation is erection-focused, but several fabrication and detailing items surface. The preferred anchor-rod detail moves the four rods outside the column footprint so a wind-induced 32 kip-ft base moment runs at about 90 percent utilization, versus 146 percent concrete breakout when the rods sit inside the footprint [73:46]. Moment connections detailed with flange CJP welds (slide 64 typical beam-column flange moment connection, top and bottom flange CJP, web bolts to develop the reaction) give zero frame action until the field welds are complete [74:11]. The speaker recommends the EOR show optional bolted moment-connection details on design drawings so the erector has flexibility, since a bolted connection can be partially populated (top four and bottom four bolts) for temporary frame action [74:50]. For HSS bracing, knife-plate gusset connections with shop-installed lug plates carrying a shackle are preferred for cable-brace terminations [44:45].

### Erection Notes
Each erection sequence is analyzed as its own standalone building, with wind applied per sequence and temporary bracing sized to the resulting base shear [29:51]. Temporary bracing is typically steel wire-rope cable with turnbuckles for initial tensioning, terminated at a keeper angle on the back of the column so the choker cannot slide [42:38]. Three X-brace configurations trade quantity against cable force (single-story X braces give lower force but more locations; a brace bypassing a floor halves the locations but forces metal-deck cutting and higher cable force) [40:13]. Bracing must remain until the permanent lateral system and diaphragm connections for that portion are installed [47:00]. Long-span trusses need checks both under hook (pick-point selection so chords are not overstressed) and at set (top chord becomes the compression member); a rule of thumb is slenderness L/r under 80 likely stable, over 100 likely needs secondary bracing, and the example W12x40 top chord at L/r 150 required guy cables to dead-men [66:54]. OSHA requires each member end secured with two bolts or equivalent before release from the crane [53:55].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The most expensive failure shown was a lack of communication on a precast-shear-wall job where the structural steel frame had no lateral system of its own; the precast designer never accounted for incidental steel lean-on load, forcing a hard W14x74 brace system and a 5 kip lean-on limit, a cost not in the original bid [56:00]. A common detailing trap is the four-bolt single-angle connection: per AISC Figure 11-2, strain incompatibility means only the two interior bolts are fully effective, so capacity is overestimated [75:42]. Anchor rods inside the column footprint failing concrete breakout at 146 percent under temporary wind is another named failure [73:02]. Cable braces that slide down a column because the choker only bites on itself, with no physical keeper, were flagged as having no test basis for relied-upon friction [43:42].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The dominant cost message is that nonstructural-only lateral systems (precast shear walls, tilt-up) can multiply temporary-bracing cost, and the precast lean-on coordination gap on Example Project 2 created a change cost outside the bid [56:00]. Bringing the erection engineer in early lets the team substitute bolted for welded moment connections and choose anchor-rod layouts that avoid temporary cable bracing entirely, speeding erection and cutting brace-location count and end-termination labor [74:50]. The shielding and stacking-factor methods directly reduce the temporary wind load and therefore brace sizing, so the analysis method chosen has a real cost lever [25:02]. Staged-construction lock-in forces should be reported to the EOR so the owner, not the erector, decides the acceptable percentage of additional locked-in force [96:21].
