## Bolting & Welding Primer - Part 1

### Core Concept
This is the opening welding session of a two-part AISC 2006 educational seminar (the bolting session by Dr. Kulak follows). The speaker frames the entire welding subject around two physical requirements that must be met for any weld to form: atomic cleanliness and atomic closeness [16:33]. Heat is not on that list; it is only the common means to achieve the two requirements [17:08]. He separates fusion (the molecular bond between weld metal and base metal, always required) from penetration (arc energy melting into the base metal, desirable but not always necessary) using a copper-pipe soldering analogy where solder fuses with zero penetration [12:41, 14:00]. The back half walks through the arc-welding processes used on structural steel and how each one shields the molten pool from nitrogen and oxygen [20:43].

### Technical Details
The five arc processes covered are SMAW, FCAW, GMAW, SAW, and GTAW [21:05]. SMAW uses a 12 to 14 inch flux-coated stick electrode with a core wire of about 1/8 to 3/16 inch [22:25]; the arc runs roughly 6,000 to 8,000 degrees F against steel that melts near 3,000 degrees F [22:01]. FCAW reverses the stick by putting flux inside a steel tube fed from a spool or barrel, in gas-shielded (-G) and self-shielded (-S) variants [27:01, 28:14]. GMAW (MIG) uses a solid or metal-cored wire with no flux, so it demands cleaner surfaces and heavily scaled material is shot-blasted first [30:55, 31:23]. SAW runs the arc submerged under a granular flux blanket, limited to flat and horizontal positions because the flux must stay in place [33:09, 34:35]. The AWS electrode numbering was on screen as E7018: E is electrode, 70 is the 70 ksi minimum specified tensile of the deposited metal, the third digit is welding position, and the final digits give coating type and current [24:40, slide]. The slide shows the low-hydrogen E7018; the speaker generically referenced "E70...1" all-position electrodes [24:56].

### Fabrication Notes
Shop process selection ties directly to shielding method and position. SAW is the bridge-fabrication workhorse and is shown mechanized on a buggy with a preheat torch ahead of the arc [35:13, 35:17]; the slide labels a "Mechanized SAW" track laying granular flux. SAW was chosen on a shopping-mall column job specifically for weld appearance because it deposits cosmetically clean welds [34:42, 35:01]. Gas-shielded FCAW is favored in closed northern shops; self-shielded FCAW is common in open warm southern shops even indoors [30:13, 30:24]. GMAW suits clean tubular (HSS) stock and avoids slag cleanup, which is why automotive and robotic lines use it [31:37, 31:52]. SMAW carries a built-in interruption: burn the stub, eject it, chip slag, restrike [24:53].

### Erection Notes
Most field welding today uses self-shielded FCAW because the gasless process is not disturbed by wind, whereas a gas shield blown off the puddle stops protecting it [29:53, 30:02]. The opening field photo is labeled SMAW, a welder running a stick electrode on an open steel frame [frame, SMAW]. Field welders carry a face shield against arc UV, protective clothing against sparks, and require adequate ventilation for fumes [23:02, 23:18]. The talk is process-focused and does not cover erection sequencing, bracing, crane, or rigging.

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The failure mechanisms are metallurgical, not workmanship lists. Nitrogen is highly soluble in hot liquid metal; as the weld solidifies, solubility drops, dissolved gas congeals into bubbles, and trapped voids become porosity while residual nitrogen embrittles the weld metal [18:23, 19:00]. Oxygen oxidizes alloy elements such as chromium into the slag so they are no longer present to do the job the electrode was designed for [19:02, 19:28]. Dirty or oxidized surfaces defeat atomic cleanliness and prevent a sound bond [14:40, 15:30]. The talk also notes the implementation failure of writing "comply with FEMA 353" into contract documents when that guideline left many engineer decisions open [07:53, 08:24].
