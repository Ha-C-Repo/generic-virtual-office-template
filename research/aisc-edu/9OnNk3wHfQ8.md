## Fundamentals of Connection Design: Shear Connections, Part 1
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9OnNk3wHfQ8 | Duration: 1:35:45 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Dr. Brad Davis teaches the design of simple (shear) beam-end connections, the most common connection type in a steel building. The governing idea is that a shear connection is designed as a pin even though a real moment-rotation curve shows some end moment [02:56]. This works because the connection is detailed for rotational ductility: the pin is placed at the most flexible side, which is back at the face of the support [03:18]. Ductility is achieved by keeping plate and angle material thin (5/8 in cap), using wide bolt gauges or widely spaced vertical welds, and never welding across the top of an angle or end plate [05:43]. The session walks the load-path / limit-state tracking method through two connection families: shear end plates and double angles (welded-bolted, bolted-bolted, bolted-welded).

### Technical Details
Single-coped flexural strength follows a chapter-F slenderness pattern with lambda = h0/tw, lambda P = 0.475 sqrt(k1 E / Fy), and a modified plate-buckling coefficient k1 = max(fk, 1.61) where k depends on c/h0 and the adjustment factor f depends on c/d (slide 28) [21:19][29:30]. The worked W14x30 A992 example with a 3 in deep, 8 in wide cope gives h0 = 10.8 in, S net 8.37, Z net 15.1, lambda 40 landing between lambda P 23.4 and 2 lambda P, so inelastic buckling controls at Mn 517 kip-in, phi Mn 465 kip-in [27:00][31:14]. The eccentric C-shape weld group resolves through Table 8-8 with k = kl/l = 2.25/8.5 = 0.265, x interpolated to 0.0465, A 0.307, yielding a weld design strength of 100 kips [55:43][59:43]. The bolted-welded knife connection uses the elastic vector method: uniform vertical weld force plus a linearly varying horizontal force over the bottom 5/16 of the weld length, summed as vectors at theta zero [69:53][71:05].
### Fabrication Notes
The beam is not cut to exact length; design assumes a plus or minus 1/4 in length tolerance, and holes are dimensioned from one end so the far-end edge distance absorbs the error [08:39]. The standard fix is a 1/2 in setback to absorb the plus tolerance and subtracting 1/4 in from the design end distance to cover the minus tolerance, applied in block-shear and tensile-rupture areas [09:46][55:25]. Shear end plates are the exception: they require the beam cut to near-exact length with no plus tolerance, which is why they are uncommon in the US [33:11]. End plates need no holes in the beam, only welds [33:11]. Horizontal slots in welded-bolted double angles let one angle size fit varying web thicknesses across many connections [50:13]. Connection depth should be at least T/2 (manual recommendation, not spec) for erection stability [07:45]. Effective weld length subtracts one weld size per end that stops at the air, L minus 2w [11:20].
### Erection Notes
Stability during erection drives the T/2 minimum connection depth recommendation [07:45]. The shared-bolt problem at a double-sided column-web connection is an erection safety issue: it is resolved with an extra bolt row on one beam, a temporary or permanent erection seat, never the old "suicide connection" where an ironworker holds a beam with a spud wrench after releasing the crane [47:12][48:28]. Bolted-welded (knife) connections require the bottom flange coped so the web can be lowered, or knifed, down between angles already shop-welded to the column; cope the bottom not the top so a hung-up beam puts slack in the sling rather than increasing crane-line tension [63:31][64:58].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The presenter flags a printing error in the first printing of the 15th edition manual where a 1.84 limit read "less than or equal to" but should be "greater than or equal to," corrected in the second and third printings [25:34]. Welding across the top of an angle or end plate destroys the ductility assumption and makes it not a shear connection [65:38]. Forgetting to deduct the beam-length tolerance understates block-shear and tensile-rupture demand. Using Z net alone on a coped web can yield at service loads, which is why the 1.6 M sub Y cap exists [67:55]. No weld-profile acceptance/rejection imagery or NDE failure photos appear; this is a design-method session, not a QC inspection session.
### Bid / Cost Implications
Connection-type selection has direct cost weight. End plates save drilling holes in the beam but demand tight length tolerance [33:11]. Bolted-welded knife connections are chosen when a column flange is too thick to economically drill, so welding to the face beats drilling [64:01]. Double angles are the strong fallback used on the few high-load connections where a lighter connection type fails everywhere else [47:44]. Thick column flanges, shared-bolt resolution, and erection seats all add labor and detailing that a takeoff should anticipate. The video is a design course and states no dollar figures or schedule durations.
