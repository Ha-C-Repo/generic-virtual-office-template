## Fundamentals of Connection Design: Fundamental Concepts, Part 1
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=myhliC9s658 | Duration: 1:30:41 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This is session one of a four-part AISC webinar by Dr. Brad Davis covering the fundamentals of bolted and welded connection design under the 2016 AISC Specification, LRFD method. It establishes the design inequality (required strength must not exceed available strength), resistance factors, steel material properties, the four connection families, beam-to-column classification, the load-path approach to identifying limit states, and the per-bolt and per-weld strength checks. The unifying method taught is to visualize force traveling from one member through the connection into the support and to check each limit state it crosses [18:16].

### Technical Details
Resistance factors: 0.90 for ductile limit states such as tension yielding, 0.75 for non-ductile such as tension rupture, bolt shear, bearing, tear-out, and weld rupture [06:49-07:52]. Material defaults taught: plates A36 (Fy 36, Fu 58); angles now A529 Grade 50 (Fy 50, Fu 65); beams and columns A992 [07:52-08:54]. Bolt groups: A307 machine bolts (60 ksi); Group A high-strength F3125 grades A325/F1852 and A354 grade BC (120-125 ksi); Group B F3125 A490/F2280 and A354 grade BD; and a super-high-strength group F3043/F3111 not yet readily available [21:48-22:50]. Four hole types are standard, oversized, short-slotted, long-slotted, with standard as the default [30:02-31:34]. Three diameters are used: effective hole diameter (nominal + 1/16) for hole-related checks, nominal hole diameter for tear-out, and bolt diameter for bearing [43:58-45:29]. The directional weld factor (1.0 + 0.5 sin^1.5 theta) raises fillet strength up to 50 percent at theta 90 but reduces ductility [56:16-57:50]. A handy unit: a 70-ksi fillet weld at theta 0 carries 1.392 kip per sixteenth-inch leg per inch of length [61:30-62:00].

### Fabrication Notes
Slotted holes are a shop consolidation tool: horizontal slots in web angles absorb web-thickness variation across beam sizes and vertical slots in flange angles absorb beam-depth variation, so the fabricator can cut one angle pattern that fits many beams [31:34-32:36]. In AISC teaching sculptures, painted bolts are shop-installed and unpainted bolts are field-installed [11:03]. Larger bolts (over 7/8 inch) get a 1/8-inch standard-hole clearance rather than 1/16 because of bolt-and-hole tolerance problems installing big bolts [30:34]. Slip-critical faying surfaces require Class A (clean mill scale, mu 0.30) or Class B (prepared surface, mu 0.50) [25:57, 38:20]. The 5/16-inch fillet is the practical economy threshold: it lays in one pass, while 3/8 needs three passes (roughly 3x cost) and 1/2 needs about six passes (6x cost), so lengthening a weld to stay at 5/16 is preferred over thickening it [69:42-71:14].

### Erection Notes
Bearing-type connections only need snug-tight, defined per RCSC as a few impacts of an impact wrench or the full effort of an ironworker on a spud wrench, enough to bring plies into firm contact [24:56-25:27]. Slip-critical and pretensioned connections require one of four RCSC pretension methods: turn-of-nut (snug then rotate through angle theta from RCSC Table 8.2, a function of bolt length; one-third turn when length is at or under 4D), calibrated wrench (wrench calibrated on site with a Skidmore-type device, shuts off at target), direct tension indicators (DTI nubs flatten and a feeler gauge confirms the gap closes), and twist-off bolts (spline shears off at proper pretension) [26:29-30:02]. Slip-critical joints add field inspection and surface prep that bearing joints do not [25:57, 40:22].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The named failure modes are bolt shear rupture, bearing (steel piling up at the hole edge under contact pressure), and tear-out (shear planes at 0.6 Fu tearing a plate segment free), shown from lab specimens [44:29-46:00]. Tension-side failures are tension yielding, tension rupture with shear lag, and block shear [18:46-20:16]. A common detailing error in welds is undercutting the upper plate: welding too high along a plate edge removes the corner and leaves an apparent throat larger than the real effective throat, which is why max fillet size along edges thicker than 1/4 inch is held to plate thickness minus 1/16 [64:33-66:07]. A common analysis error, caught in the poll, is using the nominal hole diameter plus 1/16 for tear-out clear distance; tear-out uses the nominal hole diameter only, while the extra 1/16 applies to tension rupture, block shear, and bolt shear rupture [72:16-74:30]. RCSC discourages torque charts because the torque-to-tension relationship depends heavily on thread friction [79:08-80:11]. Slip-critical connections are warned against as a careless default because of their surface-prep and inspection cost [40:52-41:25].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Slip-critical connections are explicitly called expensive relative to bearing type because of faying-surface preparation (especially Class B), tightening procedures, and inspection, and the speaker's bottom line is to specify them only where genuinely needed rather than blanket them on drawings [40:22-41:25]. Weld cost scales with passes, not just leg size: doubling a fillet from 5/16 to 5/8 doubles strength but costs about 6x, and stepping 5/16 to 3/8 buys little strength for about 3x cost, so 5/16 is the economic sweet spot [69:42-71:14]. Twist-off and DTI hardware carry their own per-bolt cost and care requirements that a snug-tight bearing joint avoids [29:31-30:02].
