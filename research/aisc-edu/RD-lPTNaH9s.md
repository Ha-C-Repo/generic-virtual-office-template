## Field Fixes - Part 2

### Core Concept
This webinar by Jim Fisher (with co-author Larry) catalogs real field problems on steel-frame jobs and the engineered fixes for them, organized around anchor rods, base plates, spandrel and curtain-wall support, and bolted-connection behavior. The recurring theme is that most field fixes trace back to a foundation or detailing miss that is correctable, but the correct repair must be analyzed, not improvised. The presenter stresses that a "quick weld" type fix is often invalid and that the engineer should pre-draw standard correction details so they can be issued to the field without redrawing each time [09:17].

### Spec References
The speaker explicitly cites AISC Design Guide 1 by topic, naming it as "design guide number three" by mistake but identifying it as the base-plate and anchor-rod guide he co-wrote with Mike West [18:34]. He references "design guide 3" for serviceability of buildings up to five stories, with spandrel deflection criteria such as span over 480 and span over 600 [19:36][20:07]. He cites the RCSC specification as having "three pages on arbitration" for resolving bolt-tension disputes [29:00], and the AISC snug-tight definition of all plies in firm contact [29:32]. He references ASTM F1554 anchor-rod material (A36 / 36 ksi grade) and Grade 55 implications [07:12][07:45], the AISC steel manual standard detail for slab-edge plates [15:58], and an OSHA tripping-hazard rule for anchors projecting over a beam [30:07]. AWS qualified-weld requirements are named generally without a clause number [00:02].

### Technical Details
A fully-threaded nut on an anchor rod develops the full rod strength so the rod breaks before the threads strip, and strength is roughly linear in thread engagement, so half the threads engaged gives about half the strength, allowing a thread-engagement capacity calculation [01:02][01:32]. Buried-nut anchor rods give a significant strength increase for overturning and uplift versus bent (hooked) rods, which can straighten in the concrete and pull out [05:09]. An A36 / 36 ksi rod bent no more than 45 degrees can generally be cold-bent or heat-straightened back; high-strength rods cannot be bent or welded and must be replaced [07:12][08:17]. Tolerance accumulation between steel, concrete, and curtain-wall trades can force tighter-than-normal adjustment provisions [20:38].

### Fabrication Notes
The fix for short anchor rods is to extend with a threaded rod and coupling nut, weld the base plate to the rods, or use epoxy anchors, and the engineer should draw these as standard AutoCAD details for reuse [00:32][09:17]. A clean weld-on extension uses a fabricated runoff-tab plate sized 11/16 inch larger than the rod, slipped over and tacked, with the extension rod beveled for a legitimate weld; flare-bevel welds with side plates are an alternative [02:36][03:08]. Do not specify high-strength anchor rods unless required, since a field weld repair may be needed [02:04]. With threaded studs the weld flash requires holes at least 3/16 inch larger or shims to seat a mating plate, and a reduced-base stud keeps flash within the base diameter to avoid back-charges [24:51][25:23].

### Erection Notes
A collapse during erection is described where bent anchor rods were set with the hook about 4 inches too high (in dirt below the footing), so wind lifted the frame and P-Delta tipped it, a reminder that embedment must be verified against the shop drawing [04:39][06:10]. Leveling nuts must be tacked so workers cannot lower them, and heavy columns favor shim stock over leveling nuts [10:48][12:22]. Spandrels and facades need adjustable connections, plumbness checks as the structure rises, and adequate bracing; sweep can be heat-straightened or jacked into position [13:56][15:28]. Curtain wall is best foundation-supported, next best off columns, and worst off a deflecting, twisting spandrel beam, so spandrels must be detailed stiff [12:53][13:25].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named failures include welding a nut to a tensioned anchor rod (cannot produce an AWS-qualified weld) [00:02], anchor rods set too short or too long, too-long usually meaning insufficient embedment [04:08][04:39], base plates punching through leveling nuts when only a regular washer is used instead of a thick plate washer [10:48][11:51], and a metric-to-inch drafting error that misplaced 8-inch anchor rods buried 12 feet deep [22:14][23:17]. Banging bolts are detailed at length: a snug or pretensioned bolt slips into bearing after loading with a gunshot-loud report, all known cases occurred in shear-tab connections lacking flexibility, and the fix is snug-tight bolts or single-angle connections that deform [26:24][28:27]. Anchors projecting over a beam are an OSHA tripping hazard and iron workers will refuse to erect [30:07].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Pre-cast curtain-wall engineers in the speaker's office bid steel-supported projects higher than concrete-supported ones because steel spandrels deflect and create erection problems, a direct precedent that flexible support structures carry an erection-risk premium [14:27]. Coupling-nut and weld-on anchor-rod extensions add the cost of torching out the base plate to clear the extra material, so anchor-rod placement near the web or flange affects feasibility and cost [03:38]. Specifying ample thread length and not requiring full bolt torque where snug-tight is acceptable avoids inspection and rework cost [05:40][29:00]. Reduced-base studs and correctly oversized holes avoid back-charges [25:23].
