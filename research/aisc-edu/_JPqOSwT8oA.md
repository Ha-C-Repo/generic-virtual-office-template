## Structural Integrity of Buildings
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_JPqOSwT8oA | Duration: 1:28:13 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
This webinar (session E4, Ronald Hamburger of SGH and Kurt Gustavson of AISC) covers the new structural integrity provisions added as Section 1614 to the 2009 IBC, applicable to high-rise buildings in occupancy categories III and IV [25:42, 37:32]. Hamburger gives the history (Ronan Point gas-explosion progressive collapse in 1960s London, Oklahoma City 1995, WTC 9/11, the NIST recommendations) and explains the provisions were deliberately written by the NCSEA ad hoc committee to satisfy the political push without changing how engineers actually design or what construction costs [19:37, 20:07]. The technical heart is a set of minimum tie/connection tension requirements: column splices must carry tributary dead plus live load in tension, and beam/girder shear connections must have a minimum nominal axial tensile strength tied to the vertical reaction [30:49, 31:49]. These are minimum interconnection rules, explicitly not progressive-collapse member-removal analysis [66:07].

### Spec References
Cited verbatim by the speakers: IBC Section 1614 (with subsections 1614.1 applicability, 1614.2 definitions, 1614.3 frame structures, 1614.4 bearing-wall structures) [25:42, 27:48]; ASCE 7 Section 1.4 disproportionate-collapse language and ASCE 7 Seismic Design Category A tie/continuity provisions [09:20, 09:51]; ACI 318 structural integrity reinforcement (two bottom bars or PT cables through the column core, Class A splices) [08:17, 28:48]; AISC Specification Chapter B and Chapter J limit states [42:12, 45:22]; the 2010 AISC Specification ballot (third ballot, adding "normal usage" language) [44:49, 91:00]; SCI Publication 212 and the SCI/BCSA manual "Joints in Simple Construction" [50:33, 54:41]. The 75-ft high-rise threshold ties to the IBC fire-department-access definition [76:28].

### Technical Details
For steel frame structures the two requirements are: each column splice has minimum design strength in tension to transfer the tributary dead plus live load between splices (example: 50-kip loads at each floor, splices every second floor, so 100 kips tributary tension) [31:19]; and all beam and girder connections have a minimum nominal axial tensile strength equal to the required vertical shear for ASD, or 2/3 of the required shear for LRFD, but not less than 10 kips [31:49, 39:34]. A composite-slab exception with specified shear studs reduces these to half (ASD) or 1/3 (LRFD) [32:20, 40:35]. The check is at nominal strength (no phi or omega factor), evaluates rupture and bolt-shear limit states rather than yielding or bearing-deformation serviceability states, and the axial force is not concurrent with the vertical reaction [35:58, 42:44, 73:20]. Connection performance was drawn from UK testing: single-plate shear works well, all-bolted double angles yield then rupture near the heel, end-plate connections work, while single-angle connections and angles welded to the support are untested or problematic [49:31, 53:08, 59:16].

### Fabrication Notes
The speakers note the shop trend away from welding toward bolted/punched connections: beam shops favor single-angle and single-plate shear connections because their beam lines punch everything and they prefer to do very little welding [57:45, 61:22]. Connections with the angle welded to the outstanding leg put the weld in torsion and will peel off, so they are flagged to avoid for axial tension [59:16, 86:54]. The provisions create an inducement to detail composite slabs (with adequate shear studs and welded wire fabric or reinforcing) because that halves the required tie force [32:52, 40:35]. No preheat, electrode, or WPS specifics are given; this is a design-provision webinar, not a welding-procedure session.

### Erection Notes
The webinar does not cover field erection sequencing, bracing, crane, or rigging. The only erection-adjacent point is that the 75-ft applicability threshold derives from fire-truck access height, not from erection method [76:28]. General principles only, no erection sequencing cited.

### Bolting Requirements and Welding Requirements
Bolting: the check looks at bolt shear and bolt bearing/tear-out at nominal strength; bolt tension was shown by Gustavson's calculations to never control the double-angle case when 80% of nominal bolt tensile strength is used per SCI Publication 212, which lets the prying force be neglected [54:41, 55:11]. A proposed 2010 spec change permits bearing bolts in connections with short-slotted holes parallel to the force direction for the integrity check only, with the bolt assumed at the end of the slot, waiving the normal slip-critical requirement [46:56]. Welding: CJP/PJP, electrode, and NDE specifics are not addressed; the relevant welding caution is that welds across the outstanding angle leg act in torsion with no reliable capacity and should be avoided [59:16, 86:54].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The named failure modes are tensile fracture of double-angle legs near the heel under axial tie force [53:08]; weld peel-off when a tee stem or angle is welded to the support and the weld is loaded in torsion, which progressively rips off [86:54]; seated connections that carry large vertical reactions but have limited horizontal tie capacity [62:26]; and inadequate edge distance along the beam axis on single-plate connections, which Hamburger calls out as "something stupid like put no edge distance" [83:50, 75:28]. Single-angle and welded-to-column connections are untested and flagged to avoid until research is published [62:26, 78:39].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The explicit design intent was provisions with little effect on the cost of construction and little change to how connections are designed [20:07]. Gustavson's 39-page conference paper and limit-state studies conclude the horizontal tie requirement almost never controls connection design, so most single-plate, double-angle, and end-plate connections meet the requirement without modification [64:01, 66:37, 85:22]. The practical cost driver is detailing discipline rather than added steel: the engineer states the vertical reaction on the plans and the fabricator's detailer designs for 2/3 or 1/3 of it, same as current practice [80:45]. Seated connections and welded-to-support angles may need detail changes or reinforcement, which carries some cost [64:01].

### Your Company Application
For Ivan, this confirms member takeoff and connection design rarely change tonnage from these provisions, so they should not inflate an estimate; the cost exposure is detailing and a few problem connection types on tall occupancy-III/IV jobs, not extra steel.
