## The Manufacturing of Structural Steel Shapes

### Core Concept
This is AISC Night School session two, presented by two plant metallurgists from a mill in Blytheville, Arkansas, covering how structural shapes are made from scrap to finished beam, not how they are fabricated or erected [00:02]. The core teaching is mill metallurgy: chemistry control, the grade specifications a fabricator reads on a Mill Test Report (MTR), and the production sequence of scrap yard, electric arc furnace (EAF) melting, ladle metallurgical furnace (LMF) refining, casting to beam blanks or blooms, reheating, and rolling to finish length [02:09]. The recurring theme is that strength is bought through controlled micro-alloy additions rather than carbon, to preserve weldability and Charpy V-notch toughness [10:15].

### Technical Details
Structural carbon runs roughly 5 to 10 points (0.05 to 0.10 percent) with iron at about 97 to 98 percent; low carbon protects toughness, ductility, and weldability [03:09]. Manganese is the main strengthener after carbon at about one-sixth the strengthening effect, near 1 percent in A992, A572, and A588, and it ties up sulfur to improve rolled surface [04:42]. Micro-alloys vanadium and columbium (niobium) strengthen by grain refinement through nitride and carbide precipitation during rolling, at very small additions (15, 10, 5 max) [07:44]. A588 must hold copper, nickel, and chromium (chromium 40 to 70) inside ranges and meet a corrosion index of 6.0 or higher to qualify as a weathering grade [13:22]. A913 reaches grade-65 properties at grade-50 weldability through in-line quench and self-temper, and its CE is spec-set at 0.38, 0.43, and 0.45, with a mandated Charpy V-notch [14:53].
### Fabrication Notes
This session does not cover shop fabrication, fit-up, or QC. The only fabrication-adjacent content is upstream weldability: CE indicates heat-affected-zone hardenability and potential cracking, which the mill controls by chemistry while the fabricator controls cooling rate, preheat, and filler metal [05:43]. A992 carries a yield-to-tensile ratio for seismic ductility, and elongation across grades runs about 18 to 20 percent [13:52].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Scrap chemistry drives steel cost: lower-residual scrap (number one heavy melt) is the premium feed and the cleaner, lower-residual the charge, the cheaper the resulting steel; residual copper, chrome, nickel, and molybdenum bump yield and tensile by 3 to 4 KSI as a side effect [19:53][07:14]. For estimating, the practical takeaway is that grade choice (A992 versus A913 versus A588 weathering) carries a real material-cost and availability difference that should be sourced, not assumed.
