## Constructability of Structural Steel Buildings: Part 2
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iB0NLSM6H68 | Duration: 1:28:28 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
This webinar, presented by Dave Ruby of Ruby and Associates with project engineer Chad Fox, teaches constructability as a continuous design process rather than a one-time review event, built around AISC Design Guide 23 [00:00]. The thesis is that construction knowledge and experience must be infused into design from concept through field operations, not just at the bid stage, and that doing so improves schedule, cost, RFIs, claims, and profitability [05:04][08:09]. The single most repeated operating lesson for a fabricator-erector is that steel cost is driven by piece count, repetition, shapes, connections, and field labor, not by tonnage, so the controlling economy metric is dollars per square foot, not dollars per ton [55:44][62:53][64:23].

### Spec References
The speakers cite AISC Design Guide 23 (Constructability of Structural Steel Buildings) as the governing source throughout [00:00][43:23]. They repeatedly invoke the AISC Code of Standard Practice, which defines what is delivered and installed if the designer specifies nothing different, and which requires the lateral load resisting system to be defined on the documents [20:23][53:42][54:44]. They reference the AISC Specification and Code for steel buildings and its tolerances, including the camber tolerance of minus zero plus one half inch [27:36][27:36][28:09]. Bolt grades named are A325 and A490 [38:16][42:23]. No specific AISC 360 section numbers, AISC 341 seismic sections, AWS D1.1 clauses, or RCSC clause numbers were stated aloud; spec references here are general principles plus the named documents and tolerances only.

### Technical Details
Connection economy was demonstrated with a six-bolt single shear connection carrying 52 kips using 1 and 1/8 inch A325 bolts, where snug-tight threads-excluded is three bolts at $21, threads-included is four bolts at $28, pretensioned rises to $31.50 and $38, and slip-critical jumps to $68 [38:16][39:18][40:50]. The lateral system comparison (chevron double-angle bolted, tension-X double-angle bolted, bolted end-plate moment frame with column stiffeners, and field-welded moment frame) showed braced frames at roughly $1,900 per ton versus moment frames at roughly $2,100 per ton, which masks an $8.83 versus $13.30 per square foot spread [58:19][62:53][64:23]. The L/B stability rule was given: divide member length in inches by flange width, under 80 is stable as set, 80 to 100 needs a lateral stability analysis, over 100 will likely collapse after release and needs erection bracing [49:31][50:32]. Camber is only a means to compensate for concrete dead-load deflection and never establishes elevation; critical elevations must be defined explicitly with their own tolerance [27:36][29:09].

### Fabrication Notes
The standing rule is to maximize shop work and minimize field work, avoid built-up shapes, avoid framing deeper members into shallower ones, and avoid web connections into shallow wide-flange columns; columns smaller than 14 inch are generally not good to frame into and 6 inch columns only suit pass-over conditions [31:10][37:15][37:45]. Designers should use AISC table connections (single shear plates, single and double angles, seated and stiffened seated, standard shear clips) and minimize bolt diameters and grades to hold field inventory down [41:53][42:23]. Detailing cost tracks piece count and repetition far more than tonnage, and the shop worker, like anyone, gets faster and makes fewer errors with repetition [56:14][57:16]. Joints must be drawn to large scale (3/4 inch or 1 inch to a foot, not 1/8 inch) before fabrication to confirm gusset plates do not overlap and that net section governs tension diagonals, since analysis programs size on gross area and miss net-section reductions [44:25][45:26][46:26].

### Erection Notes
Site review is a design input: access, laydown, pre-assembly area, stock area, truck and crane room, and crane type (crawler, tower, luffer) all affect both cost and the feasible structural system [15:18][15:49]. Long-span trusses shipped in pieces must be assembled on the floor, which loads the floor and changes dead-load distribution, because a structure is erected one piece at a time and dead load follows the as-built geometry, not the completed model [16:20][24:31]. Shoring strategy matters: a 150 to 200 foot truss can sometimes be cantilevered to use two towers instead of three, or split into sections to use one tower, which on a tall building is large schedule and cost savings [21:55][22:25][23:28]. Member maximum column length should be maximized to avoid needless splices, but local union rules and the 30-foot safety-net trigger constrain how many stories of column can be set at once [32:41][33:11]. Field fit suffers at stiff truss nodes; reconfiguring brace framing into the weak axis of a chord makes adjustment easier when bolt holes do not line up [67:30][69:34].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The marquee bolting failure was a contractor whose entire project was rejected because he ordered pretensioned bolts and never tightened them; the inspector required the bolts to be tightened [39:49][40:19]. The Millennium Park Frank Gehry project, built within AISC tolerances, ended with steel joints up to 12 inches off the 3D model from deflection, temperature, and construction tolerances, which left the skin 12 inches from its attachment and triggered a major dispute, because AISC building tolerances apply to vertical columns and horizontal floors and locate only work points and floor lines, not the centers of curved structures [34:11][35:43][36:14]. A New York chevron joint with 14x730 columns and 14x550 diagonals was never drawn to scale; the diagonals were undersized because the program checked gross area not net section, producing a first-week claim to upsize to 730s [44:25][46:26]. A New York shear-wall transfer joint with a 14x90 column buried in a 24 inch shear wall carrying about 2,178 kips had no real load path and required a full redesign of the wall reinforcing; the repeated designer response was that it worked in the model [51:37][52:40][53:10].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The central bid lesson is that price per ton is a poor economy metric because it is only a back-calculation (price divided by tons) and hides the real cost drivers; dollars per square foot is the controlling figure [62:53][64:23][64:53]. In the lateral-system study the material cost varied by only $40,000 to $50,000 while shop and field labor swung by about $120,000, so material is a small part of the total and labor and schedule dominate [64:53][65:26]. The Lansing Community College redesign moved beams from 3-foot to 10-foot spacing, cut 700 members and 1,400 connections, removed steel tonnage, and added a fourth floor for $200,000 less than the original, still on schedule [72:37][74:40][75:40]. The Georgia crane building moved trusses from 25-foot to 50-foot spacing, removed about 835 tons, saved close to a million dollars and 16 weeks while making the structure stiffer by adding bottom-chord bracing (72 members to 172) [76:44][78:46][84:25].

### Your Company Application
For Ivan takeoff and estimating, the strongest takeaway is to drive every bid off dollars per square foot and piece count, not dollars per ton, since the L/B over-100 instability cost, slip-critical bolt premiums, and built-up sections are invisible in tonnage but real in price; the $21-to-$68 single-connection spread is exactly the kind of detail aisc_validator.py member data plus a connection-method flag should surface. For Mario shop and field bolting, the pretensioned-bolt rejection story is a direct field-QC checklist item, and the A325 versus A490 inventory discipline (one bolt type per joint, minimize grades in the raising gang's bag) maps to shop kitting and field bolt control. For Tekla detailing and the in-house PE stamp, the rule to draw joints to 3/4 inch or 1 inch scale before fabrication and to check net section on tension diagonals is a detailing gate worth encoding, as is defining critical elevations explicitly rather than relying on camber (minus zero plus one half tolerance). For Paul safety, the spud-wrench connector story and the 30-foot net trigger tie member-length decisions to fall protection. For joists, anchor rods, and ACP controls, the embed-oversize guidance (make the embed two inches larger to fix 50 to 75 percent of mislocations) and the steel-to-concrete-core tolerance mismatch are practical setting-tolerance notes.
