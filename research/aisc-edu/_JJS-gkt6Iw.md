## Design Guide 32: AISC N690 Appendix N9
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_JJS-gkt6Iw | Duration: 1:25:31 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This webinar by Dr. Amit Varma and Sahas Bardwaj of Purdue presents AISC Design Guide 32, which explains how to apply AISC N690S1 Appendix N9 to design steel-plate composite (SC) walls and their connections for safety-related nuclear facilities [01:32][09:44]. An SC wall is two steel face plates with concrete infill, where the plates act as both stay-in-place formwork and primary and secondary reinforcement, anchored to the concrete by shear studs and tied across by tie bars [04:36][05:06]. The system exists to eliminate rebar congestion (wall reinforcement ratios reach 1 to 2% in nuclear and high-rise work) and to enable modular prefabrication that compresses construction schedule [03:35][06:08]. The talk walks a full design example for a 56-inch wall plus a wall-to-base-mat connection [11:48][15:57].
### Spec References
Cited verbatim by the speakers: AISC N690 and its supplement AISC N690S1 (published August 2015, free download from AISC) [09:44][10:46]; Appendix N9 with its four sections covering design requirements, analysis, wall design, and connection design [10:46]; ACI 349 (used in place of N690S1 for RC design, source of the 1.25 capacity factor, and used for shear-friction design) [09:44][64:01][67:34]; Chapter NB-2 for basic load combinations [37:57]; specification Section I8.3 for steel headed stud anchors [67:34]; Chapter NJ for welds and bolts [67:34]; Section I6.3 for compression transfer by bearing [67:34]; AISC 341 seismic provisions Chapter H7 for wall piers [78:14]; AISC Design Guide 1 for base-plate design (bending and prying) [70:05]. The joint shear strength was given as 12 times root f'c times Ac, drawn from an ACI joint-shear publication [75:10].
### Technical Details
Minimum-requirement gates were checked numerically: SC thickness 56 inches, face plate 0.5 inch, yield stress between 50 and 65 ksi, concrete between 4 and 8 ksi, no lightweight concrete permitted [18:31][19:02]. Three detailing controls govern: face-plate slenderness (largest unsupported length over plate thickness must stay under root E over Fy so the plate yields before it buckles), shear-connector spacing (set by slenderness, by a development length around 3 times the SC thickness for 75 to 90% partial composite action per Zang and Ollgaard, and by an interfacial-shear limit), and tie detailing (spacing under the section thickness, ties classed yielding or non-yielding, with a tensile-strength check against delamination) [19:32][22:36][25:38]. The example used 6-inch stud spacing and 24-inch tie spacing both ways [20:34][26:08]. Analysis uses elastic 3D thick-shell finite elements with element size under twice the section thickness in interiors and under one thickness at connections and openings; two models are run, condition A operating and condition B accident thermal, with calibrated stiffness, modulus, density, and specific heat [28:40][29:10][35:53]. Each element returns eight demands (out-of-plane MX, MY, twist MXY, membrane SX, SY, SRX, and out-of-plane shears) checked through interaction surfaces [39:00][43:34].
### Fabrication Notes
SC modules are shop-fabricated with plates, studs, and tie bars, then shipped and craned into place; the talk shows a 1,100-ton CA20 module built in a dedicated module shop [08:42][09:13]. The steel face plate doubles as stay-in-place formwork, which removes the formwork-set and strip steps of modular RC walls [05:36]. Tie bars hold the form of empty modules during lifting and transport, so detailing serves both structural and handling needs [25:38]. Tie-to-face-plate welds in the example were complete-penetration (the speaker said the weld makes the connection stronger than the tie, so tie rupture governs) [27:08]. Embed plates, attachments, and piping sleeves can be built into the module during fabrication [05:06][16:28]. Fabrication and construction tolerances are specification-mandated and matter because adjacent modules must mate; the example assumed they were met [16:28][16:58].
### Erection Notes
Modules are set onto dowel bars protruding from the concrete foundation and lap-spliced into the SC wall, a detail shown from the Sanmen, China site [08:11][49:44]. Because modules grow very large very fast, crane lifts and field mating dominate erection planning, and tie bars keep empty modules in shape during the lift [07:40][25:38]. The connection region (the transition where stresses equalize between wall and foundation) must be less than twice the wall thickness and preferably about one thickness, both to develop composite action and to avoid cramming so many anchors and ties that the region congests [57:25][58:55]. A Q-and-A point noted that large bars (number 14, roughly 100-inch development length) cannot be developed by bond inside a short connection region, so mechanical anchors or terminators are needed, with the dropped stress picked back up into the steel plate [80:46][81:48].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The speakers named several failure modes the detailing rules are written to prevent: face-plate compression buckling before yield, interfacial (stud) shear failure before out-of-plane shear failure, and section delamination or splaying if ties are absent [19:32][24:08][25:38]. A splice between RC and SC without ties can split open from the eccentricity between the tension force and its reaction, producing a tension field, especially on thick walls [50:45][51:15]. In the worked example two interaction-surface points fell outside the envelope, forcing a redesign with higher-grade steel or demand averaging [44:35][45:06]. A recurring design-process error flagged: an analytical assumption made months earlier (modeling a slab connection as fixed) comes back and forces shear transfer that was never intended, a sign the model and the connection philosophy were not reconciled [83:50].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The whole motivation is schedule: modularity through prefabrication and preassembly cuts construction time versus rebar-cage RC walls [03:05][06:08]. Nuclear walls are routinely over-designed because thickness is set by radiation shielding, not structural demand, so demand-to-capacity ratios can sit at 0.2 or less; this drives the choice between a costlier full-strength connection and a leaner over-strength connection [61:59][62:29]. The closing message was design optimization: the example's first pass ran utility ratios near 0.6 with heavy 6-inch stud spacing and 3-square-inch ties, and a second optimization pass pushed ratios up to 0.9, reducing steel, though the optimization itself is calculation-heavy [84:21][85:22].
