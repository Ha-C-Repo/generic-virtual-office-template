### Core Concept
This is the opening session of an AISC course modeled on a design firm's in-house program to bring new and returning engineers up to speed on the 2005 AISC specification [00:32][01:33]. After a roadmap of seven sessions (non-composite flexure, composite beams, lateral/wind design, metal deck, diaphragms, base plates and anchor rods, trusses, plus computer modeling), the bulk of Part 1 teaches the strength limit states for non-composite flexural members [08:47]. The three governing strength limit states taught are local buckling (flange and web), lateral-torsional buckling (LTB), and yielding, all keyed to the slenderness parameter Lambda [09:19][11:56]. The central practical message is that taking Cb equal to 1.0 by default is needlessly conservative, and a few minutes of calculation can yield real economy [10:21].

### Technical Details
Local buckling uses b/2t for the flange (b is flange width, t is flange thickness) and h/t for the web (h is web depth, t is web thickness); for channels the flange ratio is b/t [16:04][16:35]. If Lambda is less than Lambda p the section develops full plastic capacity (forms a hinge); between Lambda p and Lambda r it reaches initial yield before buckling; above Lambda r it is a slender compression element, rare in wide-flange building design but common in light-gauge metal [14:32][15:33]. The plain (non-seismic) b/t limits target an inelastic rotation capacity of 3, sufficient for wind and low-to-moderate seismic; seismic provisions impose stricter ratios [17:06][18:08]. The b/t limits derive from finite-element analyses and can be reproduced by meshing a section (a W6x15 was the worked example) and loading it until local ripples appear [18:38][19:09]. LTB capacity depends on section properties (Z, r, S, J, etc.), modulus E, yield strength Fy, and Cb, but the major driver is unbraced length, divided into three zones by Lp (full plastic) and Lr (inelastic vs elastic LTB) [22:49][24:21][26:23]. Weak-axis bending of wide flanges, channels, tubes, pipes, and plates cannot produce LTB; it is a strong-axis phenomenon only [25:22]. A brace point exists where lateral displacement of the compression flange OR twist of the cross-section is prevented; a point that prevents twist counts as braced even if the compression flange can still translate, demonstrated by two parallel beams laced with X-bracing every 3 ft that reach full plastic capacity despite lateral movement [27:27][28:29][29:33].

This is a design-theory session and does not cover shop fabrication, fit-up, or QC. It touches fabrication only indirectly: when an engineer builds a plate girder or a custom built-up wide-flange section, thin webs or thin flanges make local buckling a real check rather than a non-issue, so member proportioning at design time constrains what gets fabricated [21:45][22:17]. ### Erection Notes
No field erection, sequencing, crane, or rigging content appears; the bracing discussed is permanent design bracing of the compression flange, not temporary erection bracing. The practical bracing examples (metal deck or concrete slab bracing the top flange, stiffeners plus anchor bolts at the base to prevent twist, kickers, or X-braces) describe the in-service braced condition that the designer must identify, not an erection procedure [30:35][31:09].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
No inspection or rejection content; the errors named are design mistakes. Defaulting Cb to 1.0 for simplicity is flagged as overly conservative and economically wasteful, since Cb can reach 1.5 to 3 [10:21]. The recurring conceptual error is misjudging what constitutes a brace point: engineers wrongly assume beams with an unrestrained compression flange are unbraced when prevented twist alone provides the brace [28:29][30:05]. The closing session warns against over-reliance on software, letting the computer think for the engineer, and skipping checks of input data and output before placing it on drawings [05:40][06:11][06:41].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The economic theme is design optimization, not bid pricing. Properly computing Cb instead of assuming 1.0 lets a section carry more moment over the same unbraced length, which can downsize members and save steel weight [10:21]. Recognizing that prevented twist counts as a brace point can shorten the effective unbraced length and unlock full plastic capacity without adding bracing material [29:33][30:05].
