## That's Not Fracture Critical: Classifying System Redundant Members
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fDcX9uopcRs | Duration: 1:32:15 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
This is a highway bridge webinar (FHWA, TxDOT, WisDOT) on reclassifying steel members that look fracture critical (FCM) as System Redundant Members (SRM), not a building-steel fabrication talk. An FCM is defined as a steel member in tension or with a tension element whose failure would probably cause collapse of a portion or all of the bridge [07:50][08:23]. The 2012 FHWA FCM memo created a path where a member without load-path redundancy can be deemed redundant through demonstrated system behavior, removing the requirement for hands-on FCM inspection every two years [08:23][20:17]. The whole case study is steel twin tub (box) girder bridges whose bottom flanges and webs are fracture critical in the positive moment region [14:37][15:07].

### Spec References
No AISC 360, AISC 341, AWS D1.1, or RCSC building-code sections are cited; this is an AASHTO LRFD and FHWA regulatory talk. Concrete references named aloud: the FHWA 2012 FCM memo [07:50][20:17]; 23 CFR and the National Bridge Inspection Standards (NBIS) as the legal basis [02:09][05:17]; the 1978 AASHTO non-redundant FCM guide specification and AWS the welding fracture-control plan referenced as "AWS C15 class 12" [05:17] (transcript audio, treat the clause label as approximate); AASHTO Strength I and the Extreme Event limit state, with TxDOT adding "Extreme Event 3" for member failure [27:02][29:35]; AASHTO Article 5.7 for deck shear and Article 6.16 for shear connectors [32:40][33:41]; TxDOT Bridge Design Manual Chapter 3 Section 17 plus its Appendix C guide [38:53][37:20]; NCHRP Project 12-87A and NCHRP Report 883 from Dr. Connor at Purdue [53:25][53:55]; and the 2018 AASHTO Guide Specification for analysis and identification of fracture critical and system redundant members [53:55].

### Technical Details
The TxDOT method assumes a full-depth fracture of the bottom flange and attached webs of one girder at the location of maximum factored tensile stress under Strength I, then checks the intact girder under Extreme Event 3 with all fracture-event load effects amplified by 1.2 for dynamic effect [27:32][29:35]. Two analysis routes exist: an approximate method assuming the intact girder alone carries the full self weight and live load (valid for spans under 250 ft, skew under 20 degrees, radius over 700 ft) [35:46][36:17], and a refined nonlinear 3D finite element model with shell-element girders, solid-element deck, and two-node truss deck reinforcement [38:23][38:53]. WisDOT used Purdue nonlinear FEA benchmarked to full-scale UT Austin testing, with strain acceptance limits (average strain under the lesser of 5x yield strain or 0.01, max strain under 0.05, concrete strain near 0.003) plus a 15 percent reserve margin [55:58][59:31][60:36]. UT Austin research project 0-5498 included explosive linear-shaped-charge fracture of a real out-of-service Houston box girder; the cut bridge deflected under one inch after flange cut, seven inches after full web cut, and held 363,000 lb statically (over four times legal load) [16:08][18:13][19:14].

### Fabrication Notes
The webinar keeps the Fracture Control Plan in force during fabrication even for SRM-reclassified bridges: members are still fabricated to FCP standards (higher steel toughness, fine grain, higher shop welding and inspection standards) and only the in-service hands-on inspection relief changes [05:17][52:54][53:25]. Shear stud connectors must be detailed to extend above the bottom mat of deck reinforcement so they can develop the plastic deck mechanism after fracture [33:41][34:46]. No building-shop fit-up, camber, or AWS WPS detail is given.

### Erection Notes
Erection-side content is limited and bridge-specific. The design requires at least two permanent external and intermediate full-depth diaphragms on each side of the assumed fracture location, composite with the slab, to provide alternate load paths [30:36][31:39]. WisDOT stressed that full-depth solid-plate external diaphragms composite with the deck (located about third-span) are left permanently in place and not removed for aesthetics, since their stiffness is what prevents shear-stud failure in the faulted state [48:46][73:22]. Diaphragms should be as deep as practicable, at minimum 0.75 times the tub girder depth [86:39].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The failure modes named are the reasons four WisDOT bridges stayed fracture critical after analysis: bottom flange buckling over the pier on a straight tub with no shear studs over the piers and weak angle cross frames [71:50][71:50]; bottom flange buckling on a Mississippi River structure with no intermediate diaphragms [72:20]; local flange buckling on a long unbalanced end span [72:51]; and shear stud failure on a single long span lacking solid mid diaphragms [73:22]. The historical driver is brittle fracture: Point Pleasant 1967 (eyebar, low fracture toughness, inaccessible detail, 46 deaths), Mianus River 1983 (pin-and-hanger), and Hoan Bridge constraint-induced fracture without prior fatigue crack growth [03:11][04:15].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The economic argument is the entire point. TxDOT spends about 2.3 million dollars every two years inspecting over 480 existing twin tub girder spans, not counting traffic control [15:38]. WisDOT puts a single fracture critical inspection at roughly 36,000 to 66,000 dollars per bridge per biennium, about one million dollars every two years across 17 bridges, each inspection taking 10 to 24 hours over several nights with snooper trucks, four to six operators, lane closures, and law enforcement [50:19][51:21]. Reclassifying members as SRM removes that recurring biennial cost for the life of the structure, and WisDOT removed 17 structures from the FCM list [66:10][73:52].
