## Concrete Filled HSS
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1fzqBwZKLJQ | Duration: 1:02:43 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Jason McCormick (University of Michigan) teaches the design of concrete filled hollow structural sections (CFTs) governed by AISC 360 Chapter I, treating the member as a composite of a steel HSS shell and an internal concrete core that develop composite action [01:02]. The webinar covers section classification, four analysis methods, and the design requirements for axial tension, axial compression, shear, flexure, and combined compression-plus-flexure, then closes with research on lightweight foam fill as a seismic alternative to concrete [01:32]. The central theme is that filling an HSS shifts the local-buckling classification favorably (more sections become compact) and lets the concrete carry compression, giving smaller, cheaper steel sections with added stiffness, ductility, and fire resistance [05:04].

### Spec References
The cited references are AISC 360 Chapter I (the governing CFT chapter, named repeatedly), Chapter G for HSS shear (the 0.6 Fy Aw yield/buckling approach) [37:23], Chapter H interaction equations for combined axial-plus-flexure [39:59], Chapter C second-order analysis and Appendix 7 amplified first-order / direct analysis method [40:30], AISC Design Guide 6 for the interaction approach (noted as based on concrete-encased, not filled, and an earlier spec) [39:59], and ACI 318 for the reinforced-concrete shear contribution [38:26]. Material standards named: ASTM A500 Grade B and C, and ASTM A1085 Grade A [07:08]. Manual and document tables cited verbatim: Table 5-4 (HSS tension capacity) [21:28], Table 6-4 (rectangular/square compression-flexure interaction) and Table 6-5 (round) [33:47][34:18], and the AISC Design Examples Volume 15 worked CFT designs [30:43]. Steel Tube Institute design manuals and a forthcoming HSS concrete-fill design guide (Zhao, Packer, Wang, McCormick) were noted as resources, not requirements [56:48].

### Technical Details
Strength material caps: HSS yield used in strength calcs is limited to 75 ksi, rebar to 80 ksi [07:08][07:40]. Normal-weight concrete is 3 to 10 ksi, lightweight 3 to 6 ksi, the 10 ksi ceiling reflecting limited research and a behavior change above it [09:12]. Sections classify as compact, non-compact, or slender by the lowest element slenderness; the compact/non-compact limit for rectangular CFT is 2.26 sqrt(E/Fy) versus 1.40 sqrt(E/Fy) for bare HSS, and round goes from 0.11 to 0.15 E/Fy [12:14][12:45]. Four analysis methods: plastic stress distribution (compact, used for the Manual composite tables), strain compatibility (non-compact/slender, max concrete compressive strain 0.003) [40:55], elastic stress distribution, and effective stress-strain [16:54]. Concrete compressive stress is taken at 0.85 f'c for rectangular and 0.95 f'c for round (hoop confinement) at the plastic level, dropping to 0.70 f'c for non-compact and slender sections where the wall cannot confine the dilating concrete [17:54][22:31][26:04]. Phi for the composite member is 0.75 axial and 0.90 flexure, versus 0.90 for a bare HSS, which can make a bare HSS calc out higher than the filled one [28:37][30:12]. A1085 permits the full nominal wall thickness; A500 must use 0.93 times nominal because of the +/-10 percent tolerance [08:42].

### Fabrication Notes
The HSS shell acts as its own form, eliminating formwork [05:04]. Mills now produce dual-grade B/C HSS, so specifying Grade C captures the higher yield you are likely already getting [08:11]. When filling from the top, do not let concrete free-fall more than three to five feet to avoid segregation and to avoid damaging a thin HSS wall on impact; pumping from the bottom is the alternative but a cap plate then needs vent holes [50:40][51:10]. If internal rebar is used, the mix design and bar spacing must allow full placement, and self-consolidating concrete is recommended to fill voids around reinforcement [51:40][52:11]. Detail the top of the column for a shrinkage void; shrinkage can leave the concrete short of any cap plate so a load assumed to bear on concrete may actually land on the tube [06:07][52:11].

### Erection Notes
The webinar is design-focused and gives little field-erection sequencing. The erection-adjacent content is load introduction: the external force can be applied to the HSS only, the concrete only, or both, and the transfer must be detailed accordingly with attention to shrinkage [47:38][48:08]. Load transfer uses one mechanism only (no superposition): direct bearing via a bearing plate, shear connection via steel headed studs or steel channel anchors, or direct bond [49:39]. The load introduction length is the connection length (for example a shear-tab length) plus 2B or 2D, extendable only in the direction of the applied load [50:10][50:40]. A load may not be applied directly to a slender HSS wall because premature local buckling would prevent transfer into the concrete [49:08].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named pitfalls: letting concrete fall more than three to five feet causing segregation or wall damage [51:10]; ignoring shrinkage so an assumed concrete bearing actually loads the tube [06:07][48:08]; rebar interfering with through-plate or other connections in a reinforced CFT [06:37][52:11]; and forgetting net-section fracture and block shear at tension connection regions, which Chapter I does not cover [20:57]. On the calculation side: superimposing shear strengths of the steel and the reinforced concrete is not allowed (take the larger of HSS-alone, concrete-alone, or HSS-plus-rebar) [37:23][39:28]; superimposing load-transfer mechanisms is not allowed [49:39]; and in the piecewise interaction the D-point can drop below the nominal curve and become unconservative, so the simplified C-to-B straight line is the safe fix [44:36][45:06]. The simplified rectangular concrete-area method (ignoring rounded corners) always reads slightly high, so re-run with corners when capacity is close [25:34].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The economic case for CFT is reduced steel: filling moves most HSS into the compact category, so smaller, thinner shells reach full capacity, cutting steel cost [05:35]. Eliminated formwork and reduced external fire protection are direct cost savings [05:04][05:35]. The counterweight is that the composite phi of 0.75 (axial) can erase the concrete benefit on lightly governed members, so a section may not be worth filling if the bare HSS already works [30:12][30:43]. Estimating must price the concrete-fill operation, mix design, and trade coordination as added scope, and reserve the more rigorous calc effort (corner-accurate areas, piecewise interaction) for members near their limit [06:37][25:34][45:38].
