### Core Concept
Carol Drucker walks the full AISC Steel Construction Manual (14th edition referenced throughout, with the 15th noted as imminent) part by part, showing how to pull strengths and tolerances from the design tables instead of grinding the specification equations by hand [01:34][88:00]. The manual has 17 parts: Part 1 shapes and properties, Part 2 general design info, Parts 3 to 6 main-member design (flexure, compression, tension, combined), Parts 7 to 15 connection design, Part 16 the specifications, and Part 17 miscellaneous [01:34][05:10]. The recurring lesson is to read the brief introduction to each table before using it, because the table description states the controlling assumption, for example that loads above the heavy horizontal line in the UDL tables are shear-limited not flexure-limited [55:25][63:38].

### Technical Details
Table 3-2 lists plastic flexural strength of wide flanges in descending order with LP and LR, so an unbraced length below LP gives MP directly and a length between LP and LR is interpolated between MP and MR [12:52][74:09]. Table 4-1 carries the PWO, PWI, PWB, and PFB constants that check whether a column needs stiffeners for web yielding, web buckling, and flange bending, though it currently omits crippling constants so Table 9-4 must be used for crippling (Drucker says crippling variables are expected in the 2022 manual) [51:51][53:22]. The instantaneous-center C prime table reduces three pages of elastic bolt-group calculation to three lines and yields a higher connection strength, 384 versus 360 kip-inches in her example [64:14][67:22]. The UDL tables assume full plastic bracing, and she warns the percentage of UDL is a guess on composite jobs, hitting 119 to 190 percent in her examples, so a shear schedule or actual reactions beats a 50 percent UDL assumption [55:25][61:33].
### Fabrication Notes
The maximum single-pass fillet weld is 5/16 inch; a 5/8 fillet takes six passes, so twice the strength costs six times the labor, favoring smaller longer welds [24:34][25:06]. Fabricators prefer a maximum 3/4 inch fillet and switch to a PJP above that [25:37]. Clean column shafts without stiffeners are preferred for both cost and erection, since a beam can slide down a clean shaft [14:23]. The weldable flat width in Part 1 governs reinforcing plate width on an HSS face because corner radii prevent the longitudinal fillet from being placed [08:47]. Bent plates for skewed connections are capped near 1/2 inch thickness by what fabricators can bend [34:10], and many fabricators avoid CJP plates, preferring a PJP with reinforcing fillet [33:06]. Column underrun of up to 1/4 inch should be accounted for when checking connection encroachment into the column [70:31].
### Erection Notes
Beam-bearing connections need rotational stability at the support so the beam does not roll off, covered in Part 2 [10:19]. Stitch plates between double angles must be specified or verified, because without them you have two single angles at reduced strength; Table 4-9 gives the number required [14:54]. Field-fit issues are handled with Table 10-2, which Drucker uses for a quick field-weld size when holes in an outstanding angle leg do not align [35:16]. Clear-distance and encroachment checks (figure 10-3) decide whether a chosen shear connection physically fits a small W8 column [27:39][70:01]. The detail itself should carry all data to support field decisions, including where the design loading came from if it arrived by RFI, email, or phone [42:31][43:01].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Anchor rods misaligned in base plates on every job traced to standard or oversized holes instead of the AISC maximum recommended base-plate hole sizes; switching to the larger holes via an approved RFI eliminated the alignment problems [37:18]. A490 bolts of 1 inch diameter or larger in oversized or slotted holes require thickened washers per RCSC Table 6.1, and an inspector will catch ordinary washers in the field [40:58]. Using a single bolt in a single-angle brace forfeits the simplified E5 procedure and forces eccentricity to be checked about both axes, which can quietly lower strength [16:25][17:26]. Local flange bending is the limit state most likely to control and govern stiffener need, so it is the one to check first [50:50][51:20]. Over-stiff WT or end-plate shear connections can attract moment to a weld line sized only for shear and crack the weld unless rotational ductility is provided per Part 9 [26:07].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Weld-pass economics drive cost directly: a larger fillet multiplies passes faster than strength, so detailing to smaller welds and PJPs over 3/4 inch saves shop hours [24:34][25:37]. Avoiding column stiffeners keeps cost down and speeds erection, so the Table 4-1 stiffener check has a real estimating consequence [14:23]. On smaller or industrial jobs the bid driver is getting tonnage down, which pushes W8 columns and tighter connection fits that the encroachment checks must validate [28:12]. Specifying a flat 50 percent UDL on composite framing is risky and can be grossly unconservative (190 percent in one project), so connection capacity tied to actual reactions protects the bid from rework [61:02].
