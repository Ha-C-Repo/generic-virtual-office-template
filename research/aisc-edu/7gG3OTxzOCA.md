## Towards an Integrated Fracture-Control Plan for Steel Bridges
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7gG3OTxzOCA | Duration: 1:10:02 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This is the 2018 T.R. Higgins lecture by Dr. Robert Connor of Purdue, on rethinking fracture-critical steel bridges as a risk problem rather than a member count [01:01]. The central idea is an integrated fracture-control plan: instead of treating every non-redundant tension member as automatically high-consequence, you engineer the fracture limit state the way you engineer buckling, controlling likelihood through tough material, fatigue-resistant details, and low stress ranges, and controlling consequence through redundancy analysis [23:40][51:19]. Connor argues "fracture critical member" is a misleading name driven by emotion, and that bridges built to the modern fracture-control plan have had no recorded fractures [03:35][48:17]. The payoff is rational inspection intervals in place of mandatory 24-month arms-length inspection.

### Spec References
The talk is bridge-code-centric, not building steel. Cited verbatim: AASHTO LRFD bridge design specifications (illustrative details to prevent constraint-induced fracture, plus temperature zones 1, 2, and 3) [16:29][67:20]; AWS D1.5 bridge welding code, specifically clause 12 as the fracture control plan and its commentary on intended scope [57:34][60:05]; the 2018 AASHTO Guide Specifications for Internal Redundancy of Mechanically Fastened Built-Up Steel Members [39:33]; the AASHTO Guide Specifications for system redundancy evaluation derived from NCHRP Project 12-87A, published as NCHRP Report 883 [44:09]; the Code of Federal Regulations special-inspection provision [40:04]; and the FHWA 2012 memo defining the system redundant member (SRM) [59:05]. Note: this is AWS D1.5 and AASHTO, not the AISC 360, AISC 341, AWS D1.1, or RCSC standards Your Company works under.

### Technical Details
Two redundancy mechanisms are distinguished: internal (member-level) redundancy, where a built-up member of angles, plates, and cover plates lacks a continuous crack path so a fracture cannot jump component to component, and system redundancy, where a composite deck and cross members let the bridge carry load after a full girder or chord fails [26:49][59:35]. The property that fractures do not propagate across riveted or bolted interfaces is named cross-boundary fracture resistance, valid only when the member stays within prescribed proportion and fatigue-life limits [39:03]. Two new evaluation load combinations were introduced, Redundancy I and Redundancy II, with Redundancy II being Strength I at reduced reliability indices of 1.5 or 2.5 on HL-93 loading [62:10]. Probability of detection data showed a 50-percent-detection crack near one inch and 90-percent detection requiring about a 5.5 inch crack, with some veteran inspectors detecting worse as cracks grew [22:38].

### Fabrication Notes
The relevant fabrication content is historical failure causation rather than current shop practice. Named root causes were electroslag repair welds where a spool change left a defect that a repair made worse (Neville Island) [13:56], poor-quality intersecting welds (Lafayette Street, Hoan) [15:29][16:29], and plug-welded filled holes in non-weldable steel that was meant to stay riveted (Delaware River) [17:00]. Connor stated that modern fabricators know not to plug-weld and that repair welds now carry strict procedure and inspection requirements [14:27][17:31]. Test specimens were full-scale 36 and 46 inch deep built-up girders, notched to grow sharp fatigue cracks, then cooled to between minus 60 and minus 120 Fahrenheit to force lower-shelf single-digit foot-pound toughness before loading [30:58].

### Erection Notes
There are no conventional building erection, sequencing, crane, or rigging procedures in this lecture. The nearest content is field demonstration: on the Milton-Madison bridge in Indiana the lower chord was severed in stages with shaped charges to prove internal redundancy, and even with the full chord cut and 300,000 pounds of sand at the panel point the bridge sagged only an additional three-eighths of an inch [28:50][29:23]. The practical erection-adjacent point is safety risk from inspection access: closing interstate lanes for snooper trucks creates queues that Connor cited as 24 times more likely to produce a serious accident after a short queue [20:35].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Several documented bridge failures were attributed to specific defects: the Silver Bridge I-bar with a defect the size of half a pinkie nail, hidden inside near the pin and undetectable by visual inspection [12:52]; the constraint-induced fracture detail with intersecting welds (Hoan three-girder) [16:29]; intersecting welds at a gusset (Lafayette Street) [15:29]; and electroslag repair-weld defects (Neville Island) [13:56]. Connor stressed that most of these were sudden pop-in fractures with no detectable fatigue cracking even under an electron microscope, so field inspection would not have caught them [17:00]. The inspector probability-of-detection study is itself the key inspection-failure finding: detection is highly variable, small cracks are routinely missed, and seniority is no guarantee of performance [22:08][23:10].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The economic thesis is that mandatory 24-month arms-length fracture-critical inspection is very expensive and often low-value [11:49]. Reclassifying a member as internally redundant (IRM) or system redundant (SRM) replaces FCM inspection with a special inspection at an owner-set or calculated interval, where a calculated 18-year faulted-state fatigue life yields a 9-year interval after a safety factor, rounded to 10 [41:36]. For Wisconsin twin-tub-girder bridges this removed the need for nighttime lane closures and inspectors crawling inside the boxes [46:12]. The broader cost argument is that engineering two-girder and tied-arch systems out of the FCM penalty unlocks more efficient, lower-cost structural forms [05:40].
