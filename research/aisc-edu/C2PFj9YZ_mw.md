## Quality Control and Quality Assurance
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C2PFj9YZ_mw | Duration: 1:29:44 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This is the final session of the AISC night-school series "from the mill to topping out," presented by Larry Owner (former VP at Douglas Steel Fabricating, now AISC). The talk demystifies AISC 360 Chapter N, which became the single recognized source for special inspection of structural steel under the IBC. The central distinction is that Quality Control (QC) is provided by the fabricator and erector controlling their own work, while Quality Assurance (QA) is provided by others (third party, EOR, or AHJ) to assure the quality was met [29:55, 30:33]. Every inspection task in Chapter N is tagged either Observe (random or intermittent check) or Perform (must be done on each joint, each weld, each bolt) [51:09]. The recurring theme: if an inspection is not documented, it was not performed [32:08].
### Spec References
AISC 360 Chapter N is the controlling document, with cited sections N1 general provisions, N2 fabricator/erector QC program, N5.1 QC tasks, N5.3 coordinated inspection, and N6 approved fabricators/erectors [30:01, 31:31, 34:19, 37:00, 38:36]. Cited tables include Table N5.4-1 (inspection tasks prior to welding) and on-slide Table N5.4-3 (inspection tasks after welding, frame 5). Section M2.5 appears on the special-inspection list (frame 3). AWS D1.1 clauses are cited verbatim: 6.3 (WPS available), 6.2 (consumable certs and material ID), 6.4 (qualified welder), 5.17 (no welding over cracks), 6.5.2 scope of examination (Observe, "at suitable intervals"), and 6.5.3 extent of examination "size and contour of a weld shall be measured with suitable gauges" (Perform) [12:53, 27:42, 28:22, 86:52]. AWS D1.3, D1.4, and AWS A5 filler-metal documents appear on inspection lists [07:57, frame 3]. RCSC (Specification for Structural Joints Using High-Strength Bolts) sections 2.1, 9.1 (manufacturer certs / fastener marking), 7.1 (tension calibrator) and Figure C2.1 are cited [13:52, 21:03]. ASTM F3125 / F1852 (TC bolts) and ASTM F1554 (anchor rods) are named [17:59, frame 3]. IBC references: 2009 Chapter 17, sections 1702 and 202 (continuous/periodic definitions), 2018 sections 1704.2.5.1 and 1705.2.1 [25:17, 26:39, 40:09, 41:02]. Code of Standard Practice Section 6.1 is cited for material identification [32:53].
### Technical Details
QC inspects against the approved shop and erection drawings plus reference standards; QA uses those same documents AND the design drawings/specifications as an extra check that information transferred correctly from design into the shop/erection set [34:37, 35:37]. Coordinated inspection (N5.3) lets a single party perform a task that QC and QA share, but only if the EOR and the AHJ approve the QC procedures as meeting QA criteria [36:18]. Perform tasks require an actual measurement on each item; Observe tasks are random or intermittent. Every weld must be visually inspected for crack prohibition, weld/base-metal fusion, crater cross section, weld profiles, weld size, undercut, and porosity (Table N5.4-3, frame 5). Fillet welds and most CJP welds can be inspected visually with a fillet gauge for size and a tape for length; not every weld needs NDT [16:38, 48:58].
### Fabrication Notes
A fabricator's quality management system must spell out written procedures for engineering/detailing, contract review, document control, drawing preparation, RFI tracking, drawing issuing and change control, purchasing/material control, prequalifying sublet detailers and sublet fabricators, material identification, and each shop operation (cutting, welding, drilling, bolting, surface prep, painting) [52:35 through 57:43]. Pre-installation verification (PIV) per RCSC 7.1 requires testing three bolts per lot (not per keg) in a Skidmore-Wilhelm tension calibrator; the RCSC commentary permits direct tension indicators (DTIs), which are also produced in lots, to serve as the tension calibrator, avoiding a Skidmore on every jobsite [18:35, 21:46]. TC bolts marked "A325 TC" are F3125 / F1852; bolts are stored in dust-tight containers because lubrication is critical, and the keg lot number ties the bolt back to its PIV test [17:59, 20:29]. Douglas Steel added a voluntary welder identification system (welder number/symbol stamped at each shop weld) and a matching bolter identification system, neither required by Chapter N, so a defective weld or loose bolt can be traced to the person for retraining [43:04, 46:50].
### Erection Notes
The erector's QC program mirrors the fabricator's but for field work: field bolting procedures, field welding procedures, and a nonconformance reporting procedure [58:42]. Owner recommends, though Chapter N does not require, a site-specific safety plan and a site-specific erection plan that establish where the crane sits and how material reaches the right location [58:56]. Field inspection does not require crawling over every bolt: if proper bolting training and PIV were done, TC bolts and fillet/CJP welds can be inspected largely visually for size and length [48:37]. A simple field inspection report keyed to column grid lines (for example grid A1-A3, levels 2-3) records pieces installed, bolts installed per procedure, field welds at all detailed locations, who did it, the date, and any incomplete items [49:14].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Several real Douglas Steel nonconformances are shown. A clip angle detailed 4-1/2 inches to the first hole instead of 3 inches (detailing error) was field-fixed with field-welded clips [60:55]. Missing shop clip angles on piece 57B1 were sent to the field and installed (frame 17) [61:36]. Erectors cut a frame apart thinking it would not fit and could not reassemble it, forcing a rebuild [61:56]. The most serious failure: an HSS 20x8x1/2 (piece 123B1) detailed with a temporary two-bolt connection for erection per OSHA, intended to be field-welded top and bottom, but the field-weld section bubble was never cut on the erection diagram; the weld symbol was missed, the bolt bent and failed under load after concrete pour (CAR #008, frames 18 and 21) [62:37, 64:32]. The corrective action added a detailing-standard note requiring a section bubble at every field-welded joint. A separate case: sublet clip angles (mark 6A209, which was the subfabricator's code for 2-9/16 gauge, unknown to the shop) arrived with no holes in the CNC file; a night-shift CNC operator wrongly halved the 5-1/2 inch gauge to 2-3/4 and added holes himself, producing the wrong gauge on a large quantity that reached the field where holes would not line up (frame 19) [68:47, 73:55].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Owner argues nonconformances should be measured not by raw count but as cost as a percent of yearly sales: most quality programs call a program excellent under 5 percent, and Douglas Steel ran around 0.2 percent even in its highest-count year (141 NCRs in 2012) [77:58]. Counterintuitively, a rising NCR count can mean a healthier reporting culture, not a worse program, because people are comfortable reporting [78:36]. Being an AISC-certified (approved) fabricator lets the AHJ waive third-party QA inspection (NDT itself is never waived, only the requirement that a third party perform it), which removes duplicate inspection cost, but the fabricator must still issue a certificate of compliance [37:39, 82:00]. The closing point ties quality to bid value: "the bitterness of poor quality remains long after the sweetness of low price is forgotten" [79:54].
