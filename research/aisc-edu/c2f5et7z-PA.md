## Designing of Strengthening for Existing Steel Members
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=c2f5et7z-PA | Duration: 1:36:03 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Joe Schuster of Simpson Gumpertz and Heger, co-author of the second edition of AISC Design Guide 15 (Rehabilitation and Retrofit), teaches how to evaluate and strengthen existing steel beams, columns, and connections when loads change [04:10][04:40]. Triggers for strengthening include added stories, change of occupancy (for example office at 50 psf live load converted to assembly at 100 psf), heavier finishes or dead loads, reframing for stairs and elevators, and repair of fire, impact, or corrosion section loss [05:41][06:11][07:43]. The method is verify by analysis: pull current loads, determine the historic member's actual material and cross-sectional properties, check available strength to the current AISC specification, and design reinforcing only where the demand exceeds the existing capacity [12:49][30:25].
### Spec References
AISC Specification Appendix 5 (evaluation of existing structures), with Section 5.3 evaluation by analysis and Section 5.4 evaluation by load test; sections 5.2.2 through 5.2.6 for required material tests including 5.2.6 for bolt and rivet strength [11:47][12:49][16:28][25:48]. Strength provisions from chapters B through K of the current specification, specifically chapter E (E3-4 elastic buckling, E6 and E6.2 built-up column welds), chapter F (F4.2 singly symmetric LTB, F13.3 cover plates), chapter I (concrete encasement), and chapter J (J2.2 weld minimums, J3.2 nominal shear, J1.9 reinforcing existing connections with welds, J1.10 sharing load between rivets and slip-critical bolts) [40:14][53:12][43:17][67:24][71:28][82:14]. The load test uses factored gravity 1.2D plus 1.6L held one hour with recovery to within 10 percent [14:23]. Design Guide 15 supplies historic shape tables and material specs; Design Guide 11 supplies historic rivet tensile values [29:00][79:09]. External codes cited: IEBC (repairs, additions, alterations, the 5 percent stress and substantial-damage thresholds), ASCE 7, ASTM A370, ASTM F606, and historic steel grades A7, A9, A36, A307, A502 grade 1, A325, A490 [08:43][13:21][17:58][25:48][81:13].
### Technical Details
Substantial structural damage means a lateral element reduced more than 33 percent or a gravity member supporting 30 percent of the building reduced more than 20 percent; additions or alterations that raise member stress over 5 percent must meet the current IBC [09:44][10:47]. Historic A7 and A9 (1901) carried 55 ksi tensile and yield at half the tensile; A36 became common after 1963 and grade 50 after the 1970s [19:32][20:12]. Weldability is judged by carbon equivalent: under 0.45 is weldable with standard procedures, 0.45 to 0.6 needs preheat to slow cooling, over 0.6 favors a bolted detail [21:43][22:43]. Beam reinforcing shifts the plastic neutral axis by welding a plate, WT, or angles to the bottom flange; the worked example raised a W12x16.5 from a plastic section modulus of 20.6 to 26.6 cubic inches with a 5 inch by quarter inch plate over only the 7.5 ft of overstress plus development [33:30][41:45]. Loaded columns lose strength near welds because steel above roughly 650 degrees weakens; designers treat the 1 to 1.5 inch zone around the weld as inactive and check the remaining section against the load present at welding [64:23][64:53]. Column preload may be ignored if the reinforced least radius of gyration stays at or above 85 percent of the original [65:54].
### Fabrication Notes
Limit heat input to loaded members: prefer fillet welds over groove welds, use the smallest adequate weld size, use intermittent welds, avoid overhead welding and welding across stress lines [24:48][25:18]. Verify weldability before fabricating reinforcement, by inspecting existing welded connections, by a carbon-equivalent chemical test, or by a tab-plate test (quarter inch by 2 by 2 plate with a 5/16 inch fillet, struck with a mallet, sound if the fracture runs through the weld not the interface) [23:14][23:45]. Make reinforcing plates wider than the existing flange so they weld topside; in the example the 5 inch plate beat the 4 inch flange [40:45]. Termination welds develop yield times area (45 kips on the beam plate, 108 kips on the column plate), and a weld across the plate end lets the side welds equal plate width rather than 2W [44:20][45:23]. Asymmetric single-side column plates risk welding distortion and P-delta and must be quantified [62:51].
### Erection Notes
This is rehab field work inside occupied buildings, not new erection. Shore before cutting rivets or replacing connections so the joint does not deform; if four of five rivets carry the in-place load, rivets may be swapped one at a time without shoring [81:43][81:13]. Shear-stud retrofit cores discretely through the slab, protects the top flange, controls coring water against the space below, then fills with non-shrink grout [57:47][58:49]. When the underside is inaccessible the team cut the slab topside and rewelded deck as demolition progressed [60:50]. New infill beams, props, and jacking-out of preload must follow loading sequence because added members only carry loads applied after install [54:12][51:37].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Over-conservatism is the named failure: bumping member or weld sizes adds heat and distortion to a loaded member and can cause cracking or P-delta [24:48][62:51]. High carbon causes low ductility and cracking, high phosphorus causes brittleness and weld cracking, high sulfur causes porous welds [21:43][22:13]. A tab-plate fracture at the weld-to-base-metal interface signals unweldable steel [23:45]. Coring can damage the top flange or hit slab reinforcing [57:47]. An unstiffened seat deforms too much before engaging load, so a stiffened welded seat with bolted seat-to-beam connection is required for ductility [84:14][85:47]. Combining new welds with existing bolts in one new joint is normally barred for stiffness incompatibility; the J1.9 and J1.10 allowances are the exception for existing riveted joints [82:44][83:14].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The strength-based current-code approach lets a much smaller plate replace a large tube that an old allowable-stress check would require, cutting steel and weld [39:42][41:45]. Reinforcing only the overstressed length matters: the column example reinforced the middle half and lost about 1 percent capacity while roughly halving plate and weld; intermittent staggered welds further cut deposit [76:33][77:05][46:53]. Load testing is rarely chosen because field survey plus analysis is cheaper than instrumenting and loading a structure [13:52]. Reliable project records remove the cost of coupon pull-testing to ASTM A370 [17:58]. Sharpening dead loads, live-load reduction, clear spans, and LRFD over ASD can each save reinforcing [34:00][34:30].
