### Core Concept
This is session 6 of Rafael Sabelli's eight-part AISC night-school series on seismic design in steel, covering building lateral analysis and diaphragm design for a building that uses special moment frames (SMF) and buckling restrained braced frames (BRBF) [01:02]. The first half walks through lateral analysis methods in ASCE 7, load cases, accidental torsion, structural modeling, stability and second-order (P-delta) effects, and developing strength-level design forces [02:36]. The second half develops diaphragm forces, shear, collectors and collector connections, chord forces, capacity-design forces, and analysis around a diaphragm opening [03:37]. The recurring theme is that moment frames are usually drift-controlled while braced frames are strength-controlled, and that everything in the lateral system carries an amplified design force from the P-delta effect [37:37].

### Technical Details
The example building uses SMF in one direction with reduced beam section (RBS) connections and BRBF in the other, on rigid concrete-filled-deck diaphragms [16:31]. Base shears were 271 kips for the SMF and 331 kips for the BRBF; vertical distribution used K of 1.21 for the SMF (period near 1 second) and 1.125 for the BRBF [13:57]. Accidental torsion at 5 percent of building dimension drove each frame line to 55 percent of base shear, a 10 percent increase [13:26]. BRBF modeling multiplied work-point-to-work-point brace stiffness by a stiffening factor KF of 1.4 (range 1.2 to 1.8, from manufacturer literature, varying with connection type, brace area, and length) [22:12]. Calculated B2 factors were all below 1.1, allowing K of 1 and the effective length method [41:44]. Diaphragm collector design used omega-sub-O of 3 for the SMF; a roof diaphragm force of 142 kips gave 71 kip reactions, and capacity-design brace forces were 118 kips tension and 135 kips compression with a 179 kip horizontal component [57:42]. The collector beam was a W18x50 checked for constrained-axis flexural-torsional buckling (386 kips capacity at 13 ft), with composite flexural strength of 516 kips from Table 3-19 requiring 33 studs [62:49].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The speaker flags engineering errors rather than shop or field rejections. The most emphasized is K-factor miscalculation: he recommends avoiding hand-calculated K factors because engineers commonly get them wrong with leaning columns, and instead using the first-order then direct analysis method [30:56]. A second-order analysis that omits gravity load is not actually second-order, since the destabilizing P-delta effect needs the gravity load present [31:57]. Mixing load-combination factors (1.2 on one term and 1.0 on another) between the seismic mass and the P-delta gravity load is called out as inconsistent [38:39]. He notes B2 must never exceed 1.5 for a seismic design because ASCE 7 stability would not allow it [26:17]. Tonnage error from column-size modeling shortcuts is the one item that maps to a takeoff/estimating risk [17:33].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The equivalent lateral force procedure is slightly conservative and can drive members one or even two sizes larger than modal response spectrum analysis, a direct tonnage and cost difference [04:38]. BRBFs are increasingly chosen over special concentrically braced frames (SCBF), now used even in one- and two-story buildings, which shifts which system is most economical on a given job [85:26]. Sizing moment frames for drift first rather than strength is presented as the efficient design path, avoiding wasted effort on members that drift later forces to grow [82:22]. The model-derived tonnage caution at [17:33] is the clearest estimating-accuracy flag: tonnages pulled from an analysis model can be off if column sizes are placed at floor lines rather than true splice points.
