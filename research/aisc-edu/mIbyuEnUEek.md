## The AISC Seismic Provisions: Past, Present, and Future
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mIbyuEnUEek | Duration: 1:33:47 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This is Jim Malley's 2010 TR Higgins lecture on the AISC Seismic Provisions (AISC 341), tracing seismic steel design from the 1920s through the 2005 and 2010 editions [11:38][12:09]. The controlling idea is the three-part philosophy of AISC 341: identify a target yield mechanism, designate deformation-controlled ductile fuses where earthquake energy is dissipated, then use capacity design so the rest of the system stays essentially elastic against the forces those fuses can develop [26:42][27:12][28:15]. A key consequence for fabricators is that seismic design works from expected material strength, not minimum specified strength, because the fuse capacity has to be the real capacity, not the floor value [37:35][38:05].
### Spec References
Cited verbatim by the speaker: AISC 341 (seismic provisions, 2005 and 2010 editions) [24:38][26:10]; AISC 360 (main specification) [30:19][90:23]; AISC 358 pre-qualified connections for special and intermediate moment frames [54:00][54:32]; AWS D1.8 seismic welding supplement, newly referenced in 2010 versus the 2005 Appendix W [31:22][41:39]; ASCE 7-05 and ASCE 7-10, including ASCE 7-10 Chapter 16 for nonlinear response history analysis [26:10][53:00]; ACI 318 including Appendix D for anchorage at composite systems and column bases [30:50][49:55]; ACI Chapter 22 for diaphragm coordination [46:48]; FEMA 353 (basis for the Appendix Q quality program), FEMA 695 / ATC 63 (system validation), ATC 3 and ATC 58, and the AISC Seismic Design Manual [73:57][89:52][74:28]. System acronyms named: SMF, IMF, OMF, STMF, SCBF, OCBF, EBF, BRBF, and SPSW.
### Technical Details
Width-thickness limits in ductile elements are stricter than AISC 360, with 2010 introducing the moderately ductile and highly ductile element terms tied to lambda-ps and the plastic limit [44:46][45:46][91:00]. Demand-critical welds require two toughness levels, roughly 20 ft-lb at zero degrees for all seismic welds and 40 ft-lb at 70 degrees for demand-critical welds, both from the FEMA/SAC research [42:11][42:43]. SMF connections must be tested to 0.04 radians of drift, achieved by project test, appendix pre-qualification, or AISC 358; IMF drops to 0.02 radians [53:30][59:07]. SCBF analysis in 2010 adds a two-level check using a 30 percent residual brace compression capacity to capture the unbalanced post-buckling force [63:45][64:46]. EBF links must be designed with strain hardening, about 1.25 for braces and 1.1 for columns, with all three links above a base column taken as yielding simultaneously [68:18][68:48].
### Fabrication Notes
Heavy shapes and plates carry Charpy V-notch toughness requirements on the base material, slightly more restrictive than the older large-truss thickness triggers [38:35][39:05]. Protected zones at plastic hinges are no-fly zones: no curtain-wall attachments, no shot pins for metal deck, only small spot welds for OSHA deck hold-down, and the engineer must mark them on drawings so the shop and field do not disturb them [42:43][43:45][44:16]. AWS D1.8 now governs backing bars, welding procedure specifications, and continuity-plate detailing that the 2005 Appendix W carried [41:39][41:39]. Column splices favor complete-joint-penetration welds because real ground motions move the plastic hinge through the story and can drive near-full column moment at the splice [56:34][57:05].
### Erection Notes
Bolted joints in seismic frames use fully pretensioned high-strength bolts with Class A slip-critical faying-surface preparation but are designed for bearing, so small earthquakes do not slip while major events use the bearing strength [39:36][40:07]. Standard holes are the default; oversized holes are allowed at braces to ease erection fit-up [40:38]. Lateral braces for moment-frame beams must sit roughly one flange width clear of the plastic hinge and never inside the protected zone, and the brace force at a hinge is about 6 percent of the flange capacity, a large number to deliver back into the diaphragm [57:36][58:07][58:37]. Column bases must be designed to the same force as the member above, putting anchorage squarely into ACI 318 Appendix D territory [49:23][49:55].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The speaker shows a Lehigh test photo where a stud connector placed over the middle of the plastic hinge caused a fracture straight through the beam top flange, a direct protected-zone violation [43:15]. He notes the recurring field problem of a plumber or other trade attaching to a protected zone unaware, which then must be repaired with the contractor [44:16]. A second slide shows a lateral brace mistakenly placed in the middle of a plastic hinge because the design engineer did not communicate the zone [58:37]. Appendix Q sets the QA/QC inspection tiers (observe, perform, document), with ultrasonic and magnetic-particle NDE required at CJP welds and other special locations [74:28][74:59][75:30].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Using the seismic provisions in low categories (A, B, C) buys a higher R factor and smaller members, but the speaker is explicit that smaller members do not necessarily mean a cheaper structure once the seismic detailing is added [34:31][35:01]. EBF strain-hardening capacity design can make lower columns humongous and inefficient on tall buildings, which is why nonlinear response history analysis is used to right-size them [79:36][80:07]. The 2010 cycle deliberately relaxed some 2005 rules, dropping the belt-and-suspenders ductile-limit-state requirement on connections to ease bolted-connection design [40:38][41:09]. The mix-and-match concept (special, intermediate, and ordinary connections in one frame based on demonstrated demand) is flagged as a future economic gain [88:51][89:22].
