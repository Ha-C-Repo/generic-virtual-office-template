## Quality Assurance for Structural Engineering Firms
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ofkHTRSZDPU | Duration: 1:00:59 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Clifford Swinger, full-time QA manager at The Harmon Group (a 50-person, 40-engineer firm with offices in Las Vegas, Philadelphia, and Pittsburgh), describes a 20-year-old in-house quality-assurance program for the engineer-of-record side of structural work [00:00][46:49]. The program rests on six components: training for young engineers, design standards, drafting and CAD standards, a project delivery system, a server-based knowledge base, and involvement of a QA manager who performs reviews [09:14]. The driving thesis is that fast-track and "hyper-track" scheduling, complex codes, and over-reliance on computer software have eroded document quality, so QA must be ongoing across the whole design rather than a single end-of-job check [03:35][18:59].
### Spec References
This is a firm-management and document-quality webinar, not a code clause walk-through, so it cites very few standards. The one document repeatedly recommended as a template for a QA program is the CASE publication "962D, a guideline addressing coordination and completeness of structural construction documents" [07:09]. The speaker also recommends a trade book called "the contractor's guide to change orders" for understanding how contractors mine documents for change orders [07:42]. AISC is credited generally for two decades of guidance to show beam reactions on framing plans for connection economy, but no AISC 360 section, table, or Design Guide number is cited [33:29]. ASD and LRFD are named as the two acceptable design methods [10:46]. No AISC 341, AWS D1.1, or RCSC sections are cited.
### Technical Details
Lateral-system and computer-model judgment dominate the technical content. The speaker warns against trusting infinitely rigid diaphragm outputs that funnel 95 percent of lateral load into one shear wall when a flexible precast diaphragm cannot deliver it, and his rule is to never put less than the tributary load on any brace frame or moment frame and to envelope the rigid-diaphragm result [26:46][27:48]. For low-seismicity regions he insists on a seismic response coefficient R factor of three, noting engineers who used three-and-a-half or six drove unnecessary cost into connections without understanding the connection-design consequence [20:35]. He flags drag-strut load paths that rigid diaphragms ignore when a brace frame shifts bays, requiring an explicit drag strut and drag-strut connections sized for the real force, for example 150 kips [28:49][29:20]. He checks framing by hand-designing one typical beam, girder, and column and comparing to the model, then scanning all member sizes for consistency, and he runs quick wind and seismic hand checks (average wind pressure times sail area for base shear; approximate versus exact building period for seismic) [30:23][31:55].
### Fabrication Notes
The detailability of connections is treated as a QA gate. He cites a W40x593 framing into another W40x593 at a steep skew with a roughly 760 kip reaction that was virtually impossible to detail and had to be reconfigured for constructability [36:34][47:53]. For roof joists, where W shapes on the roof can be shallow, he requires at least a W14 framing into the girder so two rows of bolts fit at the connection [37:34]. He argues that clean, simple drawings using dittos for repeated members lower the fabricated cost per ton, and that small unintended variations (different cambers, 35 versus 37 kip reactions on otherwise identical W18x35 beams) should be enveloped to a single repeated mark [42:13][42:43].
### Erection Notes
The webinar does not cover field erection sequencing, crane, rigging, or bracing procedures. Its construction-phase content is shop-drawing review during construction administration and coordinating late loads (rooftop mechanical, hung piping, theater rigging, catwalks) that are not known until end of design [33:29][35:33]. Field topics such as column splices appear only as design-document subtleties: a splice four feet above a mezzanine floor that does not brace the column effectively doubles the unbraced height [39:07].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named recurring errors include self-weight entered twice (analysis switch left on while dead load was also added by hand), the wrong R factor, missing or improper expansion-joint layouts that lock up the structure, unbraced columns sized off an unrealistic rigid diaphragm (W12x65 modeled where W14x211 was actually required for an 80-foot theater column), and a girder moved off a column line at the last minute raising a reaction from 36 to 100 kips into an unrevised beam [25:13][40:39][38:06]. He stresses drafting errors that cause structural problems, citing a 111 kip reaction that read as 11 kips and a decimal slip that hid a 155 kip reaction [44:17][44:47]. Missing items (reactions, sections, details, dimensions, member sizes, even an entirely omitted infill beam at a 15-to-16-foot gap) are called the hardest defects to catch, along with architectural-coordination misses such as a slab opening cutting a column free of its two-way slab [43:15][45:18].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The economic case for QA is fewer RFIs and change orders on increasingly adversarial fast-track jobs, where any missing information quickly becomes a change order [03:05][06:39]. Showing actual reactions on framing plans lets fabricators detail connections for real loads instead of a fictitious percentage of member capacity, which the speaker ties directly to lower structural cost [33:29][34:30]. Clean repetitive drawings with dittos are described as a way to lower a fabricator's quoted cost per ton [42:43]. The firm reports an insurance premium reduction for carrying a formal written QA program, and the program's cost is roughly one senior engineer's salary out of fifty staff [50:29][53:33].
