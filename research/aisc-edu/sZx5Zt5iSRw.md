## Staggered Truss System: Lessons Learned
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sZx5Zt5iSRw | Duration: 58:39 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
The webinar teaches the staggered truss structural system through two completed mid-rise projects: a 14-steel-floor hotel tower over four post-tensioned concrete parking levels in downtown Chicago (Staybridge Suites, 186 ft, 190x60 footprint, 10 ft floor-to-floor) and two rural college dormitories in southern Ohio (each about 45,000 SF, 200 beds, up to seven stories, 10 ft floor-to-floor) [02:02][05:38][07:10]. A story-deep truss sits on every other floor along a column line, and the trusses stagger floor-to-floor on adjacent column lines in the traditional checkerboard arrangement so the floor plank spans from the top chord of one truss to the bottom chord of the next [07:10][12:18]. The system is purpose-built for repetitive modular-dwelling layouts (dormitories, hotels, apartments) with a central running corridor, because the Vierendeel panel of the truss is placed at that corridor [11:48]. The headline benefit is a low floor-to-floor height with everything prefabricated for fast erection.
### Spec References

Treat every other engineering point here as general principles, no section cited.
### Technical Details
The common truss is a Pratt configuration so the longer diagonals carry tension and the verticals carry compression, with diagonals economically set at 45 to 60 degrees and a Vierendeel panel centered at the corridor [11:48][12:18]. Design Guide 14 suggests double channels for tension diagonals and tubes (HSS) for compression verticals [33:39]. Engineers are told to keep diagonal-member stress ratios at or below about 0.80 to 0.85 so shear-lag and effective-area reductions still permit a workable connection [12:48]. Trusses resist lateral load in the short direction, often giving lateral resistance "for free" once sized for gravity, with rigid moment frames in the long direction; Staybridge instead hid brace frames behind elevator shafts to avoid expensive moment connections [04:06][15:20]. Diaphragm design is treated as central because of the vertical irregularity: lateral force enters the top chord, passes through the web to the bottom chord, then must transfer through the diaphragm to adjacent trusses one bay over since no vertical element sits below that bottom chord. The team drew shear and moment diagrams for every story in both directions and designed chord forces from the diaphragm moment divided by diaphragm depth [25:00][27:32].
### Fabrication Notes
Truss chord flanges must be wide enough for plank bearing on both sides plus the grouted joint over the chord; with a roughly 2 in plank bearing each side and a 4 in grouted joint, chords needed at least 8 in flange width, with 10 to 12 in more ideal [16:50]. Bottom-chord gusset plates run large because the planks must seat on either side (an 8 to 10 in plank plus about 2 in clearance), and pre-composite loads can drive gusset design through whitmore buckling over that length [17:21]. Fabricators debate weight optimization versus truss repetition: designing one worst-case truss and repeating it lets the shop keep a jig and consistent workable gauges, while Staybridge optimized each truss for weight so upper-floor trusses are lighter [29:35][30:05]. Section availability should be confirmed early with a small rural fabricator. Headed (Nelson) studs on the chords are easier to apply in the shop, but the team flagged the shop-applied stud tripping hazard during erection as a coordination item [47:22]. Large completed truss assemblies consume storage space when a delivery-to-erection gap occurs, as happened at Staybridge [39:45].
### Erection Notes
The dormitory specification required a signed and sealed erection plan from the fabricator/erector, and the design engineer ended up assisting that sequence [40:15]. On the stepped hilly site, erection started at the small top-of-hill end to build a stable mass, then worked down [40:45]. The governing erection problem is bracing the truss top chord, which wants to go into compression before the diaphragm exists: the crew used temporary X cable bracing to plumb the frame and carry short-direction load before moment frames and diaphragm were in place, then braced the top chord with cables, set precast to the first Vierendeel vertical, added pipe braces from the top chord down into the precast, removed the cable bracing, and ran the remaining precast, repeating truss by truss [42:18][43:19][44:21]. Pipe braces were anchored with drilled anchors into the precast and a temporary welded tab on the chord, later removed and patched under the topping slab [46:21]. It is advantageous for one erector to set both trusses and plank, and the precast used for erection stability must be detailed in step with the steel [31:07][41:17]. In an urban site with near-zero laydown, two trusses arrive per truck and the crane lifts each truss directly off the truck into place [05:07].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The named field error is treating erection-stability embed plates as a later batch job: at Staybridge the contractor erected nearly a full floor of precast plank with not a single embed plate welded, planning to weld three floors at once, which defeats the embed plates' erection-stability purpose until the engineer required welding as the crew progressed [33:08]. A coordination failure was late MEP input: the electrical engineer (a design-build sub added partway and not on the design team) needed conduit in the plane of the steel, forcing 5 in circular web penetrations through truss chords; circular holes and a forces check avoided expensive angle-and-plate reinforcing [23:29][24:00]. Other recurring issues are gusset plates protruding past the Vierendeel vertical into the finished corridor wall [38:12], combined truss-plus-precast camber eating a 2 in topping down to about a quarter inch [47:52], and an expected crack in the topping along the truss chord line even with fiber and joint reinforcing, since deflection and camber are lost where two precast pieces butt together [48:55]. Plank blockouts at exterior columns and exterior-truss gussets (a 4 ft blockout at one exterior truss) left voids needing a cantilevered-plank or bent-plate detail to support cladding [52:28].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The headline commercial driver is speed: everything is prefabricated, the truck delivers and the crane sets directly, and the speaker cites a referenced Arizona project that erected roughly 12 floors of trusses and plank in about 19 days, with a normal pace of a floor every week or two when well coordinated [54:01][57:08]. An untopped plank system (used at Staybridge and described as AISC-endorsed) removes the concrete subcontractor and wet trades above the foundation, since everything becomes prefabricated trusses, precast plank, and site-mixed grout, saving time and money, but it makes the grouted joints critical for diaphragm shear and demands a willing supplier [51:28][52:28]. The system steel weight came in lower than a traditional composite steel frame; the speaker recalled an order of about 12 to 13 psf from a fabricator study but explicitly would not state it with certainty, and could not recall his own project's number [56:37]. Early involvement of GC, fabricator, and plank supplier is repeatedly tied to cost, and is harder to secure on a hard-bid project [49:57][50:28].
