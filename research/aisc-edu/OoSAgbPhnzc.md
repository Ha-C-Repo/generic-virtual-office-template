## Introduction to Basic Steel Design
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OoSAgbPhnzc | Duration: 1:29:45 | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This is a principles review of structural steel design under the current AISC 360-16 specification, presented by Louis F. Geschwindner (former AISC VP of engineering and research, chair of the member design technical committee). The unifying idea is the safety equation "required strength less than or equal to available strength" [14:57], where required strength (load effect) comes from structural analysis and available strength (resistance) comes from the specification's limit-state equations [15:58]. He builds the statistical basis for both LRFD and ASD, including the reliability index beta and the calibration of ASD to LRFD at a live-to-dead ratio of three [38:46]. The session closes on the available steel shapes, materials, and a worked demonstration that second-order effects are real and cannot be ignored [69:29].

### Technical Details
Resistance is built on three strength limit states (yielding, buckling, rupture) plus serviceability limit states in Chapter L (deflection, drift, vibration, connection slip) [31:59]. Resistance factors given verbatim: phi 0.9 for yielding, 0.75 for rupture, 0.7 for connection slip, 0.65 for composite components with shear studs [36:13]. The tension yielding example is Pn = Fy times Ag with phi 0.9 (LRFD) and omega 1.67 (ASD) [51:16]. Shape counts from the manual: 283 W shapes, 18 M, 28 S, 22 HP, 137 angles, 281 rectangular HSS, 107 square HSS, 128 round HSS, 51 pipe, for 1,452 total; heaviest is a W36 at 925 lb/ft, lightest a half-inch pipe at 0.85 lb/ft [55:58-60:09]. A992 preferred for W shapes (Fy 50, Fu 65), A36 for M/S/C/MC/L, A572 Gr 50 for HP, A500 Gr C for HSS, A53 Gr B pipe (Fy 35, Fu 60) [64:17]. The worked second-order examples (W12x96 columns under 400 kip axial and 200 ft-kip moment) show amplification of roughly 1.14 for uniform moment, 1.11 for the beam-column gravity case, and 1.42-plus for the flagpole cantilever [71:05-76:19].

### Fabrication Notes
Chapter M of AISC 360 covers fabrication and erection, named in the chapter map but not detailed in this introductory session [12:22]. The speaker noted that fabrication variability (actual versus published plastic section modulus when every piece of a section is measured) is one of the three factors, with modeling and material, that the resistance factor accounts for [35:42]. He also flagged that building a custom built-up shape from plates and bars carries significant added cost versus using a rolled shape [60:39]. No tolerances, fit-up, or shop QC procedures were covered.

### Erection Notes
Erection is acknowledged only as the subject of Chapter M and as part of the Code of Standard Practice contractual provisions [09:14]. No field procedures, sequencing, bracing, crane, or rigging content appears in this session, which is scoped to design principles.

### Bolting and Welding
This session names connection limit states and the connection chapters (J, K) but does not cover bolt grades, pretension methods, electrodes, preheat, or NDE. The only welding-adjacent content is metallurgical: manganese improves notch toughness but harms weldability, carbon and manganese both raise strength while reducing ductility, vanadium raises strength without harming weldability, and columbium raises strength with a significant negative impact on notch toughness [65:51]. RCSC, AWS D1.1, and specific bolting or welding installation methods are not addressed here.

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
No inspection failures or rejection modes are named, consistent with a design-principles introduction rather than a shop or field session. The closest cautions are conceptual: ignoring second-order effects underestimates real moments (the flagpole case showed at least a 32 percent and likely larger increase) [76:19], and assuming a simple beam when an end connection induces moment restraint introduces model error [22:38]. The speaker also warned that being listed in A6 does not guarantee a shape is commercially available, so specifying an unavailable or offshore-only shape is a practical design error to avoid [85:26].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The explicit cost lesson is that custom built-up members cost significantly more to make than standard rolled shapes, so the designer should favor rolled sections unless a built-up shape is justified [60:39]. The W36x925 at 925 lb/ft is flagged as a member to specify carefully given its weight and cost [60:39]. The reminder that listed A6 shapes may not be procurable (some produced only offshore, some never produced) is a direct sourcing and lead-time risk; the speaker pointed to an AISC website resource for checking shape availability [85:26].
