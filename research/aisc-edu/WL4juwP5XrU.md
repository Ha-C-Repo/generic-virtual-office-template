## Design of Underhung Hoist and Crane Girders
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WL4juwP5XrU | Duration: 1:32:40 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
The webinar (Lucas Toggle, AISC, 2017-05-18) covers design of underhung bridge crane runway girders, where the hoist and trolley travel on a bridge girder whose end trucks ride on the bottom flange of the runway members suspended from the roof framing [01:32][02:35]. The controlling theme is that underhung (bottom-flange-loaded) runway design differs from top-running crane design: there is no single governing code, so the engineer assembles criteria from several documents, and strength and deflection are usually not the governing checks. Torsion, fatigue, local flange bending, and local web tension under the wheel typically control [71:43][73:14]. Practical underhung capacity is 10 to 15 tons because of local flange bending effects, with typical jobs running 1 to 7.5 tons [06:47].

### Technical Details
Design loads are maximum wheel load (bridge weight distributed to end trucks plus rated hoist-and-trolley load near one end truck), vertical impact 0 to 25% of wheel load, lateral side thrust 20% of rated capacity plus hoist and trolley weight (bridge weight excluded), longitudinal tractive 10% of maximum wheel load, plus end-stop force [21:14][22:14][24:15]. Side thrust and tractive forces act horizontally at the top of the bottom flange, perpendicular and parallel to the runway respectively, and are distributed to each wheel [49:39][77:54]. Bottom-flange loading increases lateral-torsional-buckling resistance, allowing up to a 40% C-sub-B increase per Structural Stability Research Council guidance (the example used a 1.4 multiplier) [32:30][33:34][60:09]. Deflection limits are L/450 vertical (wheel load without impact) and L/400 lateral [27:06]. Fatigue need not be checked below 20,000 cycles or where stress is fully compressive [42:57][43:27]; the worked example used ASTM A992, a W16x57 wide flange versus a W16x36 plus C12x20.7 cap channel [58:36][62:21].

### Fabrication Notes
The major shop cost driver is the runway splice: it needs a complete-joint-penetration weld at the bottom flange, ground smooth for a continuous running surface, with the backer rod removed and the CJP tested [51:49][52:50]. Leaving the backer rod in place drops the fatigue stress category (lower F-th and C-f constant), so removal is a fatigue requirement, not optional cleanup [46:04]. Cap channels add shop labor to weld two shapes together plus the channel weight, and do not resist lateral force or aid local flange bending, so a wide flange alone is often the lighter, cheaper choice unless the cap-channel section is roughly 20 lb/ft lighter [29:25][65:59][66:30]. Avoid notches, holes, cover plates, and transverse welds on the tension flange; clamped attachments are preferred over welded or bolted ones to limit stress risers [46:35][47:07].

### Erection Notes
Runway beams must be installed straight, parallel, and at constant elevation to the CMAA 74 tolerances, which are tighter than the AISC Code of Standard Practice and must be flagged to the contractor early and shown on the construction documents [26:22][55:25]. Sample CMAA tolerances: span 3/16 to 3/8 in, lateral runway straightness 3/8 in total with 1/4 in per 20 ft rate of change, and a tight differential-elevation tolerance between the two parallel runways [27:24][28:25]. Splices go near (but not at) vertical supports for safe access, away from the maximum negative moment [52:20]. Details should provide vertical, lateral, and longitudinal adjustability (shims, through-bolts with adjustable nuts, clamping) for rail alignment and to absorb dead-load deflection of the superstructure [55:25][55:56]. An initial runway survey verifies installed tolerances, and periodic maintenance must continue through service life [72:14].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The named recurring error is treating underhung design like top-running design and assuming strength or deflection governs, when torsion, fatigue, and local effects usually control [71:43][73:14]. Clients sometimes specify CMAA Service Class E or F for an underhung crane, which is wrong because E and F apply only to top-running cranes [43:59]. Unintended continuity introduced through connection plates between simple-span ends is a detailing trap [30:25]. Excessive deflection in the supporting structure is cited as a cause of a substantial number of crane problems and failures, so building members supporting the runway must meet the crane deflection criteria, not the looser L/250 to L/300 building limits [80:28][81:30]. A runway surface gap over the crane manufacturer's limit (often 1/16 in) causes wheel binding [83:06].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Splices are explicitly called expensive (CJP weld, grind, backer-rod removal, NDE), so continuous-span runways are favored to minimize them, and a checklist is recommended for repeat designs [30:56][51:49][74:15]. The example shows the cap channel forcing the wide flange up from a W16x57-class member toward a heavier W16x50-plus-channel build because of local flange bending, illustrating that adding material can raise both weight and cost rather than lower it [64:58][65:28]. Pre-engineered clamps are reported to save up to 17% versus fabricated clamps and carry manufacturer QC and dimensional/impact testing, where fabricated clamps have no standardized testing and depend entirely on shop workmanship [69:06][68:06]. Engaging the crane manufacturer and joist supplier early reduces rework risk, since SJI has no fatigue design standard for joists [55:56][56:30].
