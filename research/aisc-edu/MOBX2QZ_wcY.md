### Core Concept
This is session 1 of an 8-part NSBA / AISC night school on steel bridge design, presented by Anna Teague, Joann Shaner, and Dominic Coletti of HDR [00:01]. The session is a vocabulary-and-orientation overview: bridge nomenclature, the three main elements (superstructure, substructure, foundation) [02:41], bridge types, the typical superstructure plan-set sheets, framing-plan geometry, and a high-level survey of analysis methods. It teaches no design equations and almost no building-steel content, so its value to Your Company is conceptual literacy for any bridge or bridge-adjacent steel work, not a fabrication or connection standard.
### Spec References
Few codes are cited and none are AISC building specs. Named references: AASHTO LRFD Bridge Design Specifications as the governing highway-bridge code (full coverage deferred to session 2) [01:03][80:22]; AREMA Manual for Railway Engineering, specifically Chapter 15 Steel Structures, for railroad bridges, which follow allowable stress design (ASD) and the Cooper E80 railroad live load [80:52][81:24]; NCHRP Report 725, Guidelines for Analysis Methods and Construction Engineering of Curved and Skewed Steel Girder Bridges [77:16]; AASHTO/NSBA Steel Bridge Collaboration document G13.1, Guidelines for Steel Girder Bridge Analysis [78:17]; and the FHWA Manual for Refined Analysis [78:49]. No AISC 360 section, AISC 341 section, AWS D1.1 clause, RCSC provision, or Design Guide number is stated.
### Technical Details
Superstructure elements named: barrier, median, sidewalk, deck and wearing surface, deck forms, girders, cross frames, diaphragms, lateral bracing, and bearings [04:19]. Girder types: rolled beams (limited rolled sizes, short spans) and built-up plate girders with flanges, webs, and stiffeners for longer spans, plus box girders for torsional resistance on curved structures [24:04][25:07]. Bearing families: elastomeric (steel-shim reinforced), high-load multi-rotational (pot and disc, the disc using a molded urethane elastomer, often guided or non-guided), and mechanical (rocker and roller, now mostly rehab-only) [27:16][29:50]. Framing-plan geometry vocabulary: radial/normal versus skewed supports measured to the local tangent, contiguous versus staggered cross frames (contiguous is the term used in the AASHTO LRFD spec) [64:34][66:06], constant versus variable girder spacing, field splices, bearing and intermediate stiffeners, and girder bifurcation with a transfer beam or bulkhead diaphragm picking up an introduced girder [62:29]. Analysis methods: line-girder (1D), 2D grid, 2D plate-and-eccentric-beam, and 3D finite element, with refinement chosen to match geometric complexity [72:05][74:08].
### Fabrication Notes
Fabrication is touched only indirectly. Cross frames are individually designed built-up assemblies of angles and gusset plates and are described as expensive to fabricate, so designers balance their size and count [25:39][89:51]. Variable-width framing where every cross frame is a different length raises fabrication cost, which is the argument for grouping parallel girders so many cross frames repeat [56:17][57:19]. Girder elevations call out plate-size changes at field splices, for example a bottom flange going from 1-1/4 inch by 20 to 1-3/4 inch by 24 across a splice with a constant 3/16 inch by 62 web, and shear-stud spacing for composite action [37:36]. No fit-up tolerance, camber, or shop QC procedure is stated.
### Erection Notes
Girders are broken into shorter shipping pieces because US highway routes limit length, then erected and field-spliced in the air [43:48]. Stated shipping limits run as low as 130 feet, sometimes 150 feet or longer depending on the route from shop to site, which drove the splice layout on a roughly 200-foot single-span example even though it carries no contraflexure point [88:20]. Photos show curved and skewed girders erected before the deck pour, with overhang falsework and formwork in place and bolted field splices visible as dark bolt clusters at web and flange [47:58][48:28]. Lateral bracing in exterior bays is highlighted as needed for global lateral-torsional stability before the deck cures and acts as a composite shear diaphragm [69:14][92:31].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
No inspection failures, weld rejections, or bolting defects are named. The closest to an error theme is the engineering caution that a poorly laid-out framing plan with bad load paths causes design problems downstream [42:15], and that 3D finite-element models are more susceptible to hidden errors that are hard to detect [76:46]. This is a conceptual session, so treat it as having no inspection-failure content.
### Bid / Cost Implications
Cross frames are called out as expensive to fabricate, so reducing their size and count, and repeating cross-frame lengths through parallel-girder grouping, is a direct cost lever [89:51][57:19]. Field-splice count and location trade fabrication and field-bolting labor against shipping limits: more splices add bolted connections and field labor, fewer splices risk over-length shipping [87:16][88:20]. Analysis-method selection is framed as a time-and-money decision, with 3D FEA costing more effort than line-girder or 2D grid for simple bridges [76:46]. These are bridge-design economics, not Your Company building-bid rates.
