## Design of Curved Members with the New AISC Design Guide
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Jh9tjTDz4ms | Duration: 1:03:10 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
The session, presented by Bo Dowswell of ARC International (PhD on gusset plates, AISC committee member), previews AISC Design Guide 33, Design of Curved Members, then in development [02:08]. The central teaching is that curved members do not behave like straight members: a curved member under axial compression buckles in a side-sway mode rather than a half sine wave, and a horizontally curved beam under equal end moments develops flexure plus torsion rather than pure flexure [13:33][16:10]. The practical design method, however, converts the curved member back into an equivalent straight column or beam by using the developed (arc) length and tuned effective length factors, then runs it through familiar specification provisions [53:49][54:20].
### Spec References
The speaker cites AISC Design Guide 33 (curved members) and Design Guide 1 (base plates, anchor rods, washer plates, the practical-information model he wanted to copy) [02:38][22:30]. He references AISC Specification Section E3 for column flexural buckling, used for both in-plane and out-of-plane curved-member axial strength [56:58][60:32]. He names the AISC Code of Standard Practice, noting the 2016 edition explicitly adds straightness tolerances for curved members [41:54]. The British source is the Steel Construction Institute publication from 2001 [37:14]. No AISC 341 seismic, AWS D1.1, or RCSC bolting sections are cited in this webinar.
### Technical Details
Curve types named: vertically curved members, horizontally curved members, parabolic and elliptical (variable-radius) curves, off-axis curves, the s-curve (also called a reverse compound bend in roadway transportation), and the spiral or true helical curve [07:48][08:19][12:01]. Snap-through buckling is the governing in-plane concern: if rise-over-span H/L sub s is greater than 0.2, snap-through need not be checked; below 0.2 a design-guide table sets a minimum L/r (for example H/L 0.15 with pinned ends needs L/r greater than 36) [49:15][51:17]. The in-plane effective length factor K sub I depends on rise ratio and on circular-versus-parabolic and pinned-versus-fixed ends; a parabolic fixed arch uses K sub I of 0.5 (full sine wave) for all rise ratios [55:26]. Out-of-plane buckling is flexural-torsional, converted to flexural via a coefficient C sub o that carries the St. Venant torsion, warping constant, and out-of-plane moment of inertia, combined with the brace-segment angle theta sub B [59:02][61:40]. Local effects (flange bending, web buckling) matter only at tight radii, for example a 15-foot radius on a thin W21 flange [19:48].
### Fabrication Notes
Chapter 2 covers three primary bending processes: pyramid roll bending (cold, most common, three pyramid-arranged rolls with a supplementary roll to limit tension-flange distortion, multiple passes), incremental step bending (cold, cantilever force applied step by step), and induction bending (the only hot method, local induction-coil heating with immediate cooling around a pivot arm) [31:33][34:41][35:44]. Most steel fabricators only bend for camber on their own equipment and sub the tighter-radius work to a specialized bender-roller company [28:13]. To control distortion on an architecturally exposed (AESS) bent HSS, the bender roller may recommend a thicker wall, for example stepping up from a quarter-inch wall rather than redesigning later [26:41]. Material-property and brittle-fracture concerns arise only at tight radii combined with galvanizing and low-temperature exposed service [29:47].
### Erection Notes
The webinar is design and fabrication focused and gives little field-erection procedure. The one erection-adjacent point is support spreading: an arch on cantilever columns can spread at the supports, producing large apex deflection and pushing toward snap-through buckling, mitigated by a tension tie or a vertical truss [43:30][45:05]. No crane, rigging, sequencing, or field-bracing procedures are detailed.
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
No inspection-rejection list is given. The named failure modes are engineering buckling modes rather than fabrication defects: snap-through buckling (catastrophic in-plane flip-through, the example drops a 30-foot rise to 60 feet below), out-of-plane flexural-torsional buckling of an unbraced arch, and local flange bending or web buckling at tight radii [48:42][59:32]. Detailing errors are flagged indirectly: an off-axis curved member requires the detailer to dimension the rotation twist for the bender roller, and missing that geometry stops the bend [10:59][28:44].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Bent structural members are almost always subcontracted to a specialized bender-roller firm, which is a procurement and lead-time line distinct from in-house fabrication [28:13]. Each bender roller has different equipment and capability (induction, heavy strong-axis bends, spiral stairs needing wide rolls and large shop layout), so the right vendor must be matched to the curve type early [37:44][38:45]. Engaging the bender roller up front on wall thickness and achievable radius avoids redesign loops and distortion rework on exposed members [26:09]. The guide also gives developed (arc) length equations for parabolic curves so material quantity on a curved member is computed correctly rather than from chord length [25:06].
