### Core Concept
This part covers the analysis and modeling of steel lateral load resisting systems for wind, then transitions into metal deck design. The presenter walks through how to model moment frame joints and panel zone deformation, how to take the correct unbraced lengths for brace beams in concentric braced frames, how foundation rotational stiffness reduces drift, how to set serviceability drift and strength P-Delta limits, and how a low lateral load combined with high gravity load can produce a story stability problem even when a drift limit is met [00:02 to 29:58]. The closing section covers composite deck, form deck, and roof deck basics [30:28 to 32:32].

### Spec References
The presenter cites the AISC LRFD specification generically and names specific items: the alignment chart nomograph G factor (a base rotational stiffness of G equal to 10 for a reasonably fixed footing, and G equal to 1 for a so-called rigid foundation) attributed to a paper by Ted Galambos [11:25 to 12:55]; the 1999 LRFD spec brace-frame stiffness requirements [19:07]; the 2005 AISC specification direct analysis method in Chapter C and Appendix 7, which reduces member EI to 80 percent of gross and uses the notional load concept [19:37 to 21:08]. He also references the PCI Handbook for modeling footing and base-plate/anchor-bolt rotational stiffness [13:27], and his own 1993 Engineering Journal article on a drift damage index [21:40]. No bolting, welding, or seismic detailing sections were cited.

### Technical Details
For moment frame joints, the recommendation is to model center-to-center of joint rather than treating the panel zone as infinitely rigid, because a rigid-joint plus clear-span model underestimates drift from panel zone shear deformation; for tube buildings with columns at 10 to 15 feet on center, explicitly model panel zone deformation [00:33 to 03:41]. Brace beams in Chevron and X-braced frames carry both gravity and large axial load, so they are designed as beam-columns, and the controlling point is that torsional buckling (KLz) is often ignored by software postprocessors even though a slab can make KL minor zero; the fix is to trick the software by setting K minor to the actual torsional unbraced length (for example 30 feet, or 15 feet with a mid-span kicker) [04:13 to 09:51]. He warns against counting a non-fireproofed elevator divider beam as a brace point, and against the rigid diaphragm assumption inhibiting axial shortening of a brace beam [05:13, 09:51 to 10:52]. Serviceability uses a drift limit of 0.0025 (one over 400) under 10-year or 50-year wind, including P-Delta and composite stiffness; strength P-Delta is held to a Q of 0.25 (a 1.33 amplification), and under factored gravity Q is limited to 0.375 (1.6) [14:59 to 21:40]. The story stability index is Q equals P-Delta over VH; a narrow face with low wind area but full gravity mass can be a soft, unstable direction, as in the Boston Hancock building, which was stiffened and fitted with a tuned mass damper [24:15 to 29:28].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The errors named are analysis and design errors, not inspection rejections. The main ones are taking a beam-column joint as infinitely rigid and then using clear-span member properties, which underestimates drift [02:08]; designing a brace beam for KL major with KL minor of zero and forgetting torsional buckling as a limit state [08:17]; relying on a non-fireproofed elevator divider beam as a code-valid brace point [10:21]; letting a rigid diaphragm assumption suppress real brace-beam axial shortening [05:13]; and assuming a drift-limit-satisfying frame is automatically free of a story stability problem [24:45]. The Boston Hancock building is cited as a real case of an undetected stability and motion-perception problem [27:57 to 29:28].
