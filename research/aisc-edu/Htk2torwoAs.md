## Field Fixes - Part 8

### Core Concept
This session is a practical clinic on reinforcing, modifying, and field-fixing existing steel joists (SJI K-series and similar bar joists), not wide-flange framing. The speaker's central method is to first identify exactly what you are dealing with before touching anything: the web configuration (rod webs, crimped single angles, double angles, or cold-formed channels), the chord type (hot-rolled angles, cold-formed angles, or hat sections), and interferences such as deck above and walls behind [00:02][00:32][01:35]. He stresses that the right field fix is governed as much by access geometry and weld reach as by the calculated forces, and that material is cheap so the conservative reinforcement is usually the economical one [10:56][11:28].

### Spec References
The speaker cites SJI load tables for existing joist capacity [05:13], the SJI "75-year digest" printed in 2003 that compiles specifications and load tables for joists made over the prior 75 years (used to back out steel grade, often 36 ksi for chords and webs, when only the year is known) [05:13][06:15], and a forthcoming SJI technical/joist digest that will give reinforcement details and design procedures [18:10][20:53]. He references the joist tag now mandated by SJI to carry the manufacturer name [06:45], the 2005 AISC manual introduction of HSS design thickness versus nominal thickness (HSS supplied at about 0.90 of nominal wall) [09:20][09:51], the AISI deck thickness allowance of 95 percent of design thickness [08:50], ASCE 7 load combinations (he singles out the load-side complexity, not a specific section) [02:39], and OSHA bridging-termination requirements [28:37]. No AISC 360 chapter, 341 seismic section, AWS D1.1 clause, or RCSC bolting section is cited by number.

### Technical Details
Joist web members migrated from all-rod webs (old practice, roughly pre-1990s) to crimped single angles over the last 20 years, with the transition generally near a 20-inch depth and crimped webs typical by 30-inch K-series, though no exact cutoff exists [00:32][01:02]. Some makers now use cold-formed channel webs, and older bottom chords were occasionally rod, abandoned partly due to wind-uplift demands [01:35][02:08]. Joist members are ordered to thousandths of an inch (for example a 1-3/4 by 1-3/4 by 0.109 or 0.232 angle) and are not standard stock sections, so field measurement requires a micrometer, never a tape [07:46][08:19]. Bridging carries real in-service force: it laterally braces the bottom chord against wind uplift and stabilizes the web system, and bracing demands both strength and stiffness, which is why the end compression diagonal matters most [23:29][24:29][25:32].

### Fabrication Notes
Reinforcement design favors brute simplicity: for web reinforcing, design added angles or rods to carry the entire shear at that panel and weld them for the full load rather than apportioning between old and new members, which removes any dependence on existing weld quality [10:56][11:28]. Chord reinforcing is the exception where the speaker proportions load between chord and reinforcement for economy [11:58]. Welds on joist members must develop the strength of the member, an SJI principle that gives a minimum known weld capacity even when field welds cannot be measured [07:15][10:23]. Reinforcing rods are stuffed alongside chords and tied with flare-bevel welds; double-angle webs that nearly touch the top chord block rod insertion, so the diagonal end is sometimes cropped to make room [13:02][14:04][14:35].

### Erection Notes
Field work on roof joists is done from underneath because deck sits on top, so downhand fillet welds are easy on bottom chords but top-side access is constrained [13:33][15:36]. Reinforcing angles or plates must keep the work point so eccentricity is not thrown into the top chord [16:08][17:08]. For length errors, look past the joist itself: a joist a few inches too long or short may be cheaper to fix by moving the support beam at its column framing, or even shoving a footing, than by modifying many joists [18:44][19:16]. Bridging may be cut for duct penetrations only if X-bridging is added on either side of the cut, and bridging into a wall must terminate with two horizontals to the wall (which bend rather than break) rather than the X-termination OSHA shows, to avoid pulling joists out of the wall under uplift or snow [23:29][28:37][29:08].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named field failures include framing a joist into a beam at an angle of 30 degrees or less, which guarantees a bearing fit problem [21:55][22:27]; joists badly corroded over a chlorinated pool that were past reinforcing and had to be replaced [03:41][04:11]; and a Houston job where a contractor cut the end diagonals out of 20 joists to fit HVAC duct, destroying the most critical compression member, later patched by torching the duct and welding the diagonals back in [26:33][27:36]. He also cites bottom-bearing joists installed upside down [28:06]. Shoring during reinforcement is driven by safety, since heavy weld heat across a chord can sag the joist, not purely by stress [11:58][12:31].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Cost judgment runs through the talk: replacing severely degraded joists is often cheaper than reinforcing them, and ordering new joists when schedule allows beats reinforcing many in place [04:11][19:48]. Conservative full-load reinforcement saves design and inspection time because material is cheap relative to engineering hours and weld-quality risk [10:56][11:28]. Elaborate fixes (beveling outstanding legs, custom notched plates) are flagged as terribly expensive and to be avoided unless forced [14:35][15:06]. Cutting at joist center versus ends is partly an economic and ownership argument, since the party paying the bill usually drives the detail [19:48][20:53].
