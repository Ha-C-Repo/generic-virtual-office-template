## Safety by Design
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sep7EBrzIkI | Duration: 56:11 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This is a policy and practice webinar by Mike Toole of Bucknell University on Prevention through Design (PtD), which he calls Designing for Construction Safety (DFCS) or Safety by Design [04:08]. The thesis is that structural engineers and architects can voluntarily make decisions during design and detailing that make a building easier and safer to erect, the same way constructability and quality are designed in [04:39]. Toole frames the case three ways: an ethical duty under the ASCE and NSPE codes of ethics to hold paramount the safety of the public, including workers [08:15]; a sustainability triple-bottom-line argument where worker safety is the social-equity leg [09:18]; and a dollars argument of lower workers comp, fewer delays, and higher productivity [10:51]. Research is cited loosely: studies linking roughly 22 percent and 42 percent of construction fatalities to design factors, attributed to Mike Behm of East Carolina University and a European study, presented as contributory not causal [07:13].

### Spec References
This session is about safety policy, not connection design, so it cites almost no AISC or AWS technical sections. The one explicit code reference comes from an audience structural engineer at [49:39] who notes the AISC Code of Standard Practice requires the designer to identify the lateral-load-resisting system, and observes that bid documents rarely comply, leaving it vague or missing. Named external instruments and bodies, none being an AISC 360/341 or AWS clause: the UK CDM (Construction Design and Management) regulations of 1994-95 revised 2007 [13:26]; OSHA, NIOSH, and NIOSH's NORA Construction Sector Council [04:39][15:58]; the CII (Construction Industry Institute) design-for-safety checklist tool built by Jimmy Hinze with about 457 items [22:39]; Australia's free SHARE/CHAIR-style work product [35:22]; and the NISD (National Institute of Steel Detailing) and National Erectors Association joint safety-in-design manual for steel [25:40]. General principles, no AISC section cited.

### Technical Details
The steel-specific design measures are detailing moves, all common-sense and low cost: pre-punching holes in columns at the right locations for safety rail and intermediate rail so erectors can stand a guardrail as soon as the deck is on [44:36]; adding clips on columns so ironworkers can tie off when working a cantilever [44:36]; avoiding hanging connections in favor of seated/bearing connections so the ironworker is at less risk while landing the first two bolts [26:42]; chamfering sharp corners; and leaving enough clearance at connections to fit a torque wrench [27:13]. Built-in fall-protection anchorage in the structural frame is the headline low-hanging fruit, useful for both erection and later window washing and maintenance [11:53]. A non-steel example is raising parapets a few inches above the code 30-inch minimum so the parapet itself becomes fall protection [25:10].

### Fabrication Notes
The strong recurring theme from the fabricator and erector voices in the room is that the fabricator and detailer, not the design engineer, are the right party to embed erection-safety features, because they know the erection sequence, the standards, and the field practice [54:48]. One fabricator describes columns arriving with holes whose purpose a junior structural engineer could not explain, which is exactly the detailing knowledge that should drive the safety features [55:18]. Prefabrication is presented as a primary safety strategy: moving work off elevation to the ground or, better, into the shop where machines and automation do it, reduces exposure and often saves time and money [12:25]. Modular work platforms and lifting points fabricated on the ground and set in place are given as the Bechtel example [30:46].

### Erection Notes
Erection safety is the entire point of the design moves above. The lived erector account at [44:36] is the clearest: pre-located rail holes and tie-off clips let a worker stay safe without climbing down to order and rig temporary barricade that is often not on site. Bechtel's process embeds temporary platforms, lifelines, lifting clips, and climbing rungs in the model so erection is easier and faster [30:46]. The CDM discussion stresses the constructor side: a site-specific erection plan plus a site-specific safety plan and a hazard analysis as a legal duty [50:41]. Multiple speakers insist personal responsibility and ironworker ingenuity remain first, and that schedule pressure is what makes crews cut corners, which good design should relieve rather than replace [44:06][52:13].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
No weld rejections, bolt-installation failures, or NDE findings are discussed, since this is not an inspection talk. The named failure is a documentation and compliance gap: the AISC Code of Standard Practice requirement to identify the lateral-load-resisting system is rarely met in bid documents, showing up vague or missing [49:39]. A second recurring failure mode is the fabricator not being given all the information needed to make the critical connection [46:37]. The broader systemic error Toole names is the industry's slow uptake driven by liability fear, the 800-pound gorilla, and by structural engineers lacking any safety training in their education [17:29][20:36].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Toole concedes a real added cost: designers need training, design takes more time, and there is QC overhead for safety review, so the work is hard to sell to a low-bid owner [19:33]. His counter is life-cycle: a dollar spent in design fees can save ten in construction and shorten schedule, which lands best with sophisticated owners, design-builders, owner-controlled insurance program (OCIP) owners, and process/industrial clients, and poorly with commercial design-bid-build [20:04][29:14]. A fabricator at [47:07] makes the operational point directly: if a fabricator builds the safety features in, that cost can be included in the price, so it is a quotable line of scope rather than a hidden burden. Middle East process clients are cited as already refusing proposals that do not show a prevention-through-design program [36:23].
