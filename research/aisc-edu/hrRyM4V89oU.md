## What Engineers Need to Know about Steel Erection
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hrRyM4V89oU | Duration: 1:03:16 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Curtis Mayes, P.E., director of pre-construction and engineering at LPR Construction and a member of the Research Council on Structural Connections since 2006, argues that the engineer of record can remove most field erection risk before the construction team is even on the job, mostly by specifying intent rather than relying on assumptions. The thesis is that erectability is a design decision: connection choice, anchor-rod sizing, tolerance management, weld access, and a clearly drawn lateral load path all get locked in on the contract documents, and a constructible building costs less and is safer to put up [02:42][14:30]. The talk is structured around specifications, safety, connections, lateral force resisting systems, and sharing the BIM model with the fabricator and erector [03:00].

### Technical Details
Tolerance accumulation is the recurring failure mode. On an HSS 16x8x3/8 rolled to an arc, the cold rolling changed the section to roughly 15.5 inches tall by 8.375 inches wide, so 3/8 inch of width growth across about 20 bays accumulates into a major top-of-steel mismatch unless intermediate dimensional QC checks are specified [06:00][08:04]. Two behavior case studies: an athletic facility where the EOR specified a column detailed 1 inch out of plumb and a truss fabricated 1 inch short so that removing the shores pulled the column plumb under dead load [09:11], and a seven-bay crane-rail mill building whose arched roof spread the perimeter columns up to about 1.5 inches under dead load, throwing crane-rail alignment off [11:04][12:58]. Slots manage this: short slots placed sparingly, on one beam end roughly every five bays for beam-to-column-flange lines and roughly every ten bays for web connections, keep the building in line without forcing the erector to space every member [34:41][frame 14]. For long-span trusses, orient the top-chord web horizontal so the section is stable to lift; a 145 ft, 160,000 lb truss with a W14x370 top chord is unliftable web-vertical and erectable web-horizontal at the same weight [36:32].
### Fabrication Notes
Preferred shop connections favor single-sided erectability: shear-tab and extended shear-plate connections are easy to land, while shared-bolt through connections and beams into both column webs slow the erector even when they are cheaper to fabricate [21:41][22:54]. The favored column splice is four shop-welded inner plates on square-cut upper and lower shafts with bearing transfer and slip-critical bolts, aligned to the inside flange so W12 and W14 sizes match, avoiding any field bevel cutting [28:46]. Keep bolt diameters at least 1/4 inch apart and avoid two bolt grades at one diameter, because a 1 inch and a 7/8 inch bolt look nearly identical and a 1 inch bolt can drop into a 7/8 inch oversized hole, creating QC and traceability errors [37:57][frame 15]. Shop welding is normally gas metal arc or submerged arc with bare-wire electrode, which suits the enclosed shop with no wind to blow shielding gas away [59:54]. Specify oversized holes in all plies (not just one) on complex frames so pins land on theoretical geometry with full adjustability [31:28][frame 13].
### Erection Notes
Preferred free-standing column support uses a single center shim pack with the anchor rods left to rock and plumb the column, anchor rods extended outside the base-plate footprint, and an extra inch or two of base-plate edge distance reserved for field slotting if a rod is off [24:19][25:31]. With a shear key, use leveling nuts and leveling plates rather than shim packs on the pocket edge, which can spall and create a hazard [26:18]. Provide two temporary through bolts at brace connections instead of OSHA's one-bolt minimum so the erector can pin the first hole and bolt the second safely [20:04]. Extend one claw angle about 3 inches so braces rotate into a gusset without a sandwich-clearance fight [30:00]. On steel deck, avoid single-span deck (ironworkers call it a widowmaker) and prefer screws or button punch over welded side-laps where diaphragm demand allows [57:53]. Field welding is normally flux-cored arc with a gooseneck stinger; stick welding is now only incidental in the field [59:26]. LPR modeled cranes, rigging, modules, shoring, and bracing inside the fabricator's shared Tekla model to plan erection [49:28].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The headline field failure was the seven-bay crane-rail building: the arched roof spread the columns under dead load, so crane girders sitting on the column seats had to be physically repositioned, described as a mess; the speaker's stated root cause is the arched roof geometry, which a straight tee-to-tee roof would have avoided [60:53][62:39]. Other named errors are tolerance accumulation that surfaces three-quarters of the way through a job and forces rework [14:20], anchor rods designed only for the OSHA 300 lb at 18 inch load, which the slides show as 0.6 ft-kip versus 9.72 ft-kip at 15 psf wind, eight to sixteen times too small for real wind [18:50][frame 8], slots placed everywhere driving up cost and reducing quality, mismatched 1 inch and 7/8 inch bolts landing in the wrong holes, and overhead welds specified by default when the underside weld is often not structurally required [40:04][41:25].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The fillet-weld cost table (priority 5 slide) prices a 100 kip load at $55.00 per labor hour and sets 5/16 inch fillet as the 0 percent cost baseline at one pass; going to 3/8 inch jumps to two passes and 67 percent more cost for only an incremental capacity gain, and a 1 inch fillet runs 11 passes at 244 percent over the 5/16 baseline [43:31][55:55][frame 22]. The practical bid rule is 5/16 inch is the optimum and typical maximum field fillet, because production is governed by passes, not throat. Short slots are nearly free because they stay bearing-type and avoid slip-critical surface prep and pretension cost; the only penalty is a slightly reduced bearing capacity per bolt [53:55]. Hurricane load planning is an extraordinary measure that roughly doubles to 2.5x normal construction wind pressure, so the EOR should keep it as builder's risk per COSP 7.10.3 or call for separate pricing [15:56]. Truss top-chord orientation and connection choice change erection hours at identical material weight, so they are estimating inputs, not just analysis details [36:32].
