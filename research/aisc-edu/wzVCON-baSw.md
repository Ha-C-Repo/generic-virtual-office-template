## Stiffeners and Doublers - Oh My!
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wzVCON-baSw | Duration: 1:27:18 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Carol Drucker (Drucker Zajdel Structural Engineers) teaches why doubler and stiffener controversies arise on projects and how to prevent them through correct design, detailing, and clear assignment of design responsibility [01:00]. A doubler is a web reinforcing plate; the main case is the column panel zone at beam-to-column flange moment connections, where the offset top and bottom flange forces drive an increased horizontal shear (shear flow, VQ/It) across the zone between flanges [02:01][04:34]. A stiffener (continuity plate) reinforces the column at the same connection for the local flange limit states. The controlling design move stressed throughout is to verify the actual panel-zone shear against the column strength rather than assume full beam moment capacity, because that assumption can require doublers on nearly every column [34:58][35:29].

### Technical Details
Doubler weld configurations: flush doubler groove-welded into the column radius, or a thickened doubler prepped to clear the radius and fillet-welded to the flange; the effective thickness of a thickened doubler is the thickness at the toe of the fillet, not the full plate [06:42][07:45]. Thin doublers are 1/4 to 3/8 inch, thick over 3/8; thin can be square cut with limited radius override, thick must be prepped (30 degrees common) [08:48][09:50]. AWS D1.8 prequalifies a 30-degree prep with no radius override; override is allowed if three macro-etch tests show fusion and no cracking [11:54][12:25]. Panel-zone strength reduces sharply when axial load approaches the squash load (alpha PR over Py above 0.4 triggers reduction) [31:57][74:47]. Stiffener minimums: width one-third beam flange minus half column web; thickness the greater of width/16 or half the element delivering the force; partial-depth stiffeners acceptable except web crippling now needs 3/4 depth in the 2016 spec [47:58][49:32]. Stiffener eccentricity limited to 2 inches with effectiveness interpolated from 100% to 65% per DG13 [58:48].
### Fabrication Notes
The fabricator-friendliest detail is the thickened doubler with a single-pass 5/16 fillet to the column flange; the offset doubler with a spacer plate is the least fabricator-friendly [05:38][16:38]. Ask the fabricator early for the maximum doubler thickness before splitting into two plates, typically 1/2 to 5/8 inch [06:42]. Increasing doubler thickness to provide land for a single-pass fillet can beat increasing the fillet leg size [07:45]. Stiffeners are clipped at the web to clear K detailing and at the flange to clear k1; welding in the k area is allowed for wind/low seismic if the clip is 1.5 inches past the radius and the weld stops short by one weld size, because rotary-straightened W-shapes have low toughness there [51:05][51:35]. Fitting both stiffener ends to bear is time-consuming (fit, file, repeat), so usually only one end is finished to bear with a 5/16 fillet on the other [62:19].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The recurring failure is UT rejection of doublers groove-welded into the column radius: as the doubler overrides the radius a gap forms behind it, producing questionable UT results; the speaker cited a project where the shop inspector insisted on UT, several welds failed, and the team requalified them as PJP by accounting for root Z-loss [10:52][14:04][14:36]. Welding in the k area has caused cracking of W-section members in fabrication [51:35]. A design error named is missing the panel-zone shear entirely because 3D line-element analysis (the speaker showed RISA output) reports constant column shear with no panel-zone spike [30:56]. Over-conservatism by specifying full beam moment capacity instead of actual moments is flagged as a job-killer [31:57]. Outdated references (the old red book plug-weld rule based on the 1978 spec) cause unnecessary requirements [40:38].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The office cost study (14x90 column, two W24x55 beams, 350 kip-ft, half-inch stiffeners and doublers sent to multiple fabricators) found: thickened doubler prepped to clear the radius saved 25% over the groove-into-radius baseline; upsizing the column ~20 lb/ft to W14x109 saved 15%; the cheapest option in every case was upsizing the column to drop the stiffeners and using a thickened doubler with a single 5/16 fillet [22:29][24:03][25:07]. Eliminating both stiffeners and doublers was 8% more costly because heavier 14-columns above W14x132 jump over $200/ton [25:38][20:53]. The seismic manual notes upsizing a column 50 to 100 lb/ft for a clean shaft is often cheaper than doublers [19:50]. On a price-per-ton (unit weight) job, the upsize-and-add-tonnage option pays the fabricator more while cutting shop labor, so it is the fabricator-preferred choice [25:07]. Pricing across fabricators varied widely, so confirm with the actual shop [24:34].
