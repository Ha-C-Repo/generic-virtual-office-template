## Design of Frames Using Web-Tapered Members
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nRJoDUtlCz4 | Duration: 1:02:05 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
This webinar (session E9) walks through AISC Design Guide 25, "Frame Design Using Web-Tapered Members," presented by Dick Kaylor of Computerized Structural Design and Don White and Yuck Kim of Georgia Tech [01:41][16:43]. The guide fills a gap left when the 2005 AISC specification dropped the older George Lee web-tapered provisions from the 8th and 9th editions [02:43][06:49]. The unifying method is stated in one sentence: map the elastic buckling strength of a web-tapered member to the elastic or inelastic buckling strength of an equivalent prismatic member [17:13]. The mapping turns on a parameter gamma-e, the multiplier on the applied loads that brings the member to its elastic buckling state, evaluated where flange stress f-r over Fy is highest [08:21][19:16].

### Spec References
Cited references are AISC Design Guide 25 (the subject document) [02:12]; the AISC 2005 specification column strength equations, with the inelastic-elastic transition at KL/r limit 4.71 times sqrt(E/Fy), equivalently F_e/Fy at the 0.44 limit [17:44][18:46]; AISC 2005 Chapter A (design basis terms), Chapter C (stability, second-order analysis), Chapter D (axial tension), and beam-column interaction equation H1-1 [07:19][08:21][40:58]. Speakers also cite the AISC Commentary story-buckling concept and the B2 amplification approach [25:27], and an AASHTO 2007 Cb equation written in terms of stress rather than moments [11:24][37:44]. The frame is identified as ASCE 7 Seismic Design Category B and C, R equal to 3 [57:11]. The successive-approximations (Marks) method from 1920s-1930s Timoshenko is referenced for in-plane buckling of non-prismatic members [13:29][51:57].

### Technical Details
The scope is limited to a maximum taper angle of about 15 degrees; beyond that the user is extrapolating [05:46][50:23]. The method avoids K factors entirely by computing the elastic buckling stress F_e directly and plugging it into the 2005 column equations [12:25]. For out-of-plane checks each unbraced length is treated as a simply supported segment with K equal 1, using I_y at the segment midspan [33:10][69:00 region near 33:40]. Torsional buckling need not be checked for a doubly symmetric section when K_z*L is less than or equal to K_y*L [34:11]. Constrained-axis torsional buckling (the speakers joke "cat buckling") is the controlling case when the top flange is braced at close spacing (purlins or joists at 5 ft) while the bottom flange has kickers at much wider spacing such as 20 ft; the real unbraced behavior falls between the two and is computed at the segment midspan cross-section [13:59][35:42]. The example is a modular metal-building gabled frame with 55 ksi steel, interior leaning columns pinned at the base, exterior double-symmetric tapered columns at a 4.37 degree taper, and slender or non-compact webs and flanges checked cross-section by cross-section [20:50][21:22].

### Fabrication Notes
The document is oriented toward metal-building-manufacturer practice, where members are heavily computerized built-up plate girders rather than rolled shapes, so fabrication is implied to be shop welding of tapered plate sections [04:15][05:16]. An audience question raised that most of these sections are welded on one side of the web; the speakers noted one-sided welded prismatic tests at UT Austin and in the Lee research, with residual-stress patterns that can add some benefit but also introduce welding distortion [58:42][59:15]. Research imperfection magnitudes used were on the order of 1/1000 for local distortions [60:49][61:23]. No AWS D1.1 clause, electrode, preheat, or NDE requirement is cited; the welding discussion is research context, not a fabrication spec.

### Erection Notes
No erection sequence, crane, rigging, or field-bracing procedure is taught in this webinar. The only field-relevant content is the bracing assumption baked into the analysis: top-flange bracing from purlins or joists at roughly 5 ft on center and bottom-flange kickers at wider spacing, which the constrained-axis torsional buckling check models [13:59][35:42]. Brace point locations and the unbraced-length definition drive the member checks but the talk does not cover how those braces are installed in the field.

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
No weld rejections or bolt inspection failures are named; this is a design-methodology talk. The closest analog is design-side error: assuming a fixed effective length factor (the old habit of "K is 1.5, doesn't matter how many leaning columns") gives a unity check of about 0.68, while a proper effective-length method that knocks down Cb for the many leaning columns yields 1.1, a violation, against 0.75 from the direct analysis method [44:42][46:16]. The lesson is that ignoring leaning-column destabilization understates demand. The speakers also caution against checking a thousand points along a tapered member; instead check only where f-r over Q*Fy is maximum and at segment ends and midspan [40:25][54:35].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The direct analysis method gives substantial savings over the effective-length method for non-rectangular gabled geometries, and for wide modular frames with heavy gravity load on many leaning columns the sidesway amplification can exceed the 1.5 limit that bars the effective-length method outright [47:49][48:20]. On rafters the two methods land at practically the same strength ratio, with the direct analysis method often a shade higher (about a 0.01 penalty) because it adds frame-imperfection moments [46:47][49:20]. The method is presented as easier to program and integrate into in-house spreadsheets or software because the successive-approximations buckling solution avoids eigenvalue packages [13:29][47:49].

For Mario's shop the one-sided web-to-flange weld and the distortion-versus-residual-stress trade-off [60:49] matter for fit-up and camber control if such work is ever quoted, governed by AWS D1.1 in our own WPS rather than anything stated here. For an in-house PE stamp the takeaway is that any tapered-member or heavily leaning-column gabled frame should be designed by the direct analysis method, not a guessed K factor, since the 1.5 sidesway limit and leaning-column knockdown can flip a passing check to a failure [45:14][48:20].
