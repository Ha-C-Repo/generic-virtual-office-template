### Core Concept
This segment is welding theory and detailing: the transition from CJP groove welds into PJP groove welds, then into fillet welds, with the throat dimension as the controlling design quantity throughout. The central teaching is the split between the shop dimension and the engineer dimension. The engineer specifies the effective throat (E, "engineer's dimension") needed to carry load, while the shop chooses the depth of preparation or bevel (S, "shop's dimension"), the welding process, and the included angle, as long as the required E is delivered [04:41][07:48]. For fillet welds the same logic holds: leg size is specified in the US, but the throat governs performance [17:11].
### Spec References

The flare-bevel research is attributed to Dr Jeff Packer, University of Toronto [15:08]. The 2005 code edition is named for the flare-groove changes [09:51].
### Technical Details
Effective throat E equals depth of preparation S only when the included angle is large enough to get fusion to the root; for SMAW a 60 degree V-groove gives E = S, a 45 degree bevel gives E = S minus 1/8 inch, and angles below 45 degrees are not permitted because reliable root fusion is lost [06:46][07:16]. Penetration to the root is not itself required; only the effective throat carries stress, so reducing the included angle to save weld metal is acceptable as long as E is met [05:43][06:13]. For flare-bevel welds on cold-formed tubular members, throat is a function of bend radius (roughly 2 times material thickness, but mill practice varies and should be checked); filled flush, throat ranges by process from 5/16 R for SMAW and self-shielded FCAW up to 5/8 R or 3/4 R for GMAW and gas-shielded FCAW due to greater penetration [10:23][15:39]. AISC allows underfilling a flare groove: specify the throat, leave an unwelded X dimension measured by depth gauge off the face, subtract X from the flush-fill throat [12:29][16:09].
### Fabrication Notes
A separate welder qualification test is required for each backing material: steel backing, ceramic, or copper, and the WPS must reflect the backing used; only steel backing is prequalified, so copper or ceramic backing requires qualification by test [00:01][01:03]. Single-sided joints generally use steel backing; double-sided prequalified joints require back gouging, where the root pass is removed by grinding or air arc gouging into sound weld metal before the second side is welded [00:33][01:34]. Air arc gouging uses a copper-coated carbon electrode with compressed-air ports that liquefy and blow away the metal [01:34]. On shop drawings the depth of preparation is detailed as dimension S; root face (the "land") is the unfused portion and the root opening is the gap [02:35][03:07]. Larger flare-groove throats mean more weld volume, more distortion, and more cost, so flush filling is not always necessary [11:25].
### Erection Notes
This segment is shop-welding and detailing theory and does not cover field erection sequencing, bracing, or rigging. The flexibility provisions on fillet terminations carry a downstream field implication: weld arrangements must preserve the flexibility assumed in the connection design, and stiffener hold-backs of four to six times web thickness are allowed in part for flexibility during shipping to avoid cracking at the fillet termination [29:32][30:35].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The named abuse is welders melting away the corner of lapped material so a full-size weld appears made while the actual throat is short; J2.2b requires leaving 1/16 inch of edge on thicker material so the corner is visibly intact, and the rule was extended from "lap joints" to "along the edge" to capture more cases [20:51][23:25]. A recurring over-correction: an inspector ordering a welder to grind 1/16 off oversized corner welds full length, which only makes the joint stronger and is a poor corrective action [22:23]. The maximum-edge rule is widely misapplied to T joints where no edge exists, such as a tube-to-base-plate fillet that legitimately exceeds the tube wall thickness to reach the required throat [23:55][24:25]. On skewed T joints, measuring the total external leg as if it were the throat undercounts the real throat when a root gap is present [25:27][25:57]. Welding toward an edge tends to undercut it due to uneven heat conduction, a workmanship concern behind the hold-back rules [29:02].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Weld volume drives cost and distortion, and the talk repeatedly favors the minimum weld that meets the required throat rather than flush filling everything: oversized flare-groove and back-to-back tube details produce very large welds with no benefit [11:25][11:55]. Underfilling a flare groove to a specified throat reduces both cost and distortion [12:29]. Minimum-size tables (J2.3 for PJP, J2.4 for fillets) frequently govern over the calculated size, so the practical weld is often larger than design demand for heat-input and crack-control reasons, which raises labor on light members [08:18][19:19]. Skewed-joint preparation that bevels the cut to close the root gap is more costly and is not routine practice [25:27].
