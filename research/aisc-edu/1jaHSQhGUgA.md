## Load Paths! The Most Common Source of Engineering Errors
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1jaHSQhGUgA | Duration: 1:24:20 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
Carol Drucker (Drucker Zaidel Structural Engineers, AISC TC6 connections committee) teaches that load applied anywhere on a structure must travel through a complete, continuous path of elements down to the foundation, and every element along the way needs sufficient strength and stiffness [01:31][02:02]. The two governing rules of thumb are that the shortest load path is the best, because it also minimizes piece count and cost, and that force is attracted to stiffness, so members carrying more load get the shorter spans [03:03][04:07][04:39]. The recurring failure mode is a load path that "slips through the cracks," meaning the engineer designs a joint without tracing how force actually crosses it, which is why she calls load path the most common source of engineering errors.

### Spec References
The cited references are: AISC Specification Chapter K, which mandates the thicker and wider HSS member be the continuous member at a welded truss joint [07:42]; AISC Specification Section J1.2, which requires connections to allow simple beam rotation (so welded-up shear tabs should be avoided) [72:33]; AISC Manual figure 7-7, used to uncouple a column moment over a bolt group for KISS-method gusset-to-column-flange connections [78:00]; AISC Design Guide 13, on stiffener welds where the column-flange weld is treated as more rigid than the column-web weld [61:42]; and the 14th Edition Seismic Design Manual plus the AISC website "companion" for Chevron connection examples, which add a shear-through-the-doubler limit state [47:36][48:07]. She also names the Uniform Force Method as the AISC-preferred bracing approach and references Bill Thornton and Pat Fortney's work on adding transfer forces to Hc forces [33:00][36:04].

### Technical Details
The talk works through trusses (top and bottom chords acting as beam flanges, web diagonals as the web), vertical and horizontal bracing, Chevron connections, moment connections to column flanges and webs, sloping columns, kickers, davits, thermal-slip connections, and discontinuous columns carried back on vertical trusses [09:15][25:43][50:13][59:03][65:51]. For bracing she contrasts the Uniform Force Method (Hc, Vb, Delta-Vb forces distributed around the joint, gusset laid out in AutoCAD or Excel) against the KISS method (vertical component to gusset-to-column, horizontal component to gusset-to-beam), noting UFM special case 2 to a column web exactly equals the KISS method [33:29][41:43]. Panel-zone shear is found by assuming an inflection point at column mid-height, and the key insight is that the sum of beam moments cannot exceed the sum of the column moment strength, which can drastically cut the doubler design force [55:53][57:26]. Transfer forces, diaphragm strength, and the equal-transfer-force-on-each-side-of-a-column rule recur throughout [16:56][17:27].

### Fabrication Notes
Welded trusses are favored because they can be fabricated whole in the shop and erected in one piece, and a continuous stiffener directing the chord tension force across a panel zone eliminated the need for stiffeners and doublers on one job [30:22][30:53][31:24]. Drucker stresses that all welds in a stiffener group should be the same size to avoid deformation-compatibility problems, and she believes this is somewhat mandated in the spec [62:45][63:16]. On a price-per-ton Canadian job the EOR spliced members "as soon as they could be spliced" to cut tonnage, and she warns against splicing at joints because material is often ordered before the resulting connection problem is caught [14:23][15:24].

### Erection Notes
"Watch erection" is one of the two stated lessons: a roof-truss plan created a stability loop (T5 through T9, T8, T3 back to T5) because a stub column was placed in the wrong truss, fixed in the field by adding a shoring post and re-sequencing the erection (C5, then T3, T8, T9) [05:11][06:12]. Advanced detailing software (Tekla, SDS) is credited with making field rework less common than the old practice of designing connections for a year then doing three months of field fixes [72:02]. A field fix offered for a shear tab with mis-drilled holes was to add a tab on the backside and drill new holes through the beam, decided jointly with the fabricator [80:18].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named failures include a pool-deck kicker collapse the day before opening, traced to 5/16-inch clip angles with a few hundred kips of compression that pulled away from the column [11:51][12:21]; the David Lawrence Convention Center 2007 expansion-joint failure where bolted-welded double angles had slots too short for thermal movement, putting a moment across the fillet weld root [69:58][70:29]; and the Hyatt-style hanger-rod change where splitting continuous rods into two nuts overloaded the lower nut [70:29][71:32]. Other rejections covered are coped-beam reactions exceeding the UDL force and the beam shear strength [07:42][08:12], shop drawings returned with an X over a truss-rotation detail because the EOR had braced the column off the bottom chord [28:18][28:48], and Chevron connections missing the shear-through-the-doubler check [48:07].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Shortest load path equals fewer pieces equals lower fabrication and erection cost, which is the through-line of the talk [03:35]. The concrete cost data point: switching an entire job from the Uniform Force Method to the KISS method (because the EOR refused to touch the beam-to-column connection) added 14 percent more bolts and bumped every brace-to-column-flange angle up one size, for example a 5/8-inch angle going to 3/4-inch [38:36][39:08]. She frames the bolts-versus-thicker-angles decision as a trade-off to coordinate with the fabricator, and notes price-per-ton contracts push EORs to minimize tonnage in ways that complicate connections [39:39][14:23].
