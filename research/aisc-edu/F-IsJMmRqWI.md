### Core Concept
This session is a fast overview of column base plate and anchor rod design, framed as a preview of the reissued AISC Design Guide 1 authored by Jim Fisher and Larry Kloiber [00:32]. The base plate is presented as a critical element because it is the interface between the steel frame and the foundation, and a frequent earthquake failure point where bases rotate inelastically and fail in a non-ductile manner if not detailed properly [01:33][02:05]. The speaker walks through three load cases (axial compression or tension, axial plus shear as in braced frames, and axial plus moment as in moment frames), bearing and bending of the plate, anchor rod tension development, shear transfer, and the combined axial-plus-moment case ending in a quadratic solution for the bearing length Y [09:49][29:24].

### Technical Details
Base plate steel options given: A36 available to 8 in thickness then a lower grade above that, or A572 Gr 50 to 4 in and Gr 42 from 4 to 6 in, with A36 preferred as more economical and increasing thickness preferred over increasing strength [03:36][04:06]. Anchor rod material of choice is F1554 Gr 36 because it is weldable; Gr 55 can be special-ordered weldable; Gr 105 is not weldable under any circumstances [07:13][07:45]. F1554 is color coded blue for 36, yellow for 55, red for 105, with a white paint mark at the embedded end for weldable Gr 55 [09:17]. For high uplift, the preferred load path extends anchor rods up the side of the column into a pocket so the rod takes tension directly to the foundation, leaving the base plate to carry compression only and sparing the perimeter weld [13:20][14:23]. ACI Appendix D shear limit states named are anchor shear strength, concrete breakout in shear, and concrete pryout, with pryout generally not governing for the deep 18 to 72 in rods used in steel buildings [20:41][21:42]. Combined shear and tension uses an Appendix D interaction surface, linearized with a free zone where small tension may be ignored [24:18].

### Fabrication Notes
A36 plate is the shop default to control cost; higher grades are reserved for architectural thickness limits [04:06]. Fillet welds are always preferred over PJP or CJP welds for cost wherever forces allow, with PJP or CJP used only when fillets cannot carry the load [04:36]. In high seismic zones, CJP is preferred over PJP because of the built-in root defect in a PJP [05:07]. Galvanized anchors and nuts must be purchased from the same supplier and shipped as a matched assembly [08:46]. The combined-action anchor rod calculations near concrete edges are described as nasty and error-prone, justifying an in-house spreadsheet because the hand calc is worth doing only once [23:14][23:46].

### Erection Notes
Grout transfers base plate load to the foundation and is normally non-metallic; metallic grout is reserved for vibratory equipment and costs more [05:07][05:37]. Grout strength by cube test at 28 days is kept at twice the supporting concrete strength so the A2/A1 bearing increase is always satisfied without rechecking [05:37][06:09]. Double-nutted anchor rods in outdoor or exposed conditions are not grouted, because trapped grout cracks, holds moisture, and corrodes the rod, which is why highway light poles are nutted rather than grouted [06:09][06:40]. Post-installed anchors suit only very small loads and are discouraged for building columns unless drilled and epoxy grouted [08:15]. Anchor rod placement should use a template so rods land in the right location and shear can be designed straight through the rod [26:19].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Earthquake column base failures occur when bases are not detailed for inelastic rotation: the plate fractures, the anchor rods rip out, or high brace-frame shear is not handled [02:05]. Modeling a base as purely pinned or fixed while ignoring real rotational stiffness produces errors in computed drift and P-delta [03:06]. A missed failure path is the inclined 1 to 1.5 tension crack at the embedded nut, where lost development length on the hooked rebar up the pier means the uplift is not carried [17:31][18:36]. The speaker repeatedly flags that concrete contractors frequently set anchor rods wrong, requiring exotic field fixes covered in a separate lecture [25:49][26:19]. An edge-loaded heavy column can fail along a corbel-like diagonal plane that some engineers do not recognize [26:51].

### Bid / Cost Implications
Cost guidance is explicit: A36 plate over A572, thickness over strength, and fillet welds over PJP or CJP all reduce cost [04:06][04:36]. Taking large uplift through the base plate and perimeter weld is called too expensive a place to spend money; routing the rod up the column side is cheaper and stronger [13:20]. Four-inch rods at Gr 105 or the special high-strength grade are noted as very expensive and as demanding heavy foundation development [07:45][08:15]. For high shear, the office default is to bite the bullet with heavy anchor rods and shear friction rather than detailing shear lugs, because lugs invite both concrete placement and weld errors [25:18][25:49].
