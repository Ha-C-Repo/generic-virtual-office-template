## To 3 or Not To 3
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hJVsZLSvfTA | Duration: 1:23:00 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
The session, presented by Pat Fortney and John Hooper, examines the decision to use a high-R seismic system (anything above the R equal 3 "steel system not specifically detailed for seismic resistance") versus staying at R equal 3 in low and moderate seismicity. The central message is that choosing a high R factor to cut base shear is not free: once you claim a high R you must satisfy every strength and detailing requirement that comes with it, in both AISC 341 and ASCE 7 [03:08]. Fortney covers what the system seismic factors (R, over-strength Omega0, deflection amplification Cd, and his own ductility reduction factor Rd) physically mean and which systems are permitted by seismic design category and height [03:41]. Hooper then runs two real cost-benefit case studies (Oklahoma City and the Javits Center) showing when the R equal 6 trade actually saves money [46:08].

### Spec References
ASCE 7-16 Table 12.2-1 (system factors, height limits by SDC) [10:58][20:41]; ASCE 7 Chapter 15 (non-building structures with R equal 3) [23:14]; ASCE 7 rule that two systems in the same direction use the lesser R [54:52]; AISC 341 (seismic provisions, F2 for buckling-restrained braces, the 2010 and 2016 editions referenced for shear-stud exceptions and gusset weld changes) [02:36][27:23][31:01]; AISC 358 (prequalified connections for IMF and SMF, RBS, bolted flange plate, Welded Unreinforced Flange-Welded, with a Connection Prequalification Review committee) [08:18][37:42][42:55]; AISC 360 (the basis for R equal 3 design) [23:45]; AWS D1.1 (general welds), AWS D1.8 (seismic supplemental welder certification and demand-critical weld requirements) [07:16][39:48]; ACI 318 (special concrete shear wall detailing, boundary elements) [55:24].

### Technical Details
Systems compared by R, Omega0, Cd, and base-shear reduction versus R equal 3: ordinary concentric braced frame R 3.25 (about 7.7 percent reduction) [15:01]; special concentric braced frame (SCBF) R 6 (50 percent reduction, Cd 5) [16:01][27:53]; eccentrically braced frame (EBF) R 8, Omega0 2 (about 62.5 percent reduction, link yields in shear or flexure) [16:32][24:48]; buckling-restrained braced frame (BRBF) R 8, proprietary brace to AISC 341 F2, Rd of 4 [35:08][72:00]; ordinary moment frame R 3.5 (14 percent reduction); intermediate moment frame R 4.5 (33 percent reduction); special moment frame (SMF) R 8 (62.5 percent reduction) requiring strong-column weak-beam [17:35][36:09]. Fortney stressed that "ordinary" carries a seismic meaning (R above 3) and must not be used loosely on design criteria [17:35]. For SCBF the net area at a slotted HSS-over-gusset connection must be at least the gross area of the brace, so reinforcement is usually needed at the transition [32:32]. Connections are sized for the expected tension strength of the brace under both expected-buckling and post-buckling compression load cases [31:31].

### Fabrication Notes
Protected zones must be marked on the shop and erection drawings by the fabricator; for an EBF the entire link is protected, for an SCBF the center quarter of the brace plus the full gusset and any part of the connection touching the column or frame are protected [26:51][29:28]. Only "approved work" is allowed in a protected zone: welding HSS to the gusset and an erection hole in the brace end are approved, but welding a stray clip angle to the gusset face for lifting is not [29:28]. Gusset-to-beam (and west-coast gusset-to-column) welds are demand-critical and as of AISC 341-2016 must develop the contact shear strength of the gusset, which typically forces large CJP welds [31:01]. Demand-critical welds raise filler-metal and consumable cost and require additional testing [39:16]. Continuity-plate thickness rules relaxed in 2016 (one-sided can be 50 percent of beam flange, two-sided 75 percent), and thinner plates make the CJP to the column flange easier to pass ultrasonic test [41:53].

### Erection Notes
For SMF bottom-flange-to-column welds there is restricted access: the first weld pass must be completed before the second, made in halves on each side of the web and tied together, which AWS D1.8 covers with a supplemental welder certification that the erector must hold [06:46]. Stability bracing is required at the expected plastic-hinge location in an SMF beam plus supplemental points so the beam can reach its plastic moment, adding infill-beam connections [40:52]. The presenters warn to confirm the erector and fabricator have genuine seismic experience before selecting a high-R system in a low-seismic region, since local crews in low-seismic areas often have not built, fabricated, or erected seismic systems [05:15][46:08]. Fire-watch-heavy jurisdictions like Manhattan push the choice toward shop welding over field welding to avoid escalated field-welding fire-watch cost [07:47].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The recurring real-world error: an engineer of record uses a high R to cut base shear but then detailes and fabricates the frame as if it were not seismic, which is incorrect [02:36]. AISC 358 non-proprietary prequalified connections are prequalified only for the beam framing to the column strong-axis flange, not to the column web (weak axis); the presenters have seen weak-axis framing wrongly assumed prequalified on jobs [38:46]. Loose use of the word "ordinary" on design criteria is flagged as a trap [17:35]. ASCE 7 footnotes and exceptions throughout (not only Table 12.2-1) are described as routinely missed and important for both design and the PE/SE exam [21:43][46:40]. A slide typo claiming 62.5 percent reduction for SCBF was corrected to 50 percent live [27:53].

### Bid / Cost Implications
The connection-force factor is the key estimating lever: an SCBF connection is sized on brace tension while the brace member is sized on compression, so the connection demand runs roughly 1.5 to 2.5 times the R equal 3 demand, making every plate bigger [57:27][59:00]. A BRBF cuts that factor to about 1.25 to 1.5 because its tension and compression capacities are closer, a cheaper connection if the whole system is R 8 [59:32][78:28]. The cost-benefit weighs steel-connection premium against savings in base shear, foundations (deep drilled shafts and caissons scale directly with base shear and overturning), and diaphragms (collectors amplified by Omega0 in SDC C). Oklahoma City: going R equal 6 over R equal 3 saved an estimated 0.5 to 0.75 million dollars all-in [63:41]; Javits: 1 to 1.5 million dollars [76:56]. These quick studies were done in roughly one to six weeks of front-end analysis, and having the fabricator and erector on a design-build team to confirm the connection premium made the decision much easier [73:22].
