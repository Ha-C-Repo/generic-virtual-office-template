## Fundamentals of Connection Design: Fundamental Concepts, Part 2
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=h6bMo3AweJA | Duration: 1:28:33 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
Brad Davis (University of Kentucky, AISC Committee on Manuals and Textbooks) teaches the second of four sessions, covering eccentrically loaded bolted and welded connections, directly loaded tension connections worked as a full light-bracing-connection example, and compression connections (beam bearing plates and column base plates) [01:34]. The unifying method is the load-path walkthrough: trace force from the member through each component to the support and check every applicable limit state, where the weakest limit state controls the design strength [12:23][13:26][31:34]. He repeatedly contrasts the older elastic/vector method (conservative to very conservative) against the instantaneous center of rotation (ICR) ultimate-strength method, which is more rational and is what the AISC Manual design tables actually use [02:34][76:29].

### Technical Details
Eccentric groups use coefficient C from the table times the single-bolt or per-16th weld strength; an eight-bolt 3/4 inch A325 bracket at 8 inch eccentricity gave C = 2.93 and 52.4 kips [05:05][06:39]. Shear lag U = 1 - xbar/L, with xbar from Table 1-7 for angles; a long-leg-back-to-back double angle gave U = 0.85, a 15 percent rupture reduction [34:38][71:08]. Net area subtracts an effective hole diameter of bolt diameter plus 1/8 inch (1/16 to standard hole, another 1/16 effective), so a 3/4 inch bolt uses 7/8 inch [33:36]. Block shear takes the minimum of shear rupture (0.6 Fu Anv) or shear yield (0.6 Fy Agv) plus tensile rupture (Ubs Fu Ant), Ubs = 1 for directly loaded connections [22:40][23:44]. The Whitmore section spreads load at 30 degrees from the connection ends to set the critical-section width [28:55]. Web local yielding uses critical length Lb + 2.5k (end) or 5k (interior) with K-design, not K-detailing, and phi = 1.0 [50:39][51:40].

### Fabrication Notes
The session is design theory and does not walk shop processes, but several inputs are direct fabrication and detailing parameters. Hole sizing is explicit: a standard hole for a 3/4 inch bolt is 13/16 inch nominal, and the effective diameter for net-area math is 7/8 inch [40:15][33:36]. Bolt stagger geometry (the s-squared-over-4g term) is a detailing decision that adds net-section strength and must be modeled when present [21:06]. The K-design versus K-detailing distinction matters because the smaller K-design decimal governs bearing-plate cantilever length and web checks, and using the larger detailing value would be unconservative [51:40][59:27]. Weld size enters as D, the number of 16ths (a 5/16 weld is D = 5), and the per-inch shortcut value of 1.392 kips per 16th per inch applies at theta = 0 [11:21][47:30].

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The speaker names physical failure modes seen in lab specimens rather than inspection rejections: block-shear tear-out of a plate or angle along two longitudinal shear lines plus one transverse tension line [22:07][22:40], and a Whitmore-section tensile rupture at the end of a stiffener in a hanger-type test where the far plate carries almost no stress [28:55]. The worked bolt-by-bolt shear-transfer example predicts the realistic mixed failure: bolt A tears out the angle end, bolt B fails in bolt shear rupture, bolt C tears out the plate end [43:19]. A live-call correction warns to cross out the erroneous 0.85 in the second concrete-bearing term on the handout, a genuine errata catch [71:46]. The polling questions flag two common misconceptions: that Part 7 and 8 tables use the elastic method (they use ICR) and the scope of the Whitmore section [76:29][78:08].

### Bid / Cost Implications
No pricing, schedule, or estimating content is presented. The indirect cost lever is that the ICR method baked into the Manual tables recovers capacity the conservative elastic method would leave on the table, so designing eccentric bolt and weld groups off the tables yields fewer bolts or shorter welds for the same demand, which trims fabrication material and labor [76:29].
