## Field Fixes - Part 5

### Core Concept
This webinar covers field and shop problems on composite floor systems and their fixes, organized around three themes: beam camber and its interaction with deck and concrete, shear stud welding and inspection, and shop paint defects. The unifying lesson is that camber tolerances are workmanship guidelines, not absolutes, and that uncontrolled deflection, camber mismatch, and coating errors all produce expensive field rework. The speaker draws repeatedly on real composite-floor jobs (hospitals, Class A office midrises, parking ramps) to show how small misjudgments at fab become change orders in the field.
### Spec References
The speaker cites the AISC Code of Standard Practice as the document that first added camber tolerances in its prior edition (he names "not the current 2005 Edition but the previous Edition," roughly 2000 or 2001), where camber was previously only in the manual [01:34]. He gives the camber tolerance as minus 0, plus 1/2 inch for most beams, with an additional increase over 50 feet, measured in the unstressed shop position [01:34][03:37]. He cites AWS D1.1 for stud qualification (manufacturer qualifies the stud for the deck) and for the fillet-welding-studs procedure and minimum weld size [13:22][15:58][18:31]; he notes he sits on an AWS D1.1 subcommittee. On stud cover he contrasts the old ASD requirement of 3/4 inch cover over the stud top against the 2005 specification requirement, which he states as cover over the stud top (he misspeaks the LRFD value but cites the 2005 spec change) [19:01][19:34]. Paint cleanliness is cited by SSPC surface-prep grades by their shop shorthand: SP2 hand-tool, SP3 power-tool, and SPC1 solvent clean [22:37][23:39][26:15].
### Technical Details
Camber is induced mechanically by running the beam through a machine past yield, backing off, and measuring set; uniformity across like beams is approximate and verified by a string line and mid-ordinate tape measurement [02:05][02:36]. Camber loss in shipping was studied historically (a Bethlehem Steel study, attributed to "Larson") but was never conclusively resolved, and the speaker reports no significant field loss except possibly on very light members under 100 percent composite with heavy stud counts [04:08][05:09]. Shear studs are checked for a full 360-degree weld flash at the base, then bend-tested or ring-tested; a few bad studs change composite strength so little they are not worth losing sleep over [15:28][15:58]. He points to an Engineering Journal article by John Ruddy on level floors and ponding as the reference for floor-levelness practice [10:17].
### Fabrication Notes
The shop target is minus 0, plus 1/2 inch camber, but the speaker treats it as a workmanship band: a beam coming in at minus 1/4 inch on a 2.5 inch specified camber will not be sent back through the machine [03:07]. Fillet-welded studs require grinding off the protrusion on the stud base so it seats tight with no gap before welding [15:58]. Paint defects are diagnosed by cause: mill-scale lifting traces to incomplete loose-scale removal under SP2/SP3; paint lifting traces to surface contamination, notably forming lubricant on HSS, which needs an SPC1 solvent clean; runs and sags from too-thick a coat or wrong vehicle; mud cracks from overbuilding the coating in the flange-to-web fillet; pin holes and chipping from top-coating inorganic zinc before full cure [23:08][26:15][29:23][30:24]. He recommends staying inside the paint manufacturer's mil thickness and matching vehicle to substrate (high-solids on smooth HSS, more vehicle on mill-scaled wide flange), and prefers epoxy zinc over inorganic zinc when a shop top coat is required because inorganic zinc needs high humidity and long cure [27:19][28:23][31:26].
### Erection Notes
Cambered beams or joists adjacent to non-cambered supports (moment frames, end walls, bearing walls on concrete or masonry) create deck-bearing problems where the deck will not seat on the adjacent support [01:03][05:09]. On a Rosemont/Chicago job with moment frames parallel to fill-in beams and cellular deck, the field fix was to saw-cut the deck so it acts as a simple span rather than continuous, which is acceptable if strength and deflection still satisfy [05:40][06:13]. After-the-fact floor openings during tenant build-outs can convert deck to a single-span condition that was not designed for it; one job deflected 2 to 3 inches and was saved only because the GC shored it [06:43][07:15]. Heat straightening to remove field camber is possible but rarely done by erectors and needs a specialist or a fabricator-written detailed procedure with supervision [18:31].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Over-cambering: on a hospital floor with an operating-theater opening, members at the unloaded opening edge ended up about 2.5 inches high so studs protruded above the concrete and had to be cut off [00:01][00:31]. Excess concrete from beam deflection when chasing a level pour: a medical facility using 7-inch normal-weight concrete (chosen for vibration) on near-zero-camber beams accumulated 3 to 3.5 inches of extra concrete mid-span and required field-welded cover plates as a change order [07:45][09:16]. Laser-screed level floors thinned slabs below fire-rating cover because camber did not come out as calculated, forcing sprayed fireproofing on the deck underside [12:19][12:51]. The catastrophic case: a parking-ramp cover by a miscellaneous-metals subcontractor without a stud machine fillet-welded studs on embedded plates that also carried moment, and the structure collapsed six floors straight down [16:59][17:59].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Several failures here landed as change orders: cover plates welded in the field to deflected beams, sprayed fireproofing added when laser-screed slabs ran thin, and shoring to rescue an over-deflected single-span deck. The speaker notes that some GCs historically figured concrete volume on theoretical slab thickness and then back-charged for the extra concrete that deck and beam deflection demanded, so drawings should carry a note about extra concrete for deflection [09:16][09:46]. Paint scope is a cost lever: most steel that is enclosed in finish or fireproofed should ship bare or with only an SP2 hand-tool clean and standard primer, avoiding unnecessary paint cost and rework [21:36][22:37].
