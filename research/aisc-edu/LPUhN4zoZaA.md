## Bracing Connections

### Core Concept
This is AISC Live Webinar Session 4 of the "Connection Design for Moment Frames and Braced Frames" series, presented by Dr. Brad Davis (University of Kentucky) on March 11, 2020, covering brace-to-gusset and gusset-to-frame connection design [00:48]. The talk splits into basic bracing connections, a single gusset connected to one member, and heavy corner connections where a gusset ties into the beam-column corner and carries enormous loads [01:53]. The unifying method is to trace every limit state from the brace side across to the supporting member, then for corner gussets pin down the indeterminate internal force distribution with the uniform force method and the lower-bound theorem [38:39]. The recurring tool for sizing the critical plate section is the Whitmore section, a 30-degree load spread off the connection length [08:35].

### Technical Details
The Whitmore section spreads the brace load 30 degrees each side over the connection length; where it runs off the plate edge it does not control and the gross plate width times thickness governs [09:39]. Plate compression uses J4.4 yielding when Lc/r is at or below 25, otherwise E3 buckling with Lc = KL, and K is taken from DG29 examples, often 0.5, sometimes 1.2 for a gusset welded only to the beam [11:57][86:51]. For corner gussets the six interface forces (Hc, Vc, Mc, Hb, Vb, Mb) are statically indeterminate; the lower-bound theorem lets any statically admissible and safe force field be used, and the force field giving the highest predicted strength is closest to true [42:46]. The uniform force method eliminates gusset moments by routing all resultants through the work point, with alpha (face of column to connection center at the beam) and beta (top of beam to connection center at the column) constrained by equation 13-1: alpha minus beta tan(theta) = eb tan(theta) minus ec [49:18]. Slip resistance is reduced by the ksc factor (0.697 in the example, about a 30 percent reduction) because the applied tension component pulls the plies apart [29:26].
### Fabrication Notes
For an HSS brace, the common shop process is to slot the brace, fit it over the gusset, set one erection bolt, then field-weld the HSS [04:27]. T-stem or claw-angle connecting elements are shop-welded to the brace and field-bolted to the support [05:03][37:03]. The gusset is usually shop-welded to the beam and field-bolted to the column, via an end plate, a single shear-type plate, or double angles [37:46]. Fillet welds are preferred over CJP for the long gusset-to-beam interface to save cost [56:16]. One non-obvious fabrication rule: the gusset-to-beam fillet weld is increased by 25 percent for non-uniform force distribution even when the design says no moment, citing the Hewitt-Thornton work referenced as the Chris U and Bill Thornton paper [55:28]. Weld effective length often needs engineering judgment, and the speaker knocks a quarter inch off the computed length where the plate runs in at a sharp angle and the weld is not fully built out [16:13].
### Erection Notes
The slotted-HSS-over-gusset detail is explicitly an erection sequence: slot, drop over gusset, one erection bolt, then field weld [04:33]. End-plate and shear-plate gusset-to-column connections are field-bolted while the gusset-to-beam weld is shop work, so the corner assembly is partly shop and partly field [36:51]. The brace itself field-bolts to the gusset [37:12]. The talk does not cover crane, rigging, temporary bracing, or erection-stability sequencing; its erection content is limited to which connections are field versus shop.
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The headline field problem is detailers throwing horizontal short-slotted holes into the gusset-to-column shear plate (and the beam-to-column shear tab) by habit, because they look like ordinary single-plate shear connections, when in fact these carry large axial transfer force; the speaker is on a live job with about 50 such connections needing reinforcement [52:53][53:24]. Root cause is a communication gap: the engineer of record often hands the fabricator only the brace axial load, omitting the beam reaction Rb, the transfer force Ab, and whether the column is HSS (needed for Qf), so the detailer has no idea axial exists in that joint [50:51][90:43]. Single-bolt brace connections are discouraged because block shear and shear-lag (connection length near zero) make them very weak [91:56]. Slotted holes in a beam-to-column joint generally will not work even slip-critical because per-bolt slip force is only about 9 kips [90:11]. Forgetting the beam-to-column connection at the corner is named as a frequent and costly omission [03:50].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The choice of force field directly affects connection economy; the KISS method zeros Hc and Vb for a quick safe answer but typically yields heavier, less economical connections than the uniform force method [44:02][46:39]. The 25 percent weld increase on the gusset-to-beam interface adds weld metal on every heavy gusset and should be carried in any connection-weld takeoff [55:28]. Heavy corner connections can drive large beams (a generous spandrel beam is preferred over a W8 because Vb can hit 200 to 300 kips) and may require column web stiffeners; in the worked example the W14x99 column failed web compression buckling and needed added stiffeners, a real fabrication add [75:55][52:42]. Incomplete EOR force data (only brace axial given) generates RFIs and rework, the kind of scope risk that belongs in a bid clarification rather than absorbed silently.
