## Erection Engineering Basics 101
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jctrXmHRfd8 | Duration: 1:00:34 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Will Jacobs of Stanley D. Lindsay defines erection engineering as engineering tied to developing an erection sequence, in collaboration with the fabricator and erector, that keeps the structure stable under all applicable temporary loading at each stage of construction, plus effective communication of that to fabricator, erector, and GC [01:00]. The work consists of designing temporary bracing and shoring, reviewing and modifying the permanent system to carry temporary forces, and reviewing connection design forces to create alternate force paths or upgrade connections [01:30]. He stresses that the structural drawing note "the structure is stable only in its completed form" assumes the building appears fully built, while the real problem is getting from point A to point B safely [03:33]. The two recurring themes are collaboration and communication, especially listening to the ironworkers who implement the plan [02:01].

### Technical Details
Construction wind is the controlling load. The bare frame behaves like a row of separate logs, each with its own drag factor of roughly 1.8 to 2.0 for a standard beam, so cutting pressure in half and then doubling it by drag lands back near the permanent pressure [13:48]. ASCE 37 shielding is very conservative: no reduction on the first three rows and only 15 percent on the fourth and later rows [14:19]. Pressure varies with velocity squared, so a 20 to 30 percent velocity change can swing force 70 percent [11:46]. Reduction factors in ASCE 37 let a sub-six-week inland job take 0.75 of permanent speed, with the caveat that erecting during hurricane season in a hurricane-prone area forbids the reduction and demands ability to re-brace within two or three days [09:39]. The continuously monitored daily work period is commonly set near a 30 mph reported (cell-phone-app) wind, converting to roughly a 45 mph three-second-gust design [11:16]. Member axial capacity changes drastically with stage: a 35-ft W18x35 with no deck holds about 20 kips axial, about 52 kips with metal deck, and about 250 kips with a concrete slab because buckling shifts to the strong axis [35:45].
### Fabrication Notes
The cheapest fixes are made in the fab and design phase, not the field. Upsizing an anchor rod is minuscule in material cost since the labor is the same: a 3/4-inch Grade 36 rod carries about 6,800 pounds shear versus nearly 40 kips for a 1-1/4-inch Grade 55 rod, roughly six times stronger [28:32]. Connection type drives temporary capacity, so connections should be laid out anticipating temporary axial loads, ranked end-plate or moment best, then double angles, single plates (strong in tension, weaker in compression), single angles poor, and seated connections having nothing [33:40]. Feeding revised member forces to the connection designer as a simple PDF markup before connections are designed avoids costly redesign or, worse, rework after detailing and fabrication [48:06]. A swap from W18x35 to W16x36, one pound per foot heavier but about 50 percent better in axial, is a cheap fabrication-stage change [36:47].
### Erection Notes
The Code of Standard Practice leaves "determine" to the erector, so not every strip mall needs a sealed plan, but oddball framing, long-span trusses, and stability-sensitive metal buildings warrant an erection engineer [05:04]. Sequencing should start at the braced bay and move outward, which often conflicts with a GC who wants to start at one end, so engaging the GC early on sequencing prevents temporary-bracing problems [42:29]. Daily piece-count capacity, from an informal survey of erectors, runs about 30 to 35 columns and main beam lines and about 40 joists for low-rise, and about 25 columns and main girders, 35 filler beams, and four to five fabricated truss assemblies for mid-rise, with hydro cranes cutting counts 5 to 20 percent and high-profile or miss-fab work reducing counts further [44:33]. The temporary period per ASCE 37 runs from the first column until the building is clad and becomes the permanent diaphragm condition [56:22]. Cable bracing lashed to column bases is inconsistent; a stop angle resists the choke riding up the column, confirmed in pilot tests at Virginia Tech where low cable angles broke the cable while higher angles produced slip [37:17].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Undersized anchor rods are the named recurring failure: a 14x300-something base plate with an HSS14 brace on only four 3/4-inch rods worked in the final loaded condition through friction but fails when setting the column unloaded [28:02]. Cable braces pulling up and sideways bend or rupture anchor rods before grout or encasement, and the lever arm grows badly with shim packs and no grout [26:31]. A jersey-barrier deadman is deceptive: a 3,600-pound barrier pulled at 50 degrees yields only about 600 pounds of usable load because the uplift kills friction, the mechanism that collapsed the Indiana State Fair stage in August 2011 under about a 60 mph wind [30:04]. A 2011 Texas collapse drew an OSHA citation for lack of ASCE 37 planning [16:19]. Misaligned holes patched with field weld are shown as a connection-quality failure [33:10]. Inspection is itself a gray area: ASCE 37 does not speak to inspection requirements, so the firm self-inspects at key stages [54:13].
### Bid / Cost Implications
Erection-engineering scope must be set in the bidding stage, because carrying erection-engineering money when competitors do not can lose the job, so the bid package should flag whether a sealed erection plan is genuinely required [50:40]. Bids on larger jobs should include a construction wind study, described as inexpensive with strong return, since a recent study gave a 43 percent force reduction under six weeks and about 20 percent for longer periods, which flows directly into lighter bracing, connections, and temporary foundations [51:10]. Bids should also carry allowances for added connection-design load cases, extra temporary brace detailing and fabrication, and base-building modifications, the last of which can sometimes reduce cost by making erection more efficient [52:10]. Early design-assist involvement lets the erection engineer influence the permanent design and eliminate or reduce temporary bracing cost [24:59].
