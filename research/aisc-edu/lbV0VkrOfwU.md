## Bolting & Welding Primer - Part 12

### Core Concept
This closing session of a bolting and welding seminar covers three linked topics: combined bolts-and-welds load sharing, seismic connection design philosophy, and a full worked design example of a bolted brace gusset plate that finishes on gusset-plate buckling. The recurring theme is checking strength components successively (transverse weld, then longitudinal weld, then bolts) and designing connections by tracking real deformations rather than assuming uniform stress [01:02]. The example ends with the buckling check that controlled the tested specimen, comparing three published methods against a measured ultimate load of 164 Kips [08:17][21:36].
### Spec References
The speaker cites the AISC Code of Standard Practice (referred to as "AIS CPC") as the basis for the combined bolt-and-weld rule and says he prefers a method from the AISC Engineering Journal for upgrading existing structures [00:31][01:02]. He references the net-section / shear-lag relation U = 1 minus (eccentricity / length) with net area S^2/4G times Fu for tension members [02:38], FEMA 350 plus AISC seismic design for connection forces [04:09], and a current AISC Engineering Journal paper on gusset-plate design [19:34]. No AISC 360 chapter, table number, AISC 341 section, or Design Guide number is stated verbatim, so those are general principles, no section cited.
### Technical Details
Transverse welds are effectively ineffective in shear because they fracture early and lack ductility, so the combined rule gives roughly 25% of the slip load (with pretensioned bolts) plus about 50% of the bolt ultimate shear [00:01][00:31]. Frames are classified as ordinary, intermediate, or special moment frames, with design based on achieving a target rotation or story drift [03:38]. High-strength bolt shear strength does not depend on rate of loading, so the static capacities apply directly to seismic conditions [04:09]. The brace-gusset example tracks slip load, bolt shear, plate bearing, block shear, and gusset buckling [08:17].
### Fabrication Notes
The discussion is connection-design and analysis oriented, not shop-process oriented, so explicit fit-up and shop-QC steps are not covered. The one fabrication-adjacent point is that you usually cannot connect the whole cross-section and typically only connect the flanges, which disturbs uniform stress and forces the shear-lag accommodation in the net-section calculation [01:34][02:06]. Bolt holes in the example are standard holes with no oversize-hole discount applied [09:49].
### Erection Notes
This session does not cover field erection sequencing, bracing, or rigging. The closest field-relevant content is the seismic-slip philosophy: connections are detailed so a moderate earthquake produces no slip and a major earthquake allows slip into bearing without catastrophic collapse, which governs how the installed connection behaves [05:43][06:13].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The speaker flags a conceptual error rather than an inspection rejection: assuming the historical Whitmore method checks buckling when it only checks yielding, and using a P/A plus or minus My/I beam-type stress check on a gusset that does not predict ultimate capacity and is sometimes non-conservative [16:29][17:31][18:03]. He warns that mixing bolts and welds in the same faying surface is undesirable because the welds do not deliver enough ductility, and that seismic connections give the designer no choice but to pretension all bolts and prepare faying surfaces as slip-critical [06:44][05:43].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The example is deliberately an unbalanced design set up to force a buckling failure, so bolt, bearing, and block-shear capacities are far higher than needed, which the speaker notes invites the reaction "I could take two bolts out" [13:24][14:26]. The practical cost guidance is engineering-effort scaled to volume: predicted-to-test ratios near 1.23 mean a single connection can be left conservative and shipped, while designing many identical connections justifies refining the buckling method to recover capacity [22:09]. This maps to a fabricator deciding how much detailing time a repeated detail warrants.
### Your Company Application
For Ivan's takeoff and the in-house PE stamp, this reinforces that braced-frame gusset plates need a buckling check (Thornton or modified Thornton), not just the Whitmore yield check, before a connection design is stamped, and that braced-frame and SCBF-type gussets carry that hidden buckling demand into the estimate.
