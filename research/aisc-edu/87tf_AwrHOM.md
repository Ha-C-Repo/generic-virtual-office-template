## SteelDay 2013: The Life of the K-Factor
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=87tf_AwrHOM | Duration: 1:04:42 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Charlie Carter, AISC vice president and chief structural engineer, traces the column effective length factor K from its empirical origins to its modern role, where current AISC stability methods let the designer set K equal to one [05:13][45:47]. K is the multiplier on actual column length L that gives the buckling length KL: less than one for rotation-restrained ends, one for pinned-pinned, and two for a flagpole or cantilever column [02:37][03:39]. The unifying theme is that any stability method must satisfy "five things": flexural, shear, and axial deformations from first-order displacement; second-order effects (P-big-Delta at connections and P-little-Delta along members); geometric imperfections (initial lean and curvature); stiffness reduction due to inelasticity (residual stresses); and variability in component and system stiffness [05:44][09:51][43:15]. This is a design-engineering and code-history lecture, not a fabrication or erection talk.
### Spec References
Cited explicitly: 2005 and 2010 AISC specifications Section C1.1 for the five stability requirements [05:13]; Chapter C and Appendix 7 as the home of the prescriptive stability methods [36:34]; AISC Code of Standard Practice for tolerances, including Figure C7.5 and the one-in-500 (0.002) column out-of-plumb limit [06:45][07:17][53:29]; AISC Design Guide 28 (Larry Griffis and Don White, stability design) released December 2013 [10:22][39:08]; ASTM A6 for the W-shape straightness basis of about one in 950 versus the code-assumed one in 1000 [08:18]; and the Engineering Journal paper "A Comparison of Frame Stability Analysis Methods in AISC 360," Carter and Geschwindner, third quarter 2008 [49:53]. Historical specification milestones named: the first AISC spec 1923, then 1936, 1961, the 1963 spec where K became explicit, 1969, 1978, 1989 ASD, and the 1986, 1993, and 1999 LRFD specs [04:41][21:40][25:42][28:45].
### Technical Details
The three prescriptive stability methods are the direct analysis method (new in 2005), the effective length method, and the first-order analysis method [37:05]. Direct analysis requires a second-order analysis (or first-order plus a B1-B2 amplification), notional lateral loads of 0.002 times the gravity load at each level or node to model the one-in-500 lean, reduced stiffnesses EA-star and EI-star (generally 0.8 times EA and EI, with a tau-b factor often but not always equal to one), and strength-level (LRFD) load combinations [39:39][40:42][41:45]. For ASD the designer multiplies loads by 1.6, analyzes, then divides results by 1.6; the 1.6 (not 1.5) reflects the nonlinear growth of second-order deformations at strength-level drift [41:45][50:24]. The effective length and first-order methods are limited to structures with calculated B2 less than or equal to 1.5; direct analysis has no such limit and is favored for moment frames, mixed brace-and-moment systems, and irregular industrial or process structures [55:35][56:06]. The 1962 Kavanaugh alignment charts (originally the 1959 Julian-Lawrence charts) gave the graphical K, with separate sway and non-sway cases [22:10].
### Fabrication Notes
No fabrication, fit-up, or shop QC content. The lecture stays on analysis and member-strength theory; the only fabrication-adjacent fact is that residual stresses lock into a W-shape as the section cools (extremities first), with rolling and straightening altering the distribution, and welded shapes showing a different pattern than rolled shapes [08:49][09:20].
### Erection Notes
No erection procedure, sequencing, crane, or bracing-of-record content. The single erection-relevant item is the one-in-500 (0.002) maximum column out-of-plumb tolerance from the Code of Standard Practice that the notional-load and lean assumptions are built on; columns near elevators or the facade carry tighter tolerances, which the code conservatively ignores for stability [07:17][53:29]. If a field survey establishes the actual erected lean, the designer may model that geometry directly or scale the notional load instead of using the 0.002 maximum [53:29][54:32].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
No bolting, welding, or shop-inspection rejections are discussed. The errors named are design and code-history failures of judgment. The 1989 ASD spec omitted stability advances already in the 1986 LRFD spec, fostering the mistaken belief that stability was an LRFD-only concern [26:44]. The John Hancock Tower in Boston is cited as the dramatic case where design outpaced the code: stability was blamed for glass facade panels popping out [28:14]. The deeper point is that few if any of the alignment-chart assumptions behind a hand-calculated K are actually satisfied in real frames, so K was accepted because it did something rather than nothing, and leaning columns can drive K well above one [24:42][59:16].
### Bid / Cost Implications
No direct estimating, schedule, or dollar content. The economic thread is historical: construction-management influence and economic evaluation pushed framing toward lighter sections, longer spans, and limited lateral framing, which raised the demand for rigorous stability analysis [27:14]. The practical takeaway for a design office is that direct analysis with K equal to one simplifies moment-frame design and avoids fragile alignment-chart K calculations, which is an engineering-hours and rework consideration rather than a fabrication cost [42:45][55:03].
