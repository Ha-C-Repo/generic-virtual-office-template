## Structural Fire Engineering
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hsJEqMUDFac | Duration: 1:29:06 | Upload: NA | Tier: 2 | Watched: 2026-06-24

### Core Concept
Alan Jowsey teaches structural fire engineering as a performance-based alternative to picking a prescriptive hourly rating off a code lookup table [32:34]. The thesis is that fire is a load case in its own right with its own load combination, equal in standing to wind, snow, seismic, and vibration, and it should be designed for rather than left as an afterthought [43:19], [80:13]. The workflow is three steps: define the fire load, run heat transfer to learn how the steel heats up, then run a structural analysis at elevated temperature to check stability [07:21]. The central design lever is the limiting steel temperature, defined as the temperature the steel can reach while still carrying its applied load, which then sets how much passive fire protection is actually needed [60:01].

### Technical Details
Limiting temperatures cited: ASTM E119 holds columns to 538 C (1000 F) and beams with a concrete slab to 593 C (1100 F) [40:44]. UK limits are 520 C for tubulars, 550 C for wide-flange columns, 620 C for slab-supported beams; mainland Europe caps at 500 C and offshore at 400 C [41:15]. The 538 C value sits between a yield intercept near 469 C and a buckling intercept near 550 C at the 60 percent stress level, and it assumes a member fully stressed to its design limit; a member governed by deflection or vibration at 20 to 30 percent utilization can justify 650 to 700 C and thinner protection [64:41], [66:16]. Protection options named are intumescent coatings, boarding, cementitious/SFRM, insulation blankets, bolt caps, concrete fill of tubulars, and concrete encasement of I-sections [29:27]. Cellular/web-opening beams ("smart beams") add failure modes: web-post bending, buckling, horizontal shear, bearing, and top-T bending around openings [61:02]. Tensile membrane action lets unprotected secondary beams in a bay be carried by the slab at high deflection [18:13], [74:00].

### Fabrication Notes
The webinar treats fire protection largely as a shop-or-field applied coating decision rather than a fit-up or weld-prep topic. Jowsey notes that reducing an intumescent system from a three-coat to a one-coat application improves constructability and gives greater control when applied off site in the shop versus in the field [73:28], [74:00]. The optimization graph trades steel weight against fire-protection cost: a heavier section has greater inherent fire resistance and needs less coating, so the lightest frame is not always the cheapest once coating material and application labor are added [56:58], [58:00]. He flags that connection bolts and plates lose strength and gain ductility as they heat, so fabricated connections checked only at ambient may be under-evaluated for fire [27:52].

### Erection Notes
Construction sequencing is named as a benefit driver: thinner or eliminated fire protection is quicker to apply and means less time on site [20:16], [43:51]. Thermal expansion during heating and contraction during cooling generate forces that can fail joints and redistribute load to adjacent members, which is an erected-frame behavior to be aware of [10:54], [23:49]. The fire hose test (a North American requirement) checks whether the assembly survives the sudden cooling and contraction forces from firefighting water after a fire [82:45]. No crane, rigging, or bracing sequencing is covered.

### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
The recurring error named is treating fire protection as an afterthought rather than designing it early, which is where problems arise [80:43]. A second is the unchecked assumption that a connection that works at ambient works in fire, with nobody verifying bolt strength loss, plate ductility, or heating and cooling forces [27:52]. A third is structural engineers disowning hot design as the architect's responsibility, leaving the limiting temperature on the tender drawings unverified by the engineer of record [38:41], [44:54]. There are no AISC 360, 341, or weld-inspection rejection criteria in this lecture; the "failures" here are design-process gaps, not shop or field NDE rejections.

### Bid / Cost Implications
The cost case is explicit and quantified. Dropping a coating from a 3-hour to a 2-hour rating cut intumescent thickness from 0.42 in to 0.26 in, a 39 percent reduction; 2-hour to 1.5-hour was 14 percent; 1.5-hour to 1-hour was nearly 60 percent, all product-specific [52:12], [52:42]. A combined structural-plus-fire-engineering case took a column coating from 3.4 mm to 1.15 mm, a 66 percent material saving, multiplied across every column and floor [73:28]. A fabricator-proposed lighter cellular beam saved 9 percent weight but raised cost about 8 percent once coating was included, showing weight savings can increase total cost [77:07]. High-rise office buildings with high required ratings and uniform floor plates give the biggest savings; low buildings needing only 15 to 60 minutes often do not [85:19].
