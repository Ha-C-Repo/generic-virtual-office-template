## Weld Details: The  Good, The Bad and The Ugly
URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mRJp75pN0Ho | Duration: 1:34:04 | Upload: NA | Tier: 1 | Watched: 2026-06-24
### Core Concept
Dwayne Miller (chair of the AWS D1.1 structural welding committee, AISC TR Higgins lecturer) teaches twelve principles of correct and proper welded connection design, then walks good, bad, and "ugly" case studies against them [01:52][03:35]. The thesis is that "correct or proper" equals strong enough to transfer all applied load but no stronger than necessary, because over-strength welds are the more common and costly error [04:23][04:40]. The recurring failure mode is the complete-joint-penetration (CJP) weld specified by default to avoid calculating a fillet size, when a minimum fillet would carry the load with a fraction of the deposited metal [05:17][72:23]. The twelve principles are: strong enough, clear and direct load path ("the force goes to the stiff part" / provide a path into the member that lies parallel) [08:15][08:34], welds in low-stress regions [18:44], no stress risers [21:50], not constrained [27:32], do not load the weld in bending [27:50], protect weld toes and roots ("watch your toes, remember your roots") [39:12], a clearly defined throat (avoid the "nothing weld") [42:11], recognize/respect material properties (through-thickness ductility, lamellar tearing) [42:33], easy economical and safe to fabricate and erect [53:02], recognize commercial realities (mill tolerances, weld distortion) [53:16], and aesthetically pleasing [57:14].

### Technical Details
Load-path geometry controls weld stress: a longitudinally aligned lug loads its weld uniformly, while rotating it 90 degrees produces a peak stress at midlength, fixed by stiffeners or by sizing only the welds in the parallel plane [09:06][10:08]. On round and square HSS the parallel quadrants are at the 3:00 and 9:00 positions, so a bottom lug strains the weld toe and ends unless a bracket reaches those positions or an internal diaphragm is added near the tube end [12:27][15:13][16:48]. Welding residual stress is driven by thermal expansion being roughly ten times yield elongation (a 10 inch, 50 ksi bar elongates 0.17 percent at yield versus 1.8 percent from room temperature to melting) [35:43][36:26]; when three orthogonal shrinkage strains meet at a point (Sigma 1 = Sigma 2 = Sigma 3) the steel cannot exhibit ductility and brittle fracture is likely, mitigated by weld sequencing and weld-access-hole snipes [37:18][38:36]. Through-thickness ductility is the weak axis: conservative through-thickness ultimate is about 80 percent and yield about 90 percent of longitudinal, with reduction-of-area sharply lower, so through-thickness tension loading risks lamellar tearing [51:29][51:44]. Transverse fillets carry more load because the failure throat rotates toward perpendicular, giving about a 38 percent increase before the codified 50 percent [91:16][91:36].
### Fabrication Notes
The "nothing weld" is the central QC concept: a weld that looks like the specified profile but is nothing like it, caused by lack of fusion through the section or by an excessive root gap that shrinks the effective throat [42:47][43:49]. Fit-up must be measured before welds are deposited; D1.1 allows 1/16 inch of root gap without correction, and beyond that the fillet leg is enlarged by the gap amount [89:02][89:18]. Make welds long and small rather than short and large for economy, because doubling length doubles strength while doubling leg quadruples deposited metal, never going below the minimum prequalified size [85:00][85:18]. Welding distortion is real: the Owens Steel box-column-around-a-wide-flange case used physical mockups to prove standard mill tolerance plus weld distortion would leave members out of square, and the fix was a true fabricated box [65:55][66:51]; a heavily reinforced doubler-and-stiffener gusset assembly distorted so badly it needed a porta-power jack to straighten, avoidable by selecting a heavier member [67:45][68:38]. Detail cover plates narrower than the beam flange to avoid flipping the assembly [53:16][53:49].
### Erection Notes
This is a design and fabrication webinar with limited dedicated erection content, but field welding versus field bolting on large trusses is addressed: fabricators prefer shop-welded splices while erectors prefer bolted field splices, and when loads are too large to pass through bolts, field welded connections must be used [62:43][63:18]. One cited project hid an "ugly" multi-bolted continuous bottom-chord connection inside a sleeve and field-finished it to speed construction and meet aesthetics, coordinated around one tower crane and one mobile crane alongside deck and curtain-wall trades [frame: Steel Conference article][63:34]. Several ugly examples were field-welded details (a 5 inch rod to a 3.5 inch plate) where shop conditions would have produced cleaner work [69:34][69:52].
### Common Errors / Inspection Failures
Named failures: the default CJP specified "just in case," "when loads are unknown," or "when no one wants to calculate the weld size" [05:34][07:28]; the discontinuous/butted backing bar creating a transverse stress riser, prohibited by D1.1 and AISC for cyclic loading [25:30][26:23]; the nothing weld from poor fit-up or non-fusion [43:35]; weld toe versus root crack initiation under cyclic load depending on fillet size [41:00]; lamellar tearing through the thickness, the documented El Paso Performing Arts Center (1972) compression-ring failure where a 1 inch plate received CJP welds loaded through-thickness into 2.5 to 3 inch box members, repaired by directly welding thick-to-thick [74:53][76:23][77:33]; the lesson that using a prequalified joint detail does not by itself make a joint suitable, leading D1.1 to add the note that the engineer shall determine suitability of all joint details [80:19][80:36]. Ugly workmanship: melt-through shipped and installed, unremoved weld tabs, and "dingleberries" left unground [83:37][70:07].
### Bid / Cost Implications
The CJP-versus-fillet economics are quantified on a real ugly example [frame: load slide / 71:11]: 24.5 kips of tension on a 5 inch rod is only 1.25 ksi on the rod and 0.23 ksi distributed over the CJP welds, while a minimum 5/16 inch fillet carries it at 1.84 ksi with a capacity of 278 kips, 11 times the demand. The CJP consumed 26 pounds of weld metal versus 0.23 pounds for the minimum fillet, a 96 percent reduction in deposited metal for an equally adequate, better-looking weld [72:23][72:38][72:54]. The standing estimating rule is long-and-small fillets at the minimum prequalified leg [85:00]. Distortion-driven rework (jacking out distortion, scrapping out-of-square members) and field versus shop weld choices carry direct cost and schedule weight [63:02][68:38].
